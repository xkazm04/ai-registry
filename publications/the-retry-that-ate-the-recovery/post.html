The retry that ate the recovery

Classification, jitter, budgets and the five ways a retry stops, read against what resilience libraries ship in October 2026

2026-10-14

At about 10:45 PDT on 2025-06-12, a policy change carrying unintended blank fields entered the regional Spanner tables that hold policies for Google Cloud's Service Control. It replicated globally within seconds, hit a null pointer, and put the Service Control binaries into a crash loop in every region.[1]

The report's own finding. In the larger regions, restarting Service Control tasks overloaded the underlying Spanner table, and Service Control "did not have the appropriate randomized exponential backoff implemented to avoid this". [1]

Disabling the faulty serving path took under 40 minutes. Full resolution took far longer: up to about 2 hours 40 minutes in one of the larger regions, where engineers throttled task creation by hand and routed traffic to multi-regional databases to take load off the infrastructure underneath.[1]

This post walks the failure lane, from classifying an error to naming the state a stopped retry stopped in, against what shipping systems default to in October 2026. No measurement here is first-hand: every number is published work, cited with its date, and with the read date where the page carries none, and arithmetic on those numbers is labelled as derived.

Retry traffic is the sustaining effect that outlives its trigger

That shape of failure has a name and a literature. The framework is Bronson, Aghayev, Charapko and Zhu's, from HotOS 2021.[34] An OSDI '22 study extends it, examines 22 metastable failures in depth across 11 organizations from public incident reports, and reports at least 4 of 15 major AWS outages in the preceding decade as metastable.[24]

Metastable failure, as the literature states it. A system in a vulnerable state, a trigger that causes temporary overload, then a sustaining effect that keeps the system degraded after the trigger is gone. The OSDI study extends that model with two kinds of trigger and two kinds of amplification mechanism, with retry traffic as an instance. [34], [24]

Retry traffic feeds the overload that produced it, which is why removing the trigger leaves the system degraded. Every caller that times out adds work to a dependency already short of capacity, and the work arrives in proportion to how badly that dependency is doing. The loop turns positive precisely when capacity is impaired, which is why adding retries lowers the everyday error rate while converting small outages into internal retry storms.[35]

AWS's summary of the October 2025 DynamoDB disruption shows the same shape. On 2025-10-19 a DNS Enactor, delayed while retrying its update across endpoints, applied a much older plan over a newer one; clean-up then deleted that older plan, removing every IP address for the regional endpoint. Downstream, DWFM "had entered a state of congestive collapse and was unable to make forward progress in recovering droplet leases", with work queued to reattempt the droplet lease, until engineers throttled incoming work at 04:14 PDT. The summary names retries and queued reattempts and nowhere states their spacing; the published remediation rate limits incoming work by queue size.[28]

Inference The two incidents are one model with two sustaining effects: task restarts against a Spanner table in June, queued lease reattempts against a lease manager in October. The sources describe each incident and the model separately; the mapping reasons from [1], [24] and [28].

Replay safety gates the retry, and the failure's class decides the rest

Before any classifier runs, one question decides whether a retry is permitted: can the request be applied twice without a second effect. RFC 9110 says an idempotent method may be repeated automatically when a communication failure arrives before the client can read the response, and that a client should not automatically retry a non-idempotent one unless it has some means to know that the request semantics are actually idempotent regardless of the method, or some means to detect that the original request was never applied; a proxy MUST NOT retry one at all.[17] Those two alternatives carry the working distinction. A connection refused before any byte left the client is a known non-execution, retryable whatever the method. A timeout after the request was sent is ambiguous: retryable where the semantics are idempotent, and safe for a non-idempotent method only behind a replay-safety key such as EC2's token-based idempotency on RunInstances.[2]

With replay safety settled, the class decides the rest. A loop that catches every exception and sleeps treats four failures as one: a rejected credential, which never heals by repetition; a rate limit, which heals when the limiter says so; a connection reset, which heals at once; and a crashed dependency, which heals on no schedule the caller can compute. Classification belongs where the structured response still exists, because an error reaching the loop as a bare string has lost the field the decision needed.

The 2026 AWS SDK retry behaviour, which requires opting in with AWS_NEW_RETRIES_2026 until it becomes the default in November 2026,[7] reads the error code first and the HTTP status second, inverting the habit of switching on status. An HTTP 400 carrying RequestTimeout is retried, an HTTP 400 carrying ValidationException is returned at once, and an HTTP 5XX carrying one of the 14 documented throttling codes is throttling rather than transient. The status fallback is narrow: any 500, 502, 503 or 504 without a recognized code is transient, and the throttling class has no status fallback at all.[6]

The four classes, the evidence that assigns each and the contract it carries. The transient retry costs more quota than the throttling retry on purpose: a 500 or a connection failure often indicates a service-wide problem, while throttling signals that the service needs more time. [6], [5], [31]

gRPC puts the same discipline in the service owner's hands. Its retry design recommends listing in retryableStatusCodes only codes indicating the server did not process the request, and errors such as INVALID_ARGUMENT do not decrement the throttling token count, so one malformed payload cannot spend a budget meant for a sick dependency.[5]

Python, the documented order as a decision

def classify(error):
    # the SDK matches on error code first, then falls back to HTTP status
    if error.code in THROTTLING_CODES:    # 14 codes, no status fallback
        return "throttling"               # base 1_000 ms, 5 tokens
    if error.code in TRANSIENT_CODES:     # RequestTimeout, InternalError, I/O
        return "transient"                # base 50 ms, 14 tokens
    if error.code in PERMANENT_CODES:
        return "permanent"                # ValidationException, HTTP 400
    if error.status in (500, 502, 503, 504):
        return "transient"                # the documented status fallback
    return "unknown"                      # this post's lane: counted apart

The boundary itself is not clean, and the vendor says so: eventual consistency blurs the classification boundary, because a client error can become a success once state propagates.[2] That is the argument for an explicit unknown lane. A taxonomy whose default branch files novel failures as permanent drops recoverable work; one that files them as transient hammers a dead endpoint.

Jitter does the work backoff alone does not, and the defaults still omit it

Backoff is usually explained as politeness. The 2015 simulation that popularized it describes herd control instead. With no backoff, completion time grew linearly with contention while total work grew as the square of the number of clients in that simulated contention model, under a network delay with a 10 ms mean and 4 ms variance; capped exponential backoff alone reduced client work only slightly. Adding jitter at 100 contending clients cut the number of calls by more than half against an un-jittered capped ladder, and improved completion time as well.[3]

Which variant matters less than whether one is present. Full and equal jitter made roughly the same number of calls and decorrelated jitter more, and no variant changed the squared growth of total work in that model.[3] The production formulas are small. A configured gRPC retry policy multiplies every computed delay by random(0.8, 1.2) outside the cap, so maxBackoff is a pre-jitter ceiling and a realized delay can land slightly above it; the multiplication is unconditional, and only a server pushback replaces the computed delay.[5] The 2026 AWS SDK computes random(0, 1) x min(20,000 ms, base x 2^retry), full jitter under a 20-second cap.[6]

Shipped retry defaults on the read date, 2026-10-14. Two widely used libraries default to a constant delay with randomization off, while both protocol-level implementations randomize every delay; gRPC retries only where a service config supplies a policy, though transparent retries of RPCs that never left the client need no policy at all. What the field ships is further from the documentation than the documentation suggests. A preprint dated 2026-08-26 surveyed 200 open-source Python microservice projects and detected explicit retry logic in 11.5 percent, with its authors estimating true prevalence near 41 percent after auditing their own false negatives. Among the projects where it was detected, 60.9 percent carried at least one configuration with no backoff, and exactly 1 of 113 manually verified production configurations randomized its delay.[27] Service Control is the same gap outside a library: the correction Google published for it is an audit for randomized exponential backoff.[1]

Assumption Jitter is the cheapest control in this post, because it adds no mode, no shared state and no new failure path, only a multiplication inside a delay that already exists. The sources measure what jitter does [3] and what libraries default to [12], [15]; ranking it first by cost is this post's bet.

Amplification is bounded by a ratio, and only at the layer above the failure

A per-call-site attempt cap bounds a call site; amplification is a property of the composition. In a five-deep stack of service calls, the Builders' Library puts the load a struggling database at the bottom sees at 243 times normal, which makes it unlikely ever to recover, and recommends retrying at a single point in the stack for low-cost operations.[2] The page writes three retries at each layer and its own walk-through counts three tries then nine, so the multiplier is three attempts per layer: 3 to the fifth.

Google's SRE book reaches the bound from the traffic side. A per-request limit of three attempts alone pushes a client's attempted traffic to just under 3 times its request rate in the worst case, where a datacenter accepts few requests and rejects most; adding a rule that retries continue only while they stay under 10 percent of that client's requests reduces the growth of its own traffic to about 1.1 times. The companion rule is positional: retry only at the layer immediately above the one rejecting. The same chapter puts a bound on the other side of the call as well, client-side adaptive throttling, where a client begins rejecting its own requests locally once they reach twice the accepts it has seen over the last two minutes.[4]

Derived A ratio composes better than a count but does not vanish: five layers each holding their own traffic to 1.1 times give 1.1 to the fifth, about 1.6 times, against 3 to the fifth, 243. The 1.1 figure bounds one client at one layer [4]; the exponentiation is this post's, and it is why the positional rule exists.

Shipped implementations state that bound in three accounting units, each scoped below the stack: retries in flight as a concurrency, retries as a share of a caller's own traffic, and a health-token balance that a failure spends and a success refills. Not one of the four below counts across a fleet.

Inference A retry quota is spent only by retries: a first-try success restores a token, a first attempt costs none, and the balance is typically scoped to one SDK client instance rather than shared across processes or hosts. [6] So a fleet of restarting processes, each making first attempts against a fresh balance, spends no retry budget at all, which is why June's loop was ended by engineers throttling task creation rather than by any retry policy. [1]

Inference A usable budget is three quantities: the attempts still allowed, the delay the next attempt would wait, and the time left on the caller's deadline. gRPC states the hard part, that the call deadline applies across all attempts and the RPC then fails "regardless of how many attempts were configured or attempted", whatever a server advised. [5] Naming the three together is this post's rule; A6 is silent on a deadline expiring mid-backoff.

The counter-evidence is strong enough to move the default. Brooker's simulation of one failing service with 100 Poisson clients found three retries the most robust option and the most expensive, costing four times the work at a 100 percent failure rate, while a bucket that starts full depletes too slowly and a 0.1-token deposit per success behaves like about 0.1 retries when unhealthy, a scheme he calls "not modal in the same way".[26] A 2026 preprint pushes further: in its hardest scenario, retrying measured worse than not retrying.

A breaker's most consequential default decides when it probes

A circuit breaker turns accumulated evidence into a stated position: this dependency is believed dead, and it will be probed deliberately rather than pestered continuously. Resilience4j's breaker holds three normal states, CLOSED, OPEN and HALF_OPEN, plus METRICS_ONLY, DISABLED and FORCED_OPEN, the last of which always rejects calls.[11] Hystrix, the library that made the pattern famous, is in maintenance mode at 1.5.18 and points new internal projects at resilience4j; separately, it describes Netflix's own shift toward adaptive implementations such as adaptive concurrency limits.[32]

The defaults decide more than the design does. Resilience4j ships a count-based sliding window of 100 calls, a failureRateThreshold of 50 percent, a minimumNumberOfCalls of 100 before that rate is evaluated at all, a waitDurationInOpenState of 60,000 ms, and 10 permitted calls in half-open whose failure and slow-call rates are then evaluated against the same thresholds.[11] Polly breaks on a FailureRatio of 0.1 over a MinimumThroughput of 100 executions inside a 30-second sampling window, breaks for 5 seconds, and ignores the ratio when that throughput is not reached.[14]

Two different windows. Polly cannot open below 100 executions inside a 30-second sampling window; resilience4j opens only after 100 recorded calls, however long they take to arrive. [11], [14]

The non-obvious default is the exit from OPEN. Resilience4j's automaticTransitionFromOpenToHalfOpenEnabled is false, so an open breaker moves to half-open only when a call is attempted, not on a timer.[11] Polly runs no background timer at all and documents the consequence: an application that calls Execute only when the circuit is closed never reaches HalfOpen, and so never probes.[14]

Derived Resilience4j's documented wait in OPEN is a fixed 60,000 ms with no randomization of it, and half-open permits 10 calls. [11] Instances whose breakers opened within the same second therefore become eligible on one clock, so the probe wave is 10 calls times the number of instances, with none of the spreading the jitter section asks of a retry ladder.

Inference On a dependency quieter than either window both defaults sit close to inert, and a caller that routes around an open circuit never supplies the probe that would close it. The same inertia is why a breaker fits June badly: a restarting Service Control task needs its first call to Spanner to succeed. [1], [11], [14]

Partial failure breaks the breaker's premise, and ejection helps only where the host is the failing unit

In sharded or cell-based systems failure is partial: a breaker that trips on one overloaded shard's failures also hurts customers of the healthy shards, and a breaker that does not trip does nothing useful. Underneath sits an epistemic limit. A client cannot reliably predict whether a specific call with specific parameters will succeed, and should not need to know the server's internals to decide.[25]

Statistics is also where a breaker misreads its own evidence. In the 100-client model it "starts tripping a little early: around half the expected rate", because each client samples failures independently; across runs splitting the same traffic over 10, 100 and 1,000 clients it trips too early, approaching the performance of no retries at all.[26]

Envoy's outlier detection is the narrower instrument. Its defaults eject a host after 5 consecutive 5xx responses, check every 10 s, eject for a base of 30 s, never eject more than 10 percent of hosts, and cap ejection at 300 s;[9] ejection time is that base multiplied by the host's consecutive ejections, falling one step per interval while the host stays healthy.[10] The precondition is routing: ejection helps only while the balancer has somewhere else to send the request and the fleet has capacity to absorb it. Envoy's panic threshold names where that stops, at 50 percent of healthy hosts by default, below which it stops using health status and balances across all hosts, to "avoid a situation in which host failures cascade throughout the cluster as load increases".[36]

Derived A host that has reached the 300-second cap needs about 300 / 30 x 10 s, roughly 100 seconds of health, to decay back to the 30-second base, since the multiplier drops one step per 10-second interval. The formula and the defaults are Envoy's [9], [10]; the arithmetic is this post's.

Where the failing unit is not a host, ejection removes nothing: a shard or key range that every host fronts fails through all of them, which is the case Brooker's objection is about,[25] and the case June presented, a Spanner table every Service Control task shared.[1] The Builders' Library argues against the modal instrument on principle, holding that breakers add modal behaviour that is hard to test and can significantly lengthen recovery, with a token bucket as the lower-risk alternative.[2] The position the evidence earns is narrower than either side. In the preprint's single five-tier topology a breaker layered over the retry policy recovered almost the whole loss and matched adaptive budgeting,[39] while the objection that defeats breakers concerns sharded systems that topology does not model, so what decides the instrument is whether the breaker's scope matches the population that is actually failing.

The dependency's stated delay bounds the local ladder, where there is one

When a dependency states when to come back, that time bounds what the caller computed, and the oldest form of the signal is the thinnest. RFC 9110 defines Retry-After as an HTTP-date or delay-seconds, sent "to indicate how long the user agent ought to wait", and section 10.2.3 gives the field a meaning in two cases only: on a 503 it states how long the service expects to be unavailable, and on a 3xx it states the minimum time the user agent is asked to wait before reissuing. 429 is not mentioned in that section.[17] The status code that most needs it was defined elsewhere: RFC 6585 says a 429 response MAY include Retry-After, so a rate-limited client has no guarantee of being told when to return.[18]

HTTP, the two forms RFC 9110 allows

Retry-After: 120
Retry-After: Wed, 14 Oct 2026 19:00:00 GMT

The draft meant to close that gap is careful about clocks. The IETF RateLimit header fields define a RateLimit field whose r parameter is the remaining quota and whose t parameter is the effective window in seconds, an integer rather than a timestamp, which avoids clock-sync problems, alongside a RateLimit-Policy field whose parameters clients MAY ignore. Where both appear, Retry-After MUST take precedence, and the specification mandates no retry or backoff algorithm.[19]

Two of the three fields a careful client would want are unfinished. The RateLimit fields are an active Internet-Draft at revision 11 of 2026-05-23, IESG state "I-D Exists", expiring 2026-11-24.[20] The Idempotency-Key header draft, whose abstract offers fault tolerance for non-idempotent methods such as POST and PATCH, reached revision 07 on 2025-10-15 and is now Expired.[21] The delay signal is standardized, the quota signal is a draft with a month left on it, and the replay-safety signal has expired. No such channel existed in June, where the signal in that path was a process exit, which carries no schedule.[1]

Durable retry is a schedule, and the restart ladder is its loudest instance

A retry that must survive the process is a scheduled item: a persisted next-attempt time and an attempt count, compared against a clock. Temporal makes that the default for Activities, with a 1 second initial interval, a coefficient of 2.0, a maximum interval of 100 times the initial and unlimited attempts, while Workflow Executions carry no retry policy at all and Workflow Tasks back off outside the policy up to 10 minutes.[31]

The kubelet's CrashLoopBackOff ladder is the same machinery with a louder argument around it. It grows exponentially from 10 s through 20 s and 40 s, is capped at five minutes, and resets only after a container has run 10 minutes, twice the maximum, without problems. The cost of the fast end is measured: a benchmark of 110 instantly crashing single-container pods on one node saw /pods API traffic peak near the kubelet's roughly 5 QPS client limit before settling around 2 QPS, with container runtime CPU about 10 times higher and kubelet and API server CPU each about twice. The counter-argument sits in the same document: exponential decay mainly protects against unrecoverable failures, and frequent restarts can starve the kubelet and in extreme cases crash nodes.[22]

YAML, the per-node maximum, field names per the shipped reference

# KubeletConfiguration, kubelet.config.k8s.io/v1beta1
crashLoopBackOff:
  maxContainerRestartPeriod: 60s      # minimum 1s, maximum 300s

  KubeletCrashLoopBackOffMax: true    # beta and on by default since 1.35

What shipped is two features rather than one proposal: the configurable per-node maximum is beta and on by default since 1.35, while the cluster-wide cut to a 1 s initial and 60 s maximum delay is alpha and off by default since 1.33.[40] The field is crashLoopBackOff.maxContainerRestartPeriod, documented with a minimum of 1 second and a maximum of 300 seconds,[37] and zero was ruled invalid on evidence: a 0 second maximum raised API server QPS up to 30 times in a stress test.[23]

Inference Service Control's crash loop is this machinery one level up: binaries restarting against a shared database rather than against a node's kubelet, where the restart rate was the load. The report records the restarts and the missing randomized backoff, not the ladder they ran on. [1], [22]

Stopping is a first-class outcome, and the HTTP conventions can barely name it

Retry machinery is judged by how it stops, and five endings are distinguishable in design. They describe one logical operation, the call the caller asked for, rather than a single attempt, and they cover the endings the retry loop itself produces; a caller that cancels ends the call from outside the loop. Succeeding after N attempts is one of them, and N still matters, because a dependency needing three attempts per call is degraded even when every call eventually lands.

Inference The five-state set is this post's framework, reasoned from the sources rather than measured: each state appears in some system's behaviour [5], [6], [8], [31], but no source enumerates them and no convention names them. The budget they are measured against is the pair named earlier: attempts still allowed, and time left on the caller's deadline.

Python, the five endings given names

# the five ways one logical operation stops, named so it can be recorded
TERMINAL = ("succeeded_after_n",   # N attempts, and N is itself a signal
            "exhausted",           # the attempt allowance ran out
            "over_budget",         # next delay or deadline, whichever ends it
            "reclassified",        # permanence arrived, or a server said stop
            "denied_by_breaker")   # policy refused this attempt

Against those five, OpenTelemetry's HTTP conventions offer one signal. The span conventions define http.request.resend_count, stable, as "the ordinal number of request resending attempt (for any reason, including redirects)", recommended if and only if the request was retried and counted regardless of cause.[29] The HTTP metrics conventions carry nothing: searching them for "resend", "retry" and "attempt" on 2026-10-14 returns no match, and the page does not say whether http.client.request.duration includes time spent on retries.[30] The gap belongs to those conventions rather than to telemetry at large: gRPC measures per attempt as well as per call, and adds four per-call retry instruments including grpc.client.call.retries, all experimental and off by default.[38]

Durable engines do better and still hide the middle of the ladder: Temporal keeps Activity attempts out of Event History until the Activity ends,[31] and Envoy stops tracking remaining retry capacity in stats once a budget replaces the count.[8] June's loop reached none of the five states, because the restarts ended when engineers throttled task creation by hand.[1]

Spacing without a bound is half the control

The report's own account of 2025-06-12 is the argument in one clause: restarting Service Control tasks overloaded the underlying Spanner table, and "Service Control did not have the appropriate randomized exponential backoff implemented to avoid this", with full resolution in one of the larger regions taking up to about 2 hours 40 minutes from the start.[1] The gap between a bounded and an unbounded policy is orders of magnitude, not percent: three attempts at each of five layers put 243 times the load on the database at the bottom, while a 10 percent per-client budget holds one client's own traffic to about 1.1 times at one layer.[2], [4] Exactly 1 of 113 verified production retry configurations randomized its delay,[27] and two widely used libraries still default to a constant delay with randomization off.[12], [15]

Both incidents ended where the work enters rather than where it is retried: task creation throttled by hand in June,[1] incoming work throttled in October and then rate limited by the size of the waiting queue in the published remediation.[28] Of the seven forward-looking items Google published, the one belonging to this subject is a commitment to audit its systems for randomized exponential backoff.[1] Client spacing and client bounds shorten a storm; a bound where the work enters is what ends it.

  1. List every retry site and write two things beside it: whether a repeat is safe to apply twice, and which of the four classes it treats. A site with no class treats all four alike.[6], [17]
  2. Turn jitter on where the library ships it off: IntervalFunction.ofRandomized() in resilience4j 2.4.0, UseJitter true in Polly 8.8.0. Neither adds a mode or shared state.[12], [15]
  3. Keep the per-site cap and add what it cannot give: a ratio on retries as a share of a caller's traffic, about 1.1 times at 10 percent, and retries only at the layer above the rejecting one.[4]
  4. Name which terminal state each loop's stopped attempt reaches, then check what records it. The HTTP conventions offer a resend counter and no ending, and the mean of the largest resend count plus one is the nearest amplification figure.[29], [30]

The uncomfortable part of the evidence is that retrying can be the worse choice: in the preprint's hardest scenario a naive policy measured 41.5 percent success against 55.4 percent with no retries, on one simulated five-tier chain whose authors list a single topology among their limits.[27], [39] That is not an argument for removing retries, but for what the Service Control report asked for in one clause: a system-level audit of how every lane in the failure path spaces, counts and stops its attempts.
