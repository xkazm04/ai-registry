# Performer claims need a person

This subject's opening grammar reads marks for the claims they make: an axis
with fine ticks claims precision, an arrow claims causation, a number on
screen claims that somebody checked it. One more belongs on that list, and
it is the only one that speaks:

> **A person on screen saying "I used this" claims a person.**

A synthetic performer delivering a first-person experience claim asserts an
*experiencer*. If no such person exists, the output has outrun its evidence
in the most complete way the medium allows — and this is the case the law
handles worst, because every other form of laundering starts from a real
fact and overstates its grade. Precision laundering sharpens a real band;
certainty laundering promotes a real single source; relation laundering
invents a claim between real figures. This one invents the *witness*. There
is no grade to propagate, because there is nothing behind the claim at all.

The boundary against three neighbours, because all three are close enough to
be mistaken for this one. Casting a synthetic narrator — which voice, what
delivery — belongs to
[spoken-delivery-direction](../../../narrative-craft/creator-voice-and-tone/techniques/spoken-delivery-direction.md);
that technique governs how a synthetic presenter *sounds*, and is silent on
what it may assert. Holding one performer's face stable across a batch
belongs to the conditioning ladder in
[generated-shot-sourcing](../../../production-ops/video-assembly/techniques/generated-shot-sourcing.md),
whose anchors exist to stop identity drift, not to license what the anchored
identity says. And disclosing that a production used generative tools at all
belongs to
[provenance-signal-asymmetry](../../content-research-grounding/techniques/provenance-signal-asymmetry.md),
whose disclosure is a claim about *your process*. This technique's
disclosure is a different object: a claim about whether the person making
the claim is a person.

## The craft's own gradient runs the wrong way

Identity-locking craft — reference sheets, anchored stills, a cast member
reused across a campaign — exists to make a synthetic performer *more*
consistent and more plausibly real from shot to shot. Every increment of
that craft strengthens the assertion the frame makes. So the discipline here
runs directly against the gradient of the surrounding work: the better the
identity lock, the more the performer reads as somebody, and the more the
frame needs to say who. This is the subject's standing hazard in its most
acute form — **the violation looks like better work**, and here it looks
like better work in the specific dimension the pipeline is optimising.

## The trigger is the line, not the face

Two independent properties decide the case, and neither is the problem
alone:

1. Is the performer **synthetic**?
2. Does the line assert **first-person experience** of the product?

A synthetic presenter reading a specification asserts nothing about
experience. A real person's testimonial has a real experiencer. It is the
*pairing* that manufactures a witness, and grading it is a brief-time act:

- **Synthetic performer, no experience claim** — narrates, demonstrates,
  presents. Ordinary generated material; the subject's usual routing
  applies, and nothing here constrains it.
- **Synthetic performer, first-person experience claim** — "I used", "it
  worked for me", "my mornings". Asserts an experiencer who does not
  exist. This is not a prompt-quality problem and no amount of realism
  improves it; realism makes it worse. Two honest exits: move the claim
  into the brand's own voice, where it becomes a claim the brand can
  substantiate, or source the testimonial from someone who actually used
  the product.
- **Synthetic performer voicing a sourced testimony** — a reenactment.
  Legitimate, and it is a *citation*: the attribution rides on the frame
  and the performer is not presented as the witness. Drop the attribution
  and it collapses into the case above.
- **The composite** — a "representative customer" assembled from real
  feedback. The most tempting and the least defensible, because the
  underlying material is genuine and the assembly still asserts one person
  who never existed.

## The obligation attaches at cast time

This is the load-bearing procedural claim, and it is a claim about *when*.
By the time a clip reaches review, the performer has a reference sheet, an
anchor still and a dozen shots derived from it; "is this person real?" is
answerable then only from a record somebody wrote when the character was
first rendered. Nothing in the pixels carries it.

So the cast record carries the two properties from the first render —
whether the performer is synthetic, and whether experience claims are
permitted for it — and they travel with the asset, exactly as conditioning
provenance travels with a generated clip. A performer with no record is
treated as synthetic, because that is the honest default: a real person's
release is a document that exists, and its absence is evidence.

## Procedure

1. **Record the performer's status at cast time**, before the first
   ratified render, not when the campaign ships.
2. **Grade every line at brief time** against the two-property test above.
3. **Settle a pairing before generation, never in review.** The only
   review-time fix is regeneration, which voids every approval the locked
   identity has passed — [edit, do not
   regenerate](../../../_laws.md#edit-do-not-regenerate) has no edit to
   offer here, because the defect is in what the frame says, not how it
   looks.
4. **Treat disclosure as a property of the frame.** A label in a profile
   bio does not travel with a clip that gets reshared, and the reshared
   clip is the one a viewer meets.
5. **Keep the record with the asset.** A cast member reused next quarter
   arrives with no memory of the judgment made this quarter.

## Failure modes

- **The invented witness** — a synthetic performer asserting personal
  experience; an experiencer conjured to carry a claim nobody made.
- **The composite customer** — a persona built from real feedback,
  asserting a single person who never existed.
- **The bio-only label** — disclosure filed where the clip does not travel.
- **The uncredited reenactment** — sourced testimony performed by a
  synthetic actor with the attribution dropped, so the performer reads as
  the witness.
- **The unrecorded cast** — a reused performer whose synthetic status was
  never written down, re-decided on impression each campaign.
- **The review-time discovery** — the pairing caught after the identity is
  locked and the shots derived, where the only remedy voids the approvals.

## When not to use this

Fiction, mascots and stylised characters no reasonable viewer reads as a
customer report are outside this technique; so is pure narration, however
synthetic the voice. The technique governs what a performer *asserts*, not
whether generated performers are legitimate material — per the subject's own
boundary, most of the screen is legitimately the model's, and this concerns
the minority of it that makes a claim somebody could be asked to back.
