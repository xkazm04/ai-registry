# Tension-finding taxonomy

The tension is the single place where **what people believe** and **what the
evidence shows** come apart. It is the load-bearing field of the whole
research asset: without it the facts can only be arranged in sequence, and a
sequence gives the viewer no reason to stay. Finding it is judgment, not
retrieval — more searches do not produce a tension, sharper comparison does.

## The taxonomy

Hunt for these five shapes, roughly in order of strength:

1. **The prediction that came true and didn't work.** The causal story ran
   exactly as forecast and produced the opposite outcome. Strongest, because
   it indicts the model everyone was using, not just a number.
2. **The number that contradicts the narrative.** A widely repeated story
   carrying a figure that does not fit it.
3. **The mechanism that runs backwards.** Something everyone treats as
   one-directional that has a reverse gear.
4. **The absent thing.** A change everyone treats as done that was never
   actually implemented.
5. **The category error.** The subject is being measured with the wrong
   instrument.

Write the finding as a structured triple — `expectation`, `reality`,
`why_it_is_a_tension` — plus a `strength` grade. The triple forces the
comparison to be explicit; a tension that cannot be stated as an expectation
versus a reality is a mood, not a finding.

## Decision rules

- **When no tension exists, stop and say so.** A topic with no tension is not
  a video, and reporting the null honestly is a *successful* run. The failure
  is writing a script anyway.
- **Size the tension against a baseline before scoring it.** An extremum is a
  single observation; a baseline is a distribution of prior instances with a
  sample size. Record the normal range and locate this instance inside it. A
  tension survives only if the deviation sits outside normal variation. If
  the baseline says the event is ordinary, exactly two honest outcomes
  remain: report the null, or pivot the tension to *the reaction* — "why
  everyone needed this to be anomalous" — with the baseline itself as the
  evidence. There is no third outcome; grading a middle-of-distribution move
  as a strong tension is the specific dishonesty this rule exists to catch.
- **`strength` measures checkability, not size.** High means the premise is
  checkable, widely held, and demonstrably wrong. A huge but unverifiable gap
  grades lower than a modest, fully checkable one.
- **A surviving counter-case downgrades strength by one step.** When the
  steel-man (see prior-declaration-and-steel-man) is examined and stands
  without winning, the tension drops one grade, and the record says which
  counter-case did it. A counter that survives while the score sits untouched
  means the two halves of the pipeline are not talking.

## Procedure

1. Establish the factual spine breadth-first across the subject's own causal
   domains — derive 5-7 domains from the subject itself rather than importing
   a generic list, and record them, because downstream review surfaces are
   organized by that list. Two rows are mandatory in every derived set: the
   **baseline** row and the **counter-case** row.
2. For each domain, compare the received story against the strongest evidence
   found, looking for the five shapes above.
3. Draft the triple. Test the `expectation` half: is it actually widely held,
   or a strawman built to be knocked down? A strawman here becomes a strawman
   on screen, and audiences notice.
4. Size against the baseline; assign `strength`; note what would change the
   grade.

## When not to use it

Do not use the taxonomy on genres that do not turn on a belief/evidence gap —
a tutorial, a celebration piece, a pure explainer of an uncontested
mechanism. Forcing a tension onto tensionless material manufactures
controversy the evidence cannot back, which is a worse defect than the
timeline it was avoiding. The taxonomy is for factual narrative where the
audience arrives with a prior worth overturning.
