# Scale conversion of numbers

A number without a comparison is a number the script wastes. Audiences do not
feel magnitudes; they feel ratios, rates, and comparisons to things they
already own. Scale conversion is done at *research* time — each significant
figure is stored alongside a pre-computed felt form — because conversion is
arithmetic against sourced quantities, and arithmetic belongs where the
sources are, not in the writing pass where a slip becomes narration.

## The three preferred forms

1. **Rates over totals.** "About fifteen hundred of profit per second" beats
   "forty-five billion a year" — same fact, but the second is a statistic and
   the first is an experience. Divide big annual figures down to human time
   units.
2. **Ratios over levels — for quantities that move.** "Roughly half its
   high" survives for months; the level it replaced is wrong within weeks.
   Converting a volatile level to a ratio is the standard trade of precision
   for shelf life, and the research asset's currency analysis records which
   phrasings make that trade.
3. **Comparisons the audience owns.** "Smaller than a small state's economy";
   "about two million, from a company holding billions". The comparator must
   be something the audience can size without help, or the conversion has
   converted nothing.

## The exactness exception

The ratio rule has one hard carve-out: **a filed or published figure is
quoted exactly, with its vintage** — "3.2 million, per the second-quarter
filing". A filed number's precision is the only handle a viewer has for
checking it; rounding it to a ratio destroys checkability in the name of
memorability. The decision rule:

- Quantity that *moves* (a price, a rate, a balance) → ratio or comparison,
  because tomorrow's level falsifies today's narration.
- Quantity that was *recorded* (a filing, a statute's reference figure, a
  disclosed sum) → exact quote plus the date and instrument it came from.

Confusing the two in either direction is a defect: an exact-quoted spot price
ages the video in days; a rounded filing severs the audit trail.

## Bookkeeping that makes conversion safe

Conversions are derived facts, and they inherit the asset's arithmetic
discipline:

- Every quantity carries its **unit**, its **period** (the window it
  covers), and — for percentages — its **denominator**. A percentage whose
  "of what" is unrecorded cannot be honestly converted or compared.
- Every conversion records **which fact it converts**, so downstream gates
  can check the felt form against the graded fact it points at, and the
  fact's confidence caps the rendering. A conversion of a low-confidence or
  vendor-graded figure is itself low-confidence; polishing it into a vivid
  comparison launders it.
- **Any two quantities compared are recomputed** — magnitude, window, basis.
  The canonical scar: a load-bearing claim that one figure was "slightly
  more than" another compared a 30-day window against a 60-day one, fed a
  wrong conclusion into three rendered scripts, and passed twelve self-checks
  because none of them did arithmetic. A comparison the checker cannot redo
  from recorded unit/period/denominator is flagged, not shipped.

## Procedure

1. List every figure the script will plausibly narrate; mark the significant
   ones. Not every number earns a conversion — only the ones doing
   argumentative work.
2. Classify each as *moving* or *recorded*; apply the ratio rule or the
   exactness exception accordingly.
3. Compute one felt form per significant figure using the three preferred
   shapes; record the arithmetic and the source fact identifier.
4. Cross-check every comparison pair for unit, window, and denominator
   agreement before storing it.

## When not to use it

Do not convert numbers whose exactness *is* the story — a vote margin, a
statutory threshold, a record being broken by a specific amount. Do not stack
conversions ("a rate of a ratio of a comparison"): one transformation per
figure, or the audience loses the thread back to the fact. And never invent
a comparator the research did not size — a vivid but unverified comparison
is a new unsourced claim wearing a rhetorical costume.
