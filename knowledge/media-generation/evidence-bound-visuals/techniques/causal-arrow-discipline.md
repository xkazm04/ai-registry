# Causal arrow discipline

An arrow between two things is not a layout convenience; it is the strongest
single claim in the visual vocabulary — *this made that happen*. This
technique reserves the arrow (and its relatives: the flow line, the
domino chain, the gears diagram, the before→after pair read left to right)
for relations the research actually grades as causal, and gives sequence and
correlation deliberately weaker grammar.

## Why arrows are the dangerous default

Diagramming tools and diagramming habits make the arrow the default
connective: it is how you show two things are related at all. But the
viewer does not read "related" — the viewer reads *caused*, and reads it
faster and more durably than any spoken hedge. A narration that carefully
says "the price moved *with* yields, not *because of* them" while the
screen shows an arrow from yields to price has contradicted itself, and the
picture wins; it is on screen for the whole beat and in every screenshot
afterward. This is relation laundering in its purest form: the prose's
epistemics were correct, and the mark undid them.

The asymmetry that makes discipline necessary: adding an arrow costs
nothing and looks like clarity; the claim it smuggles is the exact claim
factual research is most reluctant to grant, because causation is the
hardest relation to source. So the mark that is cheapest to draw carries
the claim that is most expensive to earn.

## The grammar, graded by evidence

- **Sourced causation** (the research holds a mechanism, graded and cited)
  → the full kit: directed arrows, flow, chain diagrams. This is what the
  kit is *for*; a mechanism the corpus vouches for deserves to be drawn as
  one, and diluting earned causation into timid marks wastes the strongest
  material a factual piece has.
- **Correlation** (they move together; no sourced mechanism) → symmetric
  grammar only: overlaid or adjacent series, no directed mark between
  them, and the correlation figure drawn under its own precision limit —
  a measured range is a band, never a sharpened single value.
- **Sequence** (this happened, then that) → placement in time: a shared
  timeline, ordered frames, a cut. Adjacency in time is exactly what the
  evidence supports; the viewer may infer causation, but the piece has not
  asserted it. The line between "letting a viewer infer" and "asserting"
  is precisely the arrowhead.
- **Feedback loops and cycles** → the loop mark is a *compound* causal
  claim: every leg of the cycle asserts causation individually. One
  unsourced leg poisons the loop; draw the sourced legs as a chain and the
  weak leg as the open question it is — an honest gap in a mechanism
  diagram is itself a strong frame.

## Procedure

1. **Inventory the connective marks at direction time.** For each planned
   arrow, flow, or loop, name the fact record that grades the relation as
   causal. This is [figure-must-cite-a-fact](figure-must-cite-a-fact.md)
   applied to marks instead of numbers — an arrow is a figure whose value
   is "because".
2. **Read the research's own hedges as binding.** When the notes say
   "phrase as moves-with, not because-of", that phrasing constraint *is*
   a drawing constraint. The visual layer does not get a looser epistemic
   contract than the narration; it gets a stricter one, because its claims
   persist on screen.
3. **Escalate, never resolve locally.** If the visual argument seems to
   need a causal link the research does not hold, that is a research gap or
   a script problem — send it back. The direction layer drawing the arrow
   anyway "because the beat needs it" is the piece deciding its conclusions
   at the compositing stage.
4. **Audit direction reversals.** An arrow pointing the wrong way along a
   sourced mechanism is rarer than an unearned arrow but worse: it is a
   sourced citation attached to the inverse claim. The inventory from step
   1 makes this checkable — the fact record states the direction.

## When not to use it

Purely rhetorical or navigational arrows — "look here", a callout leader
line from a label to the object it names — are pointing gestures, not
causal claims, and this discipline does not apply. Keep the two visually
distinct (leader lines thin and unheaded, causal arrows emphatic) so the
viewer's reading of the causal mark stays sharp. And within openly
metaphorical frames, an arrow inside the metaphor's own logic asserts
nothing about the world; the discipline re-attaches the moment a real
quantity or real entity enters the frame.
