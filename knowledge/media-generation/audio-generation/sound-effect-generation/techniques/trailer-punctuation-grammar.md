# Trailer punctuation grammar

Trailer sound runs on a small, stable vocabulary of functional sounds, and
the vocabulary is a *grammar*: each element is defined by the causal work
it does in the cut, not by its timbre. An effects pass planned as grammar
— this beat needs a promise, this cut needs a full stop — produces a
spotting-shaped list a generator can execute element by element. An
effects pass planned as decoration ("add impacts") produces the piece
where everything hits and nothing lands.

## The vocabulary

| Element | Causal work | Envelope shape |
|---|---|---|
| **Hit** | punctuates — a full stop on a cut, a reveal, a title | sharp attack, controlled tail |
| **Riser** | promises — an audible "therefore is coming", building into a moment | swelling attack, ends exactly at the target |
| **Whoosh** | carries — moves the eye across a transition | soft-fast attack, motion in the body |
| **Drone** | holds — sustains tension under a passage | no attack, steady evolving body |
| **Cutoff / tape stop** | subverts — ends what was promised, makes the next silence land | the ending *is* the sound |
| **Boom (sub-drop)** | weights — gives a landed moment mass after the hit | sub-heavy body, slow decay |

Two grammatical rules do most of the work:

- **A riser must resolve.** It is a promise, and its target — a hit, a
  cut to black, a title — must arrive at the second the riser ends. A
  riser into nothing is the audio form of "and then"; the audience feels
  the dropped clause even when they cannot name it.
- **Silence is an element.** The loudest moment in a modern trailer is
  the cutoff into nothing before the final hit. Silence works only if the
  grammar around it creates it on purpose — it is spotted, like music's
  chosen-silence, not left over.

## Placement is spotting, restated

Elements attach to **structural beats** — the turn, the reveal, the title
— not to shot edges, exactly as music entries do; the same causality law
governs both, and the effects list is written against the cut's structural
marks so a retime re-derives placements instead of orphaning them. Density
is the budget: each act of a piece can carry only a few punctuation marks
before they stop marking anything, and the grammar's economy — every
element answerable for its causal work — is what keeps the count honest.
The tell of a failed pass is uniform density: hits on every cut is the
sound of no hits at all.

## Generating the vocabulary

Each element generates well from its envelope description plus its
production world (the restated finish the whole pass shares), and the
functional elements are the strongest case for element-wise generation:
a riser briefed alone can be given its exact length — from its start
beat to its target, to the frame — where a riser inside a composite
cannot. Build the production's own small library per project: six risers
of different lengths, three weights of hit, two drones — generated once,
reused across the cut, consistent by construction.

## When not to use this

The grammar is short-form grammar — trailers, teasers, openers, anything
argued in under three minutes. Long-form sound design (a film's, a
game's) has scene-level logic this vocabulary does not carry, and
importing trailer punctuation into it reads as exactly what it is. And
dialogue-driven pieces may need almost none of it: the grammar
punctuates *cuts*; where the voice carries the structure, effects
punctuating over it are two foreground channels halving each other —
the music craft's rule, applying to effects unchanged.
