# Character vocal brief

Before anyone writes a description, the character is read for what a voice can
and cannot carry. The output is a one-page brief that a description, an audition
set and an effect chain are all written from. Skipping it is how a casting round
spends ten generations discovering that "loyal" has no sound.

## Read the character on three levels

1. **Identity**: who the character is when nothing is happening. Age, origin,
   intelligence, status relative to the listener, how they regard the person they
   speak to. This is what the timbre must carry on every line.
2. **Function**: what the character does in the work. A guide in someone's ear,
   a narrator, an antagonist, a companion who explains. Function sets the
   working register (the delivery most lines will be in) and the pace.
3. **Arc**: how the character changes. A companion who degrades, a mentor who
   turns. The arc sets the extremes the voice must reach without breaking, and it
   is the most common source of a wrong brief, because extremes belong to lines,
   not to the timbre.

A reference character (one the audience already knows) is read the same way.
The brief describes what that character sounds like in attributes: register,
texture, pace, attitude, accent. It never names the character or its performer,
and it never carries a quotation of their lines.

## Send every trait to one carrier

Each trait is written into exactly one of three columns.

| Carrier | Owns | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Timbre (the designed voice) | what is true on every line | age band, pitch register, texture, accent, baseline warmth, articulation |
| Delivery (line text and direction) | what changes line by line | sarcasm, tenderness, panic, a laugh, a whisper, the arc's extremes |
| Treatment (finishing layer) | what marks the voice as not a person | synthetic sheen, radio band, metallic ring, holographic shimmer |

Decision rules:

- **When a trait is only true sometimes, it is delivery.** A breakdown arc, a
  flash of anger, a sad scene. Written into the timbre, it leaks into every
  cheerful line.
- **When a trait is a personality word, translate it or drop it.** "Loyal",
  "brilliant" and "protective" have no direct acoustic correlate. Translate them
  into what a listener hears ("steady pitch that never rises under pressure",
  "precise consonants", "warm low-mid, close to the microphone") or leave them
  to the lines.
- **When a trait marks the voice as artificial, it is treatment.** It goes to the
  effect chain, and the description then stops mentioning it, because two
  authorities over one channel resolve into a compromise matching neither
  ([a typed input owns its channel](../../../_laws.md#typed-input-owns-its-channel)).
- **When the character's context implies a channel** (a headset, a radio, a
  public address), that is treatment too, and it is designed as a variant of the
  chain rather than a second voice.

## Name the axes the rounds will explore

The brief ends with the two to four axes on which reasonable people would
disagree about this character: lower or brighter register, younger or older,
warmer or cooler, rougher or cleaner texture, faster or more measured. These are
the axes the slate spreads along. A brief without named axes produces a slate
without spread.

## Write the brief down

The brief is a short record kept with the voice: the three-level reading, the
carrier table, the axes, and one paragraph on what the voice must never sound
like (too sweet, shrill, breathy, theatrical). It is the document a second round
is written from and the document a rebuild on a new engine generation starts
from. A voice whose brief is lost can be copied by ear but not redesigned.

## When not to use it

For a stock or library voice chosen from a catalogue, the brief shrinks to the
audition criteria; casting among existing voices is the spoken-delivery-direction
technique's job. For a cloned voice of a consenting person, the timbre is the
person, and only the delivery and treatment columns remain.
