# Rights and provenance record

Whether a generated track may be used commercially is not a property of
the file. It is a property of **the circumstances of its generation** —
which account, on which plan tier, under which terms in force that day,
referencing what material — and none of those circumstances travel with
the audio. They exist fully at generation time, decay from the moment the
take is accepted, and are routinely unreconstructable by delivery: plans
change, terms pages change, and the person who generated the cue has
forgotten which of four accounts they were signed into. The record is
written at acceptance because acceptance is the last moment the facts are
still cheap.

## What the record holds, per asset

- **Account and plan tier at generation time.** The load-bearing fact.
  Vendor commercial grants are typically plan-scoped and — the trap —
  **non-retroactive**: upgrading later does not license what a free tier
  generated earlier, so "we have a paid plan now" clears nothing that
  predates it.
- **Vendor, model, and version**, and the date. Terms attach to services
  as they were on the day; a rights posture verified in one quarter is a
  dated fact, not a durable one.
- **The brief** — plan and prompt, or a hash of them — both as the
  reproducibility anchor and as the evidence of human creative input:
  under current registration doctrine, output with no meaningful human
  contribution may be unregistrable, and the authored plan, lyric, and
  edit history *are* the human contribution, provable only if kept.
- **Reference material used, and its status.** A reference track's rights
  exposure propagates into the output (the anchoring craft owns the
  brief-side rule; the record keeps the fact). "None" is a value worth
  recording explicitly.
- **The intended destination it was cleared for.** A grant that covers a
  web video may not cover broadcast advertising; clearance is
  per-destination, and an asset re-purposed later gets re-cleared against
  the record, not against memory.

## The gate, and the law behind it

An asset without a record **fails acceptance for commercial use**,
regardless of how it sounds — not as bureaucracy, but as the bundle's
evidence law applied to a claim: shipping a track in a commercial
production asserts "we may use this", and that assertion may not outrun
what the record can evidence. A missing record does not mean the rights
are absent; it means the claim is, and productions do not ship claims
they cannot make.

## Decision rules

- When a take is accepted, write the record in the same motion, because
  every field is free now and some are gone in a month.
- When the production changes vendor, plan, or account, treat the change
  as a rights boundary: assets on each side carry different records, and
  nothing migrates implicitly.
- When an asset's destination widens, re-clear against the record before
  reuse, because the original clearance was for the destination it names.
- When the record cannot be completed — unknown account, unknown
  reference — say so in the record itself and route the asset away from
  commercial paths; an honest gap is usable information, a backfilled
  guess is a liability wearing documentation's clothes.

## When not to use this

Exploration candidates that will never ship need no record — the record
starts when acceptance does. And the record is evidence, not counsel: it
holds the facts a clearance decision needs, it does not make the decision,
and a production operating anywhere the doctrine is unsettled buys advice
from someone licensed to sell it.
