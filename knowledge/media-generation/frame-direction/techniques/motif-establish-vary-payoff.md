# Motif: establish, vary, pay off

Direct the cut, not just the frames. A motif — a shape, an axis, a repeated
mark, a recurring diagram — introduced early can return at the turning point
and mean something it could not have meant on first appearance: the price
line that climbed in frame one *continues past its high and falls* at the
turn, and the fall is legible precisely because the viewer already owns the
line. Thirty individually excellent frames with no threads between them
produce a picture gallery; motifs are what make it a film.

## Threads with explicit lifecycle operations

Manage each motif as a **named thread** whose every appearance carries one of
five operations:

- **establish** — the motif's first appearance; everything later refers to
  this.
- **carry** — the motif is present, unchanged, keeping the viewer's mental
  model warm.
- **change** — the *same* picture, modified. This is the operation behind
  state-change visuals: five delivered items are the same five objects,
  re-composed and struck through — not a new chart about completion.
- **reprise** — the established image returns late, under a new sentence.
  The visual form of a re-description: same picture, new meaning.
- **retire** — the motif's last appearance, declared, so a metaphor used once
  is abandoned rather than haunting later frames.

Two operations are constrained: **change and reprise require an earlier
establish on the same thread**, checkable by machine. And both mean the
viewer must *recognise* the earlier asset — a reprise rendered as a merely
similar image destroys the payoff, because recognition is the payoff. This is
the cut-level face of edit-don't-regenerate: modifying an established asset
preserves what the viewer has already invested in it; regenerating a
lookalike silently voids that investment.

Thread names should name *subjects*, not positions — a thread named for what
it depicts survives beats being reordered, merged, or dropped, where an
index-based scene list rots the first time the script is re-rendered.

## The three arc obligations

1. **Establish, then vary, then pay off.** A payoff that was never
   established is a reveal (fine, but different); an establish that never
   pays off is a dangling promise the viewer half-remembers. When a motif
   returns deliberately, say why in the rationale — payoffs authored by
   accident are payoffs the next edit deletes.
2. **The turns are the spine.** Reversal beats must be the most visually
   distinct frames in the cut — often the *change* operation on the most
   established thread, because breaking a pattern the viewer owns is the
   strongest visual event available. If a turn looks like the passage before
   it, the argument reversed and the screen did not.
3. **Vary from your neighbours; let quiet beats be quiet.** Locally, if a
   composition repeats the previous or next frame, change the camera, the
   count, or the axis — a cut is a rhythm and three centred symmetrical
   frames in a row is a stall. And a question or a thesis sentence earns a
   near-empty frame: density everywhere is density nowhere.

## Decision rules

- New picture or thread operation? If the beat's argument is "the thing you
  saw, further along / undone / re-seen", it is a change or reprise on an
  existing thread; a fresh image would make the change read as a new subject.
- A metaphor thread is establish-then-retire, usually within two beats. A
  metaphor that carries across the cut starts colliding with the mechanism
  imagery it stands beside; the two must not share visual language, or the
  stand-in reads as part of the machine.
- When a re-render reorders or drops beats, re-attach direction by thread
  name, never by position.
- Budget check at the cut level: count establishes. A cut establishing a new
  motif every third beat is not threading — it is the slide deck with a
  vocabulary.

## When not to use this

Very short cuts (a handful of beats) rarely have room for establish-vary-
payoff; forcing a reprise into a six-beat clip spends two beats on ceremony
the runtime cannot afford. And never thread for threading's sake: a callback
that carries no new meaning under its new sentence is nostalgia, not payoff —
the test is whether the reprised image *means something different* the second
time.
