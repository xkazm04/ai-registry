# Data-video virality

A narration-free data video (a bar race, a line race, a map changing
month by month, a share stream) has no voice to rescue it. Whatever the
viewer gets has to come from the data's motion and the on-screen
callouts. That makes the choice of idea, and of the dataset's shape, most
of the craft. A strong pick survives average execution. A weak pick with
perfect execution still gets almost no views, and the format gives no
second chance: once the chart starts moving, no line of narration can
make a viewer care about a cast they don't recognise or a chart that
only jumps once a year.

This subject is about **choosing and scoring** ideas before anything gets
built. Opening frames belong to `hook-shape-selection`, and runtime and
aspect adaptation belong to `platform-format-adaptation`. Both are cited
here, not restated.

## The governing fact: the path has to hold the viewer

A data video is watched second by second, so the question that decides
it is **why someone keeps watching this chart now**, not whether the topic
is famous. A famous topic with coarse data or a single record holder
loses viewers after the first jump. That makes three things gates rather
than scores:

- **Continuity** (continuity-gate): enough real data steps (monthly,
  weekly, daily, per match or per event, or a very long multi-entity
  yearly series) and enough entities moving against each other that the
  screen changes at every step.
- **Path** (path-over-conclusion): lead changes, overtakes, crashes and
  comebacks along the way, written down as a concrete keep-watching
  rationale with names and dates. A payoff that lives only in the last
  frame fails.
- **Accelerator events** (accelerator-events): 5 to 15 dated real-world
  events that pop up when the data reacts to them. They are the teaching
  layer of a format without a narrator.

Evidence still matters, but it ranks ideas that already pass; it no
longer gates them. A comparable with millions of views proves that a
topic has an audience. It doesn't prove that a given chart of that topic
holds anyone past ten seconds (precedent-evidence-gate). Counter-evidence
from recent flops still lowers a score (saturation-check), and the cast
still has to be recognisable without captions (recognizable-cast).

## What passes and what doesn't

- **Passes:** ranked races of many entities on fine-grained data (top 20
  songs each month, top 10 phone makers each quarter, company values each
  day); cumulative races driven by discrete events (goals per match,
  launches, medals per competition day); map evolutions with many
  countries changing at once (every border, every flag, every month of a
  war); share streams where shares cross.
- **Anti-patterns that fail however famous the topic:**
  - **Single-value ladders.** The tallest, the biggest, the oldest, one
    record holder at a time ("how old is the president", "the biggest
    plane").
  - **Record lists and winners lists.** One discrete result per period,
    with no continuity between steps (champions' points each season, the
    yearly award winner).
  - **Yearly data over a short span.** A few decades of yearly points
    jump instead of move.
  - **Single-season and documentary stories.** One person's comeback or
    one title fight needs a narrator.
  - **Conclusion-only reveals.** A map that fills in with winners, where
    only the final frame matters.
  - **Monotone fill-ins.** Things that only switch on and never overtake.

## Speculative cases

What-if and projection videos widen a fact channel and draw large
audiences, but only with a real historical baseline, a stated model, a
limited horizon, a permanent on-screen label and both worlds visible
(speculative-grounding). Keep them a minority of the output, roughly a
quarter to two-fifths, so the channel's facts stay credible.

## Composition order

1. Generate ideas in batches, each with a hook, a data source, a
   granularity and a span.
2. Run the format and continuity gate first (continuity-gate). Redirect
   failing ideas to a finer granularity or a multi-entity framing before
   rejecting them.
3. Write the keep-watching rationale and the expected lead changes and
   movements (path-over-conclusion).
4. List 5 to 15 dated accelerator events with their on-screen effects
   (accelerator-events).
5. For what-ifs and projections, state the model, the baseline and the
   horizon (speculative-grounding).
6. Run the duplicate screen (topic-duplicate-screen), then collect
   precedent and counter-evidence (precedent-evidence-gate,
   saturation-check).
7. Score the remaining judgment axes, rank, and **calibrate the rubric
   against owner verdicts**: every accept and reject the owner gave must
   be reproduced, plus negative controls that must fail.
8. Checkpoint the scored backlog after every batch.

## Failure modes

- **Precedent carrying a weak chart**: a famous topic passed on views
  alone, with no reason to watch past the first jump. The continuity and
  path gates come first for this reason.
- **Coarse data dressed as motion**: smoothing yearly points of a single
  line to fake continuity.
- **Unlabelled speculation**: a projection or counterfactual drawn like
  history.
- **Interesting-to-me scoring**: passing ideas on the scorer's curiosity.
  Counted views on comparables are the only cure.
- **The flooded slot**: a confident pick in a cluster that already has
  thousands of near-identical uploads.
- **The niche cast**: a correct chart of names nobody recognizes.
- **The near-duplicate**: a "new" idea that is an existing case with
  different words. It inflates the pass count and splits effort.
- **The one-off**: an idea that can't become a series, so every win has to
  be re-earned from scratch (series-repeatability).
