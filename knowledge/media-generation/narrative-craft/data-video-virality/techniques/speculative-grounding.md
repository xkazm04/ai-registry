# Speculative grounding

What-if videos ("if the Soviet Union had never broken up") and
projections ("the largest economies to 2075") draw large audiences and
give a fact-driven channel variety. They are also the easiest way to
publish something that looks like data and isn't. Speculative grounding
keeps them honest: real history first, a stated model, a visible label,
and limits on how far the model may reach.

## Procedure

1. **Start from real data.** Every speculative case runs on a real
   historical series up to a branch point (what-if) or up to now
   (projection). Require roughly 20 years of real baseline, or about 10
   years when the baseline is monthly or finer.
2. **State the model in one or two sentences** that a viewer could
   check: a published scenario (a UN population variant, an energy agency
   scenario, a climate model ensemble), a fitted trend with its window,
   a simulation with its inputs ("wins each race with probability 0.38,
   his 1988-1993 rate"), or a mechanical recombination of real data ("sum
   the successor states' medals").
3. **Limit the reach.** The speculative horizon should not run more than
   about twice the length of the real baseline, and projections stop at
   2100. A 40-year history can't justify a 110-year counterfactual.
4. **Show both worlds.** For what-ifs, keep the real series on screen
   beside the counterfactual (ghost bars, a second panel). For
   projections, draw the projected part hatched or dashed, with a median
   and a band when the model is simulated.
5. **Label it on screen** for the whole speculative stretch
   (SPECULATIVE - WHAT-IF or SPECULATIVE - PROJECTION), and put the model
   on the end card.
6. **Still pass the fact gates.** Continuity, path and accelerator events
   apply unchanged. Projected milestones count as events only when
   labelled as projected.
7. **Keep speculation a minority.** A channel whose credibility rests on
   facts should keep speculative videos to roughly a quarter to two-fifths
   of its output.

## Decision rules

- A counterfactual that needs an invented narrative (who would have won a
  battle) fails. A counterfactual that recombines or extends real series
  (redistributing a company's sales, holding a growth rate at its
  pre-event trend) can pass.
- Prefer published models over your own when one exists, and name it.
- A speculative case that reuses a fact case's data is a sequel. Record
  the link, and consider shipping it as a coda to the fact video.

## When not to use it

- **Pure fiction or alt-history storytelling** with no data model. That is
  a different genre, and it shouldn't wear a chart's credibility.
- **Live, contested forecasts** (elections, markets next month), where a
  confident-looking projection can mislead more than it informs.
