# Cue-first assembly

In this form the cue is the structure and the picture is fitted to it. Editors
select the music first and lay the cue out as the framework before any footage
goes down; the acts of the finished cut *are* the movements of the cue. This is
not a stylistic preference — it is the dependency order of the form, and it is
the fact most often inverted by anyone approaching trailers from a general
video-editing model where audio is a finishing layer.

The consequence is severe and practitioners state it plainly: a late note asking
to try a different cue is the worst note there is, because everything is cut to
the music. Changing the cue is not a swap; it is a re-cut. The order is also
industrial rather than merely editorial — promotional cues are routinely chosen
before the work's own score exists, so there is frequently nothing else to cut
to.

## What a usable cue has

A cue is chosen for its **edit affordances**, not for how it sounds in
isolation. The property that matters is whether it gives the editor places to cut
— hits, fills, breaths, and a beat of silence between sections. A beautiful,
continuous, evenly-textured piece of music is close to unusable here, because it
offers no boundaries to hang structure on.

The shape the form wants is a four-movement arc with a short tail: a mood
opening; an exposition section where a rhythmic device enters and establishes
pace; a response section that adds drive; a build; a peak of full energy; and a
brief closing phrase, drawn from the peak's material, that sits under the title
and end cards. **A "trailerised" piece of existing music is one re-arranged into
exactly this shape** — sections deliberately shortened so the peaks and valleys
can be aligned to cut points, quiet verses against a large chorus, a return to
quiet. That re-arrangement is what makes a song into a cue.

## The consequences for planning

Three things follow, and they are the reason this belongs in a structural
subject rather than in a sound one:

- **Act boundaries are cue boundaries.** Where the trailer's acts *can* fall is
  decided by where the cue's sections fall. Planning act durations in the
  abstract and then looking for music that fits produces a search with no results.
- **The reset has a position, not a preference.** The dynamic reset can only land
  on a natural downbeat ([dynamic-reset](./dynamic-reset.md)), so the cue chooses
  it.
- **Shortening preserves the cue's shape, not the picture's.** The standard move
  for a short cut is to keep the cue's opening and its climax and custom-edit the
  middle to connect them — the music's arc survives when the picture's does not,
  which is a strong signal about which of the two is actually load-bearing
  ([length-ladder](./length-ladder.md)).

## Procedure

1. **Choose the cue before writing the beat plan**, on edit affordances: does it
   have hits, section boundaries, a breath before the peak, and a shape that
   rises after falling?
2. **Lay the cue down alone and mark its boundaries** — section starts, the
   rhythmic device's entry, every candidate stop, the peak. These marks are the
   cut's act structure and everything downstream inherits them.
3. **Assign the parts to the marks.** Cold open, introduction, escalation rungs,
   reset, peak, tail. If a part has no mark to sit on, the cue is wrong for the
   plan — change one of them now rather than later.
4. **Cut picture to the marks**, letting audio lead across transitions. Audio
   from the next unit beginning before the previous one ends is the form's
   default transition and is why sub-second shots still feel continuous.
5. **Freeze the cue before showing the cut to anyone who can request changes.**
   Every note after this point is a picture note by construction; make that
   explicit rather than discovering it.

## Decision rules

- **When a cue has no breath before its peak, reject it** however good it
  sounds. There is nowhere to put the reset, and the cut will have no climax.
- **When the plan and the cue disagree about where an act ends, move the plan.**
  The cue is the harder constraint: a boundary the music does not mark is a
  boundary the viewer cannot perceive.
- **When a cue change is genuinely required, treat it as a re-cut and say so.**
  Presenting it as an adjustment guarantees a schedule that assumed an adjustment.
- **When the work's own score does not exist yet, do not wait for it.** Choose a
  cue with the right shape and let the picture follow; this is the normal case,
  not a compromise.
- **In a tool, model the cue as the timeline's parent, not as a track.** A system
  where beats carry timings and music is attached afterwards has encoded the
  inverted dependency, and every structural check it runs will be measured
  against positions the music does not mark.

## When not to use it

- **When the piece is deliberately unscored.** Cuts built on silence, or on
  diegetic sound alone, are a real and respected shape — most often in the
  specialty lane — and imposing a cue-first plan on one removes the choice that
  defines it.
- **When the work's score is the selling point.** If the audience is being sold
  the composer, the cue is fixed in advance for a different reason and the
  affordance-based selection above does not apply; the picture accommodates.
- **For a piece assembled from a fixed voice track.** Where a scripted narration
  or an interview is the spine, the words fix the timing and the music fits to
  them — the dependency runs the other way, and this technique inverted is the
  defect.
