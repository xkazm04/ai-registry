# The promise ledger

A trailer makes promises it does not keep on screen, and that is the format, not
a defect. What makes it a defect is a promise the **work** cannot keep either.
The ledger is the explicit accounting of the two: every promise the cut makes,
and the thing in the finished work that pays it. A promise with no payer is the
single failure that survives release — it converts a viewer and then loses them,
and it is invisible at the moment the cut is approved because at that moment
everybody in the room already knows the work.

The literature's name for both directions of this failure is **cheating**:
overpromising and underselling are the same sin, because both misdescribe the
artifact. Overpromising is the loud one. Underselling is the quiet one, and it is
what a cut does when it advertises a safe, legible version of a strange work in
order to test well — the audience arrives expecting the trailer's work, does not
get it, and reports being misled by a cut that showed nothing false.

Promises are made in three ways, and only the first is obvious.

## Where a promise comes from

1. **By claim** — a line of dialogue, a copy card, a stated stake. Easy to audit
   because it is text.
2. **By register** — the cue, the grade, the cutting rhythm. A cut scored and
   paced as spectacle promises spectacle regardless of what any line says; a cut
   that promises comedy in its register and delivers a bleak work has made a
   false promise without a single false statement.
3. **By assembly** — the one worth its own section below. Two true images placed
   adjacently assert a relationship that neither contains.

## Promises made by assembly

Dialogue in this form is **cobbled**: lines are sub-clipped from anywhere in the
work, indexed by speaker and text, and reassembled into a sequence the work never
utters — routinely combining two lines from entirely different parts of the
runtime under a third part's images. The technique is legitimate and it is how
the form achieves density. It is also a machine for manufacturing implications
out of true parts.

The check is the same one
[output-never-outruns-evidence](../../../_laws.md#output-never-outruns-evidence)
applies to a rendered figure, moved from the frame to the cut: **the assembled
claim may not be more certain, more causal, or more specific than what the work
supports.** A question cut against an unrelated answer asserts that the answer
belongs to it. A reaction cut against a reveal asserts the character saw it. A
line cut against a face asserts it was said to them. None of these are lies at
the shot level and all of them are claims at the sequence level, so audit the
sequence.

A useful discipline: for each cobbled line and each cross-cut pair, write the
sentence a first-time viewer would take away, then find the moment in the work
that pays it. If the sentence is "they are enemies" and the work says allies, the
assembly is a false promise even though every frame is real footage.

## Procedure

1. **Extract the promises.** Watch the cut once as a stranger and write down
   everything you now believe about the work: what happens, who is opposed to
   whom, what kind of experience this is, how it ends. Do not consult the work
   while doing this — the whole value of the pass is that it is done from
   ignorance, and anyone who knows the work cannot perform it.
2. **Assign a payer to each.** For every line on the list, name the specific
   moment in the finished work that satisfies it. A promise with no named payer
   is a finding, not a maybe.
3. **Grade each payer.** *Paid* — the work delivers it plainly. *Partly paid* —
   the work delivers something adjacent, which is where most disappointment
   actually comes from. *Unpaid* — nothing in the work satisfies it.
4. **Separate spend from debt.** For every promise, also record whether the cut
   *paid it itself*. A joke, a scare or a reveal delivered inside the promotion
   is spent: it will not land again when the work ships. Spending is sometimes
   correct and always a cost.
5. **Report the register.** Add one line naming the genre and tone the cut's
   music, grade and rhythm promise, and whether the work is that. Register
   mismatches never appear in a claim-level audit and are among the most common
   real complaints.

## Decision rules

- **When a promise has no payer, fix the cut, not the promise.** Softening the
  wording of an unpaid promise leaves the register and the assembly intact, and
  those made most of it.
- **When an assembly asserts a relationship the work denies, cut it — even
  though every frame is genuine.** "The shots are all real" is not a defence; the
  claim is made by the edit.
- **Spend the strongest moment only against a decision, never by default.** A
  cut that consumes the best joke or the central scare buys conversion with the
  work's own experience. The literature is clear this happens constantly and
  equally clear that nobody has resolved it — so make it a recorded trade rather
  than an accident.
- **Withhold the mechanism, not the promise.** A trailer that promises nothing
  concrete to avoid overpromising has produced the other failure. The floor is a
  promise specific enough to be worth arriving for.
- **Pair every explicit claim with footage at the moment it is made.** A
  title card or narration line asserting a capability is paid at shot level:
  the footage under it must show the claimed thing then, not somewhere else
  in the cut. An asserted-but-unshown claim is the ledger's smallest and most
  common unpaid row, and the one a reviewer can check without judgement.
- **In a tool, make the payer a required field.** If a system composes or checks
  promotional cuts, a promise-bearing beat that carries no reference to the
  moment paying it should be surfaced as incomplete rather than accepted. What
  the author must assert, the tool can then check.

## When not to use it

- **When the work does not exist yet.** A concept piece or a pre-production
  promotion has no payer to name for anything, which makes the ledger empty
  rather than clean. The honest instrument there is a statement of intent, and
  the ledger becomes runnable once there is a cut of the work.
- **For a piece that pays its own debts.** A self-sufficient short completes what
  it opens, so its promises and its payments are the same object and the ledger
  degenerates. Use the neighbouring subject's loop accounting instead.
- **As a gate.** Every grade in the ledger is a judgement, and "partly paid" is
  the honest verdict for most rows in a good campaign. A system that blocks on it
  will block on correct work; report the rows and let a human read them.
