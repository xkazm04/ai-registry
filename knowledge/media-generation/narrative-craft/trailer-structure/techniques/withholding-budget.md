# The withholding budget

Every other short form in this bundle treats a withheld answer as a defect — a
piece that raises a question and does not answer it reads as not having one. Here
withholding is the product, and the useful object is therefore not a rule but a
**budget**: a stated allowance of how much of the work may be spent to sell it,
decided once, in advance, by whoever owns the work rather than by whoever is
measured on the cut.

The budget exists because the two things being optimised are genuinely different
and genuinely in tension. A promotional cut is measured on its own performance —
completion, recall, intent. The work is measured on the experience it delivers.
The same footage optimises those differently, and **the mechanism that pushes
toward revelation is measurement, not laziness**: the most consistently reported
finding from audience testing is that viewers say the cut did not show them
enough. A doctrine that treats every revealing cut as incompetence will
mis-diagnose every campaign that made the trade knowingly, and there are many.

So the failure this technique prevents is not "revealing too much". It is
**revealing by drift** — a campaign where nobody ever decided, each successive
cut showed slightly more than the last, and by the third one the work's central
withheld asset had become a marketing asset with no one having chosen that.

## What the budget is denominated in

Not runtime, and not shot count. Denominate it in **the work's own assets**,
listed by name before any cutting starts:

- the **turn** — the reversal the work is built around;
- the **reveal** — what a withheld entity, place or fact actually is;
- the **resolution** — how it ends, including whether a threatened outcome
  happens;
- the **best moment** — the strongest single laugh, scare or image, wherever it
  falls;
- the **novum** — for a work whose appeal is one unfamiliar idea, the idea
  itself.

Each asset gets one of three allowances: **spend** (may appear), **imply** (may
be signalled that it exists, never shown), **hold** (may not be referenced at
all). That is the entire budget, and it fits on one page.

## The default allowances, and what they cost to break

The craft consensus, where one exists, is: hold the turn, hold the resolution,
imply the reveal, spend the best moment only against a decision. The reasoning
behind "imply the reveal" is the sharpest of the four — a withheld entity kept
partly seen, in shadow, present but not resolved, is doing the format's core job,
and once the viewer has seen it in the promotion the work has lost the first
encounter it was built around.

The characteristic breach is **campaign drift**: a later cut reveals what the
first one implied. It happens because each cut is approved against its own
performance and the budget lives nowhere. That is exactly what writing it down
prevents.

## Procedure

1. **List the assets before cutting.** Five lines, named specifically, written by
   someone who owns the work rather than the campaign.
2. **Assign each an allowance.** Spend / imply / hold. Default to *hold* for the
   turn and the resolution, *imply* for the reveal.
3. **Record the trade for every `spend`.** One sentence: what conversion this
   buys and what experience it costs. A spend with no recorded reason is a drift
   that has already happened.
4. **Bind the budget to the campaign, not to the cut.** Every subsequent cut —
   teaser, spots, platform versions — is audited against the same page. This is
   the step that does the actual work, because single cuts rarely breach; series
   do.
5. **Re-audit at each approval.** Ask only "does this cut spend anything the
   budget holds", and treat a yes as a decision requiring the same signature the
   budget got.

## Decision rules

- **When conversion pressure argues for a spend, permit it — and record it.**
  The trade is legitimate and the literature contains no resolution of it. What
  is not legitimate is spending without anyone having decided.
- **When a work's entire appeal is one idea, hold the idea and spend its
  consequences.** A cut that fully explains its novum has nothing left to sell;
  a cut that shows what the novum does, without saying what it is, is the form
  working correctly.
- **When the work has no turn, say so and stop budgeting.** Not every work is
  built on a withheld asset. Applying a withholding budget to a work whose appeal
  is entirely on the surface produces a coy cut that promises a mystery the work
  does not contain — which is a false promise
  ([promise-ledger](./promise-ledger.md)).
- **Never let the budget be set by whoever is measured on the cut.** This is a
  structural rule, not a suspicion: the incentives are honestly different, and
  the person optimising conversion should not also own the allowance.
- **Treat a genre's convention as a prior, not as a licence.** Some genres carry
  a strong expectation that the work's central withheld thing stays withheld;
  breaching it there costs more than the same breach elsewhere.

## When not to use it

- **When the work is fully disclosed by design.** A demonstration, a
  documentary of a known event, or a continuation whose audience already knows
  the premise has no assets to hold, and a budget over it is ceremony.
- **When the promotion is the artifact.** A standalone piece that happens to
  advertise something is judged as itself; it opens and pays its own debts, and
  the neighbouring subject's rules apply instead.
- **As a substitute for the ledger.** The budget governs what may be *shown*;
  it says nothing about whether what is shown is *honest*. A cut can respect
  every allowance and still assemble a false implication out of permitted
  material.
