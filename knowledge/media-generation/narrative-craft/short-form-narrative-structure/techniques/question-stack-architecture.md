# Question-stack architecture

The acts of a factual video are questions, and the question stack is the
skeleton: the ordered list of questions the piece will answer, stated to the
viewer out loud immediately after the hook, and then answered strictly in
order. It does three jobs simultaneously — it opens the gaps the viewer now
wants closed, it announces that the video *has a shape*, and it lets the
viewer locate themselves at any moment ("we're inside question two"). No
other single artifact buys all three.

For anyone building or operating a script pipeline this is the
highest-transfer technique in the subject: a generator asked for "a script
about X" produces a timeline; a generator asked to *first produce the
questions the video will answer, then chain beats under each* produces
something with a spine. The stack must therefore exist as its own
reviewable, editable artifact, approved **before any prose exists** —
approving prose is approving a thousand decisions at once; approving a
three-line question stack is approving the three that matter.

## Procedure

1. **Derive the stack from the tension, not the topic.** Each question must
   be specific enough to feel answerable and uncomfortable enough that the
   viewer notices they cannot answer it. "How does the economy work?" is a
   topic; "how is a country under total sanctions posting boom-level
   growth?" is a stack entry.
2. **Size the stack to the runtime: roughly one question per 60–90 seconds
   of essay body.** Under three minutes, exactly **one** question — measured
   short-form practice is unanimous on this, and it is placed inside the
   first 12 seconds. Around fifteen to twenty minutes, three. (The 4–8
   minute middle is under-evidenced; interpolate and verify against
   retention, don't assume.)
3. **Order the questions causally.** The answer to question one should
   create question two — the stack is itself a beat chain and takes the
   same but/therefore test as any other. Three unrelated questions are
   three videos stapled together.
4. **State the stack on screen, verbatim, right after the hook** (long
   form), or let the single question *be* the hook (short form).
5. **Announce each transition.** "Whether any of that happens depends
   entirely on our third question" costs two seconds and re-anchors every
   viewer who drifted.

## Decision rules

- **One question means one.** A short that asks a second question has two
  theses, and each halves the other's payment. If a second question keeps
  forcing itself in, the runtime is wrong, not the rule.
- **Answering the question does not close the gap — if the question was
  built right.** The strongest short-form move is to answer the stated
  question almost immediately (even in one word) and let the *why* carry
  the runtime: the gap was never *what*, it was *why*. If answering the
  question early would genuinely end the video, the question was about a
  fact, not a mechanism — rebuild it.
- **Answer in the promised order, always.** The stack is a contract; a
  video that answers question three during question one has silently voided
  the viewer's map, and the disorientation reads as boredom in the
  retention data.
- **If a drafted beat serves no question in the stack, cut the beat or fix
  the stack** — never keep both unchanged. This is the mechanical form of
  "facts with no owner question".

## When not to use it

- **Sub-40-second pieces.** At that length the single question and the hook
  are the same utterance and there is no stack to architect — attempting
  one adds housekeeping the format cannot afford.
- **Pure narrative (story-first) pieces**, where the spine is a
  protagonist's causal chain rather than an inquiry. The stack is an
  explanatory skeleton; a story already has one.
- **As a rhetorical tic.** Stating questions you then answer within five
  seconds, repeatedly, is not architecture — it is padding wearing
  architecture's clothes. Stack entries are acts, not transitions.
