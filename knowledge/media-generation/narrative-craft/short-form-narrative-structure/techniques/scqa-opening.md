# The SCQA opening

The most reliable opening structure for explanatory work, from the Minto
pyramid tradition: **Situation → Complication → Question → Answer**. Common
ground the audience already accepts, stated fast; the thing that breaks or
fails to add up; the gap that break creates, made explicit; and the thesis —
delivered *up front*, then earned for the rest of the runtime.

The shape works because it manufactures curiosity mechanically rather than
hoping for it. A fact, however striking, closes a gap; only the collision of
an accepted situation with a complication opens one. And the shape's most
counterintuitive component — answering immediately — is its strongest:
explanatory content is not a mystery novel, and stating the conclusion in
the first minute spoils nothing because the tension was never *what*, it was
*why and how*. Long-form practice at the highest level gives the full thesis
away at roughly minute one of an eighteen-minute piece and spends the
remaining 94% of runtime on why it is stranger than it sounds.

## Procedure

1. **Situation** — one or two seconds of something the audience already
   believes. An aphorism, a shared observation. This is handshake, not
   information; its only job is to establish the ground the complication
   will crack.
2. **Complication** — the collision. The most isolated country on earth is
   posting boom-level growth; the intuitive answer to the title question is
   simply wrong. Spend the evidence budget here: two or three concrete,
   verifiable observations that make the complication undeniable rather
   than asserted.
3. **Question** — say it out loud. Do not trust the viewer to formulate it;
   the explicit question is what converts unease into a debt the video now
   owes.
4. **Answer** — the thesis, immediately, in one or two sentences. Then the
   body of the piece exists to make that answer earned instead of merely
   stated.

At very short runtimes the four steps compress into a breath — a nine-second
opening can carry situation, complication, and thesis in a single sentence
with one "but" doing the structural work — but compression removes no step.
What gets cut is words, never components.

## Decision rules

- **The 15-second test:** after the first fifteen seconds, can the viewer
  state the question the video will answer? If not, the opening failed
  regardless of how striking its facts were. This is the acceptance test
  for any hook, human- or machine-written.
- **Nothing before the gap is open.** No greeting, no branding, no "in this
  video". Identity is affordable *after* the question exists — three
  seconds of introduction at second twelve is fine; the same three seconds
  at second zero are the most expensive in the piece. The rule is not
  "never introduce yourself"; it is "nothing before the gap".
- **Withhold the answer only when the answer genuinely is the gap** — a
  reveal-based format — and then say so structurally. In explanatory work,
  default to answering; a withheld answer reads as not having one.
- **If the complication needs more than ~30 seconds of evidence in a piece
  under three minutes, the complication is too big for the runtime.** Pick
  the narrower collision.

## When not to use it

- **When there is no accepted situation to crack** — an audience with zero
  prior beliefs about the topic gives the complication nothing to complicate.
  Establish one image first (a paired concrete observation works in under
  ten seconds), then run SCQA against it.
- **Story-first openings** that begin inside an anecdote's action. These
  earn attention through motion rather than gap; retro-fitting SCQA onto
  them flattens the scene into thesis-speak. The two openings are
  alternatives — choose by whether the piece's engine is inquiry or
  narrative.
- **As a visible template.** SCQA is load-bearing structure, not
  labeling. An opening that audibly announces "here's the situation…
  here's the complication…" has turned the skeleton inside out.
