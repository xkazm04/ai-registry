# The reframe close

A recap tells viewers what they just heard — which they know, having just
heard it. A **reframe** hands them a new sentence for something they now
understand: a re-description of the whole piece that was not available
before watching it, ideally one the viewer could repeat to somebody else
tomorrow. That repeatable sentence is the actual product. It is what gets
the piece shared, what makes the explanation stick after the tab closes,
and what the entire runtime was quietly building the right to say.

The distinction is sharp in practice. "So that's how the boom works: trade
with two partners, counted in unusual ways" is a recap — backward-facing,
information-free. "The lights came on because a war needed shells; the real
question is whether they dim just as fast when it ends" is a reframe — it
compresses the argument into a portable claim *and* points past the edge of
the piece. Every strong practitioner refuses the recap ending; it is the
most reliably absent pattern in high-retention factual work.

## Procedure

1. **Write the closing line at composition time, alongside the hook — as
   one paired artifact.** The hook opens a gap; the close names what
   filling it taught. Drafting them together is the structural defense
   against the recap: a close written last, after the prose, defaults to
   summarizing the prose. A close written first has nothing yet to
   summarize and must therefore say something.
2. **Build the sentence to be repeatable.** Short, concrete, self-contained
   — it must survive being quoted without the video attached. If it needs a
   clause of context to make sense, it is not yet the sentence.
3. **Optionally point outward.** The strongest closes often end on the one
   question the piece has *earned* but not answered — a genuinely open
   question, framed by the argument just made. This is different from an
   unpaid loop: the piece's own debts are settled; the outward question is
   new, and explicitly offered as open.
4. **Then stop.** The reframe is the last beat. Anything after it —
   summary, throat-clearing, a second conclusion — spends the sentence you
   just handed the viewer.

## Decision rules

- **The repeat test:** could a viewer say this sentence to a colleague
  tomorrow and sound insightful? If it only makes sense as a summary of
  the video, it is a recap wearing a reframe's clothes.
- **The novelty test:** would this sentence have been meaningful at 0:00?
  If yes, it is a thesis restatement, not a reframe — the reframe must
  *require* the runtime to be understandable, which is what proves the
  runtime taught something.
- **One reframe.** Two closing formulations compete, and the viewer leaves
  with neither. If two candidate sentences exist, the better one closes
  the piece and the other usually belongs at an act boundary.
- **In payoff-by-demonstration formats, the completed demonstration IS the
  close** — plus at most a title callback. Adding a spoken reframe on top
  of a demo that already lands is a recap in disguise.
- **If no reframe can be written, the piece has a thesis problem, not an
  ending problem.** A video whose argument cannot be compressed into one
  new sentence either has two theses or none; send it back to the question
  stack, not to the prose.

## When not to use it

- **Serialized content whose explicit job is to bridge to a next episode**
  — there the close is deliberately an open loop, and a tidy reframe would
  discharge tension the series needs to carry.
- **Pieces under ~30 seconds**, where hook, payoff, and close collapse into
  one or two sentences; a separate reframe beat is runtime the format does
  not have. The hook's phrasing should simply be built to *become* the
  repeatable sentence once paid.
