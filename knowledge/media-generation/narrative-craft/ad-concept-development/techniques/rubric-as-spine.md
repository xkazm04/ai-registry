# Rubric as spine

## The concern

A spot made for a contest, a pitch or a client review is judged by a few people
against criteria. Often the criteria are written down, and sometimes they are
weighted. A team that reads the rubric after the concept is chosen ends up
**decorating**: the mandated element is bolted on, and the heaviest criterion is
satisfied by a late addition. Rubrics that weight cohesion punish exactly that. The
mandated element "feels separate" because it was.

The correction is an order of operations. **Read the brief as a scoring function
before ideation, and build the concept around the heaviest criterion.** If the
heaviest criterion is the catchiness of a mandatory jingle, the jingle is the
concept. The picture is designed to make the hook land, repeat and pay off. It is
not a score laid under a film.

This is a different thing from brand-effectiveness doctrine. Effectiveness research
measures long-run effects on consumers at scale. A contest is a one-shot panel
judgement, and a panel run by a toolmaker often rewards use of its own tools
explicitly. When the rubric states such an agenda, the rubric wins.

## Procedure

1. **Transcribe the rubric.** Write out every criterion with its weight, its exact
   wording and its scale. Read the formal terms and the launch announcement. The
   announcement often states intent the terms leave out ("central to the idea, not
   tacked on").
2. **Infer missing weights, and mark them inferred.** An unweighted brief still has
   priorities. Derive them from emphasis and order, and record them as inferred so
   nobody treats them as given.
3. **Name the spine criterion.** The heaviest criterion, or the heaviest pair if one
   criterion multiplies another (a "cohesion" criterion multiplies whatever the
   mandated element is).
4. **List the mandatories.** Required elements are hard constraints: name shown,
   product shown, logo, a mark of the toolkit, a length window, an aspect ratio. A
   concept that cannot carry them without strain is rejected.
5. **Generate concepts with the spine as the premise.** Each candidate states how
   the spine criterion *is* the idea. For a sonic spine, the hook might be what the
   characters do, the device the plot turns on, or the thing that changes the world.
   It is never what plays underneath.
6. **Score candidates on the rubric before production.** Use the rubric's own scale,
   with one line of reasoning per criterion. A candidate that scores high on the
   spine and low on concept goes back to divergence. A candidate that only scores on
   concept is the wrong brief's winner.

## When the spine is sound

Many briefs weight memorability, and a brief from an audio maker will weight sound.
What is known about sonic hooks is mixed in grade:

- **Melodic features.** A large peer-reviewed study of tunes that get stuck found
  them faster, with a common rise-and-fall contour, plus an unusual interval or
  repeated note.
- **Sonic cues in high performers.** Industry analysis of thousands of video ads,
  reported second-hand, found sonic brand cues several times likelier to appear in
  the high performers, though they made up a small share of assets.
- **Counter-evidence.** A peer-reviewed 2013 analysis of more than a hundred slogans
  found that jingle and rhyme had no measurable effect on slogan recall. Length and
  campaign duration mattered.

So treat the hook's catchiness as a craft target with measurable features, and do
not promise recall from the mere presence of a jingle. For a spine, the useful rules
are these:
- The hook carries the name when the brief requires the name.
- It is heard at least three times, varied each time.
- The picture changes on its phrases.

## Decision rules

- When a criterion is "use of our tools", it is a constraint on production, not the
  spine. Satisfy it in craft, and do not build the concept around tool display.
- When the spine and the concept pull apart (the catchiest hook makes a weak idea),
  fix the concept. The spine is fixed by the brief and the concept is not.
- When the rubric allows a wordless spot, check whether that choice gives up the
  spine. A wordless picture with a sung hook can be legal and strong. A wordless
  picture over an instrumental bed gives up a sonic spine's main lever, the name in
  the ear.

## When not to use

- Briefs with no judge and no criteria, such as an organic social post. Use
  effectiveness heuristics instead.
- Treat it with care when the rubric is obviously gameable: optimising one criterion
  to the point of a joke that judges will read as cynical. The panel is human.

## Failure modes

- **Rubric read last.** The concept is chosen, then the mandated element is
  composed to fit. Cohesion scores show it.
- **Weight inversion.** The team polishes the criterion it enjoys (picture craft)
  while the heaviest one (the hook) gets a single attempt.
- **Unverified compliance.** Length, aspect, watermark and visible name are assumed,
  not checked against the delivered file. A screening round rejects on compliance
  before quality is scored. An unchecked mandatory is an unmeasured pass.
