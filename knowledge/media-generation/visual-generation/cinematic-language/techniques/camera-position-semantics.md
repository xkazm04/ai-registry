# Camera position semantics

## The concern

Where the camera stands is the narrator's attitude toward the subject.
Shot size is emotional distance; height and angle are judgment; framing
choices — where the empty space sits — are commentary. Eye-level,
mid-distance, centered-with-headroom is the null sentence, and it is what
an undirected model produces every time. Each departure from neutral is a
statement, and each statement costs attention, so departures are chosen
per beat, not sprinkled.

## Procedure

1. **Size = distance.** The ladder runs from extreme wide (subject
   dwarfed by environment: scale, isolation) through wide (context, body
   language), medium (conversational neutrality), close-up (the face IS
   the subject: empathy or scrutiny), to extreme close-up (a detail
   screaming "this matters"). The tighter, the more subjective. Prompt in
   the ladder's own names: "extreme wide shot, a tiny lone figure in a
   vast landscape"; "close-up on her face, eyes glistening."
2. **Height and angle = attitude.** Eye-level meets the character as a
   peer. Low angle looking up grants power, menace, awe. High angle
   looking down diminishes: vulnerability, surveillance. Straight-down
   overhead turns people into pieces on a board — fate, aftermath,
   pattern. A canted (tilted-horizon) frame says the world is off its
   axis: reserve it for genuinely destabilized beats and commit to a
   decisive tilt — a slight cant reads as sloppy horizon, and habitual
   canting is the genre-cliché everyone cites.
3. **Directional space = psychology.** Lead room (empty space ahead of a
   gaze or motion) reads as open future; violating it — the subject
   crowded against the frame edge they face, dead space behind — reads
   as entrapment or something creeping up. Excess headroom shrinks a
   character. "She looks screen-left with generous empty space ahead" vs
   "framed tight against the edge she faces, uncomfortable space behind
   her."
4. **Symmetry = design.** Dead-center, one-point-perspective symmetry
   reads as order, artifice, or dread — a designed world. Off-third
   framing reads as natural. "Perfectly symmetrical composition, subject
   centered at the vanishing point" is a strong flavor; use it when the
   world should feel curated or oppressive.
5. **Relationship frames.** Over-the-shoulder holds two people in
   relationship; a "dirty" single keeps a blurred sliver of the partner
   (connected but focused); a clean single isolates entirely. Scenes
   migrate OTS → dirty → clean as characters emotionally separate.
6. **Depth staging.** Layer foreground/midground/background so one frame
   narrates without cutting: "a hand sharp in the foreground, the couple
   arguing in the midground, a figure watching from the far background."
   Negative space is a layer too: emptiness that should contain
   something is a threat.
7. **Subjectivity dial.** Objective coverage → over-the-shoulder →
   first-person POV ("looking down at trembling hands") → voyeur framing
   ("through a doorway, partially obscured, as if secretly watching") →
   reflections for doubled or divided selves. Choose how complicit the
   viewer is.

## Decision rules

- Assign each beat ONE departure from neutral (size or angle or framing
  violation), matched to what the beat means; a frame making three
  statements makes none.
- Escalate size with tension: scenes classically progress wide → medium
  → close as stakes rise; a jump from extreme wide to extreme close is a
  shock cut — spend it deliberately.
- If a character's state is the subject, move down the subjectivity dial;
  if the situation is the subject, stay objective and stage in depth.
- Write the stance in film vocabulary the model knows: "low-angle medium
  close-up", "overhead god's-eye view", "over-the-shoulder favoring her".

## Failure modes

- **The null sentence everywhere** — undirected eye-level mid-shots; the
  footage reads as anyone's phone video regardless of image quality.
- **Attitude contradiction** — a triumphant beat shot from above, a
  vulnerable beat from below; the grammar says the opposite of the story.
- **The chronic cant** — tilted frames as free energy on neutral scenes.
- **Statement stacking** — extreme angle + extreme size + symmetry +
  broken lead room in one frame; the meanings cancel into noise.
