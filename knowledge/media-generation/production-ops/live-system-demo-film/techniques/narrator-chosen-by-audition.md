# The narrator is chosen by audition, not by default

A demo film is heard for three minutes by someone with no other reason to stay.
The first synthetic voice that sounds acceptable is rarely the one that holds
them, and the default delivery settings of a synthetic voice are tuned to be
safe, which is the same as flat. The case that taught this accepted a calm
storyteller voice at conservative settings, produced a full narration, and heard
it as monotone only once the whole film was cut. Re-narrating a finished film is
the expensive way to find that out.

## Audition on the real lines, five voices, one passage

- **Use the script's own sentences.** A passage of five or six lines taken from
  across the film: the opening, a plain instruction, a line that stops and asks,
  a technical claim and the sign-off. A voice that is fine on the opening and
  stumbles on a product name is a finding you want before synthesis, not after.
- **Offer five, not two.** Two voices compare the voices. Five let the operator
  hear a range of register (warm, plain, smooth, relaxed, clear) and choose the
  register, which is the real decision.
- **Loosen the delivery for the audition.** Lower the stability setting and raise
  the style setting from their defaults for every candidate, including the
  incumbent, so that the comparison is of voices at a livelier setting and not of
  one voice at a lively setting against four at a flat one. Settings that read as
  restrained in the documentation read as monotone over three minutes.
- **Keep the passage short.** About thirty seconds per voice costs a few hundred
  characters each. The whole audition costs less than one re-narrated beat of a
  finished film.
- **Hand over the files, not a description.** The operator listens and names the
  winner; nobody can approve a voice from its label.

## Write the script to be spoken

The audition also exposes the script. Text written to be read sounds like an
announcement when spoken, and a flat voice is partly a flat script.

- **Open with what the thing is and for whom.** A one-sentence introduction in
  plain speech, and close with a sign-off that thanks the viewer. A film that
  starts on its first feature and stops on its last has no introduction and no
  ending.
- **Never announce structure.** "Part one", "Part two" and "Chapter three" are
  how a document talks. Say the transition a person would say: "Let's start by
  setting her up." "Now, give her a chore of your own." "But does she hold up
  when someone attacks her?" The section plate may still appear on screen; the
  line spoken over it is a sentence, not a heading.
- **Short sentences, spoken numbers.** Figures are written the way they are said,
  and a long claim is split where a speaker would breathe.
- **One pass of re-narration after the voice is chosen.** Choose the voice first,
  rewrite the lines second, and synthesise once, because every clip made under a
  discarded voice or a discarded line is spent for nothing.

## Failure modes

- **A voice approved from a single line.** It sounds right on the sentence that
  was sampled and wrong on the one with a name in it.
- **Auditioning at different settings.** The winner is the one that was given the
  livelier settings.
- **Choosing the voice after the lines are final.** The lines were written for a
  different rhythm and every clip has to be made again.

## Decision rules

- When a film has no approved narrator, audition five voices on one passage of
  its real lines at the same loosened settings, because the operator is choosing a
  register and can only hear it.
- When a first narration sounds flat, change the voice and the script before the
  settings alone, because a flat script stays flat at any setting.
- When the voice is chosen, rewrite the lines for speech and synthesise once,
  because the clip, the hold and the take all follow the line.
