# One overlay per shot

Each shot of a showcase has one overlay as its subject. That overlay is present, placed and
readable for the shot; the tool's other overlays are absent from the program while it
runs. Which overlay a shot is about is declared beside the beat, and the film's record says
what the program actually showed.

## Why

A stream picture with every overlay on it shows none of them. The viewer cannot tell which
one the beat's action caused, the overlays compete for one safe area, and a product that
places by default stacks them into an unreadable pile in the middle of the canvas. The cut
from the button press in the tool to the stream picture is a "therefore"; it lands only if
the picture has one thing in it that the press changed.

## Procedure

1. **Declare the shot per beat**: the overlay, its place and its scale, beside the beat's
   line. A beat with no declared shot keeps the previous picture on purpose, not by
   omission.
2. **Apply the shot when the overlay exists, not when the beat starts.** Many overlays come
   into being during the beat that is about them: a plate that exists only after a draw is
   sealed, a board that exists only after a duel is called. A shot applied at the start of
   that beat finds nothing to show, and the overlay then arrives with the product's default
   placement, on top of whatever the shot left. Re-apply the shot after any action that can
   add a source, or wait for the source and then apply it.
3. **Treat a shot that found its overlay missing as a beat failure**, recorded against the
   beat, not as a line in a log. A beat whose stream picture lacks its subject has no
   evidence, and the run must say so.
4. **Lay a stream background under the overlays.** Overlays aired over an empty black canvas
   read as broken boxes; the film's picture is a stream, so put representative stream art
   behind them, declared as sample art in the boundary.
5. **Leave provenance where the overlay put it.** A shot of a rehearsal result shows the
   badge the overlay draws for itself. The director adds no separate label source beside
   it: a label the director places is lost from any scene or crop the director did not
   arrange, and it competes with the overlay's own badge for the viewer's eye.
6. **Do not change the overlay's own settings.** The director chooses visibility, place and
   scale on the scene; it never edits the overlay's page, data or design, because then the
   film shows an overlay the product cannot produce.
7. **File every workaround.** If the shot needed the director to hide or move something the
   product would have piled, write the defect against the product before the film ships.
   Composition solved in the film is a product bug with good production values.
8. **Declare it.** The boundary states that overlays were shown one at a time and at the
   director's placement.

## Decision rules

- When a beat is about the relation between two overlays, show both and say so; the rule is
  one subject per shot, not one overlay per frame.
- When the setup beat adds every overlay on camera, it shows the product's composition as
  it is; if that is a pile, either fix the product first or do not film the setup on the
  stream picture.
- When the director enlarges an overlay to read at film size, enlarge around the overlay's
  own anchor and keep it inside the safe area, because enlarging around the canvas centre
  makes every overlay look like a centred card.
- When the director's placement differs from the product's, the film is showing the
  director's layout and must say so, because a viewer will assume the product does it.

## When not to use it

A shot whose subject is the composed scene itself, made to show that the product's layout
works, must not be directed this way: there the product's own placement is the evidence,
and directing it away is exactly the cover the overlay composition craft warns against.
