# The derived-short contract

A studio that produces mid-length factual work will derive most of its
short-form output from it. The naive method — cut the best thirty seconds out
of the long video — reliably fails, because a clip inherits context it no
longer carries: the hook is a sentence that only lands mid-argument, the payoff
leans on a setup that happened minutes before the cut. The technique replaces
surgery with **re-authorship under a four-rule contract**.

## The contract

1. **The clip is complete on its own.** It answers one real question fully. A
   viewer who never clicks through to the parent has still received something
   whole — a delivered payoff, not a teaser. Test: state the question the clip
   answers; if the honest answer is "watch the parent", the clip fails.
2. **The withheld thing is a different question.** The clip may open a new
   loop at the end to pull viewers to the parent — but it must be a *new*
   question, never the missing half of the one just closed. Withholding the
   payoff of the clip's own question converts a gift into a toll booth, and
   viewers price it accordingly.
3. **The pointer is explicit and last.** A named thing that happened, then the
   link. No coy "watch the full video to find out" — coyness reads as the toll
   booth it is trying to disguise.
4. **The clip assumes nothing from the parent's setup.** No dependence on
   knowing who the creator is, that a longer video exists, or on any term the
   parent defined. Every noun in the clip is either common knowledge or
   established inside the clip.

A clip that meets the contract typically runs: self-contained hook at second
zero → the reveal, delivered completely → the absurdity or consequence that
makes it matter → a concrete detail → context → the withheld new loop and the
pointer. Note the shape: the payoff arrives *early* (a derived short pays its
debt in the first quarter, then deepens), and each beat follows from the last
— the contract produces a causal chain in miniature, not a trailer.

## The anti-pattern: the amputated clip

The amputated clip is the mid-video segment cut and posted as-is. It deserves
its name because the wound is invisible to the surgeon: **it reads as complete
to the person who made it and as noise to everyone else.** The author carries
the parent's context in their head and cannot un-know it; the cold viewer has
only what is on screen. This asymmetry is why the contract must be checked as
explicit review items rather than left to the author's judgment — the author
is structurally the least qualified person to detect the failure. A practical
proxy: hand the clip to someone who has not seen the parent and ask them what
question it answered.

The frame has an amputation mode too: center-cropping a wide composition into
a tall one severs whatever argued from the edges. A derived vertical clip is
reframed — subject re-centered, punched in, data elements re-laid — not
cropped through.

## Decision rules

- **When choosing what to derive**, look for passages in the parent that are
  *already* nearly self-contained — a complete question answered in one run.
  A parent recorded with derivation in mind (self-contained passages, clean
  entry sentences) yields clips by selection; one that wasn't yields clips by
  rewriting, which costs a fresh script.
- **When the best moment isn't self-contained**, re-author: write a new hook
  and a new close around the moment, at short-form rules (hook shapes, one
  anchor). Budget it as new writing, because it is.
- **When reviewing**, run the four checks in order as pass/fail items —
  complete alone; withheld thing different; pointer explicit and last; no
  parent dependence. One failure is a rewrite, not a trim.
- **When the parent's argument cannot be closed in the short's runtime**,
  don't derive from it — pick a different question from the same material.
  The contract forbids the only workaround (withholding the payoff).

## When not to use

The contract governs clips *derived from a parent*. A native short written
from scratch needs the short-form rules but not rules 2–3 — with nothing to
point to, a manufactured "watch more" loop is pure friction. And when the
purpose of the clip is genuinely a trailer for a launch, the completeness rule
relaxes by declared intent — but call it a trailer, and expect it to perform
as advertising performs, not as content.
