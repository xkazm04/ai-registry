# Form follows what the track gives

A music video's form is chosen from a ladder, ordered by how much the pipeline
must hold in sync and therefore by what each usable second costs:

1. **Visualizer** — one image or a slow field of images with motion driven by the
   audio. Nothing on screen claims to make the sound.
2. **Lyric video** — the words, set and timed to the vocal. The sync claim is
   typographic: a word appears when it is sung.
3. **Performance** — the artist, or a performer standing in, singing or playing
   the track. Every visible mouth and every visible strike of an instrument is a
   sync claim the audience can check.
4. **Narrative** — a story told in picture alongside the song, with characters who
   do not need to be singing.
5. **Concept** — an idea or image system developed across the song without a plot:
   a visual motif, a transformation, a set of rules the picture plays out.
6. **Hybrid** — the most common commercial form: verses carry the story or the
   concept, choruses return to performance, and the bridge breaks the pattern.

The performance / narrative / concept split is the long-standing working typology
of the form, and scholarship on music video adds the point that matters for
planning: its editing answers the song — its sections, its rhythm, its changes of
texture — rather than the continuity of a represented space. So the form is not a
mood. It is a decision about what the picture will be claiming, section by section,
and every claim has a sync cost.

## The rule: two facts decide the form, not taste

**What the track gives.** A vocal-forward track with a recognisable singer asks for
performance at least in its hooks; an instrumental gives no mouth to sync and frees
the form entirely. Lyrics that tell a story can carry a narrative; lyrics that
repeat a hook cannot, and a story imposed on them reads as a short film with a song
playing over it. Length matters: a narrative needs the room of a full song, and a
ninety-second track rarely holds more than one idea.

**What the pipeline can keep in sync.** A performance is only available if the
pipeline can hold lip sync for a sung phrase at the shot lengths the cut needs, for
the performer the production cast, across the whole song. If it cannot — no
reliable lip sync, or sync that holds for a two-second close-up but not a
twelve-second take — the form drops to a rung that makes no claim the pipeline
cannot back: performance shown only where the mouth is unreadable (wide, back-lit,
turned away), the hybrid weighted towards narrative, or a concept form with no
singing face at all. The brief does not override this. A performance that the
pipeline cannot hold is a performance the editor will spend the whole cut hiding.

Climb the ladder only for a named reason the track supplies, the way a shot climbs
the conditioning ladder only for a named reason. Each rung up multiplies the number
of shots that can fail acceptance, and the cost is counted in usable seconds, not
in rendered ones.

## Allocation, not just selection

For the hybrid, the decision is per section, and it is written into the map: which
sections perform, which tell, which break. The default practitioners converge on is
that the hook belongs to the performer — the chorus is where the audience is being
taught the song and the face that sings it — and the verses carry whatever the video
is about. A bridge is where the pattern can be broken once. Write the allocation as
a share of screen time per section, because "mostly story in the verses" is a
negotiation in the edit and "verse 2: performance at most four bars" is a plan.

## Rungs one and two are real choices, not consolation prizes

The visualizer is the honest form for an instrumental, an album track, or a release
with no budget for a performer, and its whole craft is that the motion is a function
of the audio. Built as a composition rendered per frame, the motion must be derivable
from the frame's own time — the envelope sampled at that instant — rather than from
state accumulated while playing, or a render split across workers will disagree with
its preview; that property is the seek-stable-composition-authoring technique of
video-assembly, and the visualizer inherits it in full. A brightness pulse on the
beat is also a flash, and it is counted against the photosensitivity ceiling that
cut-rate-follows-the-section states. The lyric video's one hard
rule is that word timing comes from the vocal activity map, not from a line-level
guess: a word that appears before it is sung is the typographic version of a mouth
that moves early.

## Boundary with narrative-engine-selection

narrative-engine-selection chooses the structural engine of a factual video that
already has a story to tell. This technique decides an earlier question for a song:
whether the video has a story at all, and how much of each section the story gets
against the performance. When the answer includes a narrative, its engine is chosen
over there — with the extra constraint, set here, that the story's beats must land
on the sections allocated to it.

## Decision rules

- When the pipeline cannot hold lip sync over the shot lengths the cut needs, do
  not choose performance, whatever the brief wants; drop to a rung whose claims the
  pipeline can back, and say so in the treatment.
- When the track is instrumental, treat performance as instrument performance:
  every visible strike is a sync claim, and if the pipeline cannot place strikes on
  beats, keep hands out of the readable frame.
- When the lyrics repeat a hook rather than tell a story, do not force a narrative;
  choose concept or performance.
- When choosing a hybrid, write the allocation per section into the map before any
  shot is generated.
- When two rungs both fit, choose the lower one, and climb only for a reason the
  track gives, because every rung multiplies the shots that can fail.

## When not to use it

An artist's established visual identity, or a label's brief that fixes the form for
reasons outside the track — a series of videos, a campaign — overrides the ladder;
the technique then only checks that the fixed form is one the pipeline can sync, and
raises it early if not. And a video that is a document of a real performance is not
choosing a form at all; its sync is captured, not generated.

## Sources

The performance / narrative / concept typology is training-data convergence on
music-video studies (it is the standard working typology in teaching and criticism)
and was not fetched. That music-video editing responds to musical features — rhythm,
timbre, melody and form — rather than to spatial continuity is Vernallis's argument
(Experiencing Music Video, 2004; "The Kindest Cut", Screen, 2001), known here from a
search summary and training data, not from a fetched text. The allocation default
(hook to the performer) is practitioner convergence, not measured.
