# Cut rate follows the section

Cut frequency in a music video is set **per section, from the track map**, and the
cuts land on bars and downbeats rather than on every onset. Fast cutting is a tool
the form uses more than any other film form, and it stays legible under one
condition: the subject holds one screen position across the cuts, so the viewer's
eye does not have to search for it after each one.

The rule: **before a section is cut faster than about one cut per bar, check that
the subject's screen position is stable across the section's shots; where it is
not, slow the cut or re-frame.**

## Rate is read from the map

The map gives each section an energy figure and a tempo at the chosen metrical
level. Rate follows both: a quiet verse holds shots across several bars, a chorus
cuts on every bar or every downbeat, a breakdown can cut on beats for a few bars
and earn it because the section is short. Write the rate per section as a number —
cuts per bar — into the plan, because "faster in the chorus" is decided once per
cut by whoever is editing, and differently each time.

Cut on **bars and downbeats, not on onsets.** An onset detector fires on every hi-hat
and ghost note; a cut on each of them is a cut rate the music is not actually
asking for, and it erases the difference between sections, because every section
has onsets. The grid's hierarchy — phrase, bar, beat — is the hierarchy of cut
weight: a change of setup or location wants a phrase boundary, a change of angle
wants a bar, and a beat-rate cut is a special effect for a few bars, not a texture
for a chorus. This is also why the metrical level in the map has to be decided
rather than detected: at a doubled tempo every "bar" is half a bar and the same
plan cuts twice as fast.

## Why the eye decides how fast you can cut

Every cut is a new image the eye has to orient in. Research on attention across
cuts finds that viewers' gaze defaults towards the screen centre after a change,
and that a cut feels smooth when the centre of interest after it sits where the
viewer was already looking; rapid orienting to a new shot is helped by clear,
stable screen locations for the people in it. Editors have named this for a long
time: eye trace, where the viewer is looking, sits on the classic editor's list of
what a cut must respect, alongside emotion, story and rhythm.

For a music video this turns into a practical limit. If the performer's face is in
the upper left in one shot and the lower right in the next, the viewer spends part
of each shot finding it. At slow rates that search is invisible. At a cut per bar
or faster it consumes most of each shot, and a fast-cut chorus reads as a blur of
unrelated images instead of one performance seen from many sides. The fix is not a
slower cut by default; it is a stable anchor. Keep the face, or whatever the
section is about, in one region of the frame across the run of cuts — usually near
centre, slightly high — and change the angle, the lens feel, the light and the
background around it. The angles then read as variety and the subject reads as
continuous.

## A beat-rate cut can be a flash

A hard cut between a dark shot and a bright one is, to the eye, a flash, and so is
an effect that pulses brightness on the beat. Web accessibility guidance caps
general flashes at three per second for photosensitive viewers, and broadcast
guidance sets a similar ceiling. At 120 beats per minute a cut on every beat is two
changes a second, and on every eighth note four. So a beat-rate passage between
strongly contrasting shots, or a beat-driven brightness effect, is counted against
that ceiling before it is planned, and where it would pass it the contrast is
reduced or the rate halved. The count is computed once over the whole planned
section, not judged while watching.

## The check

For any section planned faster than about one cut per bar:

1. Mark the subject's position in each shot of the section — a box or a point is
   enough.
2. Compare consecutive shots. Where the subject jumps across the frame between
   two shots that sit closer together than a bar, either re-frame one of them, slow
   the cut there, or put a deliberate eye-leading move in between (a whip, a
   motion that carries the eye to the new position).
3. Watch the section at speed, once, looking only at whether you have to search.

The one-cut-per-bar threshold is a working figure, not a measured one. At typical
pop tempos a bar is around two seconds, and below that the share of each shot spent
finding the subject grows with how far the eye has to travel. Treat it as the point at
which the check starts being worth doing, not as a limit the form may not pass.

## Boundary with cinematic-language

cinematic-language owns what each angle and movement means: that a low angle
confers power, that an orbit isolates, that a handheld shot reads as presence. This
technique owns how fast angles may follow each other on this track and where in the
frame the subject must stay while they do. A section can be correct in every angle
and still fail here; it can pass here with angles chosen for no reason, and fail
there.

## Decision rules

- When a section's cut rate is set, set it from the section's energy and tempo in
  the map, in cuts per bar, and write it down.
- When placing cuts, place them on downbeats and bar lines; reserve beat-rate
  cutting for short passages.
- When a section is planned faster than about one cut per bar, run the screen
  position check before the section is generated, because re-framing a brief is
  cheaper than re-generating a shot.
- When the subject cannot hold position — a moving dance, a crowd — slow the cut or
  lead the eye between positions with motion.
- When a passage cuts on the beat between strongly contrasting shots, or pulses
  brightness on the beat, count its flashes per second over the whole section
  before generating it, and keep the count at or under three.
- When two sections must feel different, let the rate do it before reaching for
  effects.

## When not to use it

A deliberately disorienting passage — a section about chaos, confusion, a dream —
may break screen position on purpose, and the check then confirms the breakage is
chosen rather than accidental. A single-take video has no cut rate. And a lyric
video's rate is set by the words, not by the bars.
