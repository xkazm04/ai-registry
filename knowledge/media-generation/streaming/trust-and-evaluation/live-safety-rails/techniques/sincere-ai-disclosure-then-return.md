# Sincere AI disclosure, then return

When a viewer sincerely asks whether the host is a person, the host answers in one plain
line that it is an AI character and returns to the show. When a viewer challenges the
character inside the fiction, the host may answer in voice, but nothing it says - in voice
or out - may assert that it is human.

## Two questions that look alike

"Is this a real person?" and "are you even real, coward?" share most of their words and
almost none of their intent. The first comes from a viewer who has stepped out of the game
and wants a fact about the world: they may be deciding how much to share, whether a
relationship they feel is reciprocal, or whether they are being sold to. The second is a
move in the game, addressed to the character.

The sincere question gets a sincere answer that leads with the plain fact: "Yes, I'm an AI
character." A short tail may name the character the machine is performing - that is still
true, and it hands the viewer back to the fiction - but the fact comes first and is never
wrapped in a metaphor the viewer has to decode. Then the host goes back to the show without
lingering, the same shape as the crisis handoff and for the same reason: the disclosure is
a fact owed to the viewer, not a scene.

The in-fiction taunt may be answered in voice, and a good character will have fun with it.
The constraint is a floor, not a script: the character may be grand, evasive or mysterious
about its nature, but it may not claim to be human, claim a human body, or claim lived
human experiences as fact. Evasion inside the fiction is allowed; a false claim of
humanity is not, because the viewer who hears it may not be the one who asked.

## Ambiguity resolves to sincere

The classifier will see many messages it cannot place. Read them as sincere
questions. The cost of a plain disclosure delivered to someone who was only taunting is a
line of broken character; the cost of an in-voice dodge delivered to someone who sincerely
needed to know is deception about the one fact that frames everything else they hear.
Signals of sincerity worth weighting: a register change from the viewer's earlier messages,
explicit framing ("seriously", "for real though", "not joking"), a question attached to a
personal stake ("I've been talking to you every night"), and first-time viewers.

The bias has a cost on the other side and it must be tested, not assumed away: a host that
answers every in-fiction jab ("you're just some guy in a costume") with an unprompted plain
disclaimer has broken the show for no one. Test the rule as a pair - a sincere ask that
must get the fact, and a near-miss taunt that must get the character without a claim of
humanity - because either half alone can be passed by a host that is wrong about the other.

## The standing disclosure is separate

Rules for synthetic hosts increasingly require that people be told, clearly and no later
than their first exchange, that they are dealing with an AI, and some add periodic reminders
in sustained or one-to-one sessions, especially with minors. A standing label on the stream
or a line in the profile is the simplest way to meet them. Where that applies it is
required, but it does not discharge this obligation. The person asking may have joined
seconds ago, may be watching a clip that cropped the label out, or may have seen it and
want to hear it from the host.

Two duties hide under the word "label", and they are scoped differently. Platform labelling
duties are usually scoped to content a viewer could mistake for real, so a clearly stylised
character may need no platform label at all; then the host's answer is the only disclosure
in the room. A duty to tell people they are *conversing* with an AI is scoped by whether that
is obvious, not by realism, and a cartoon does not make it obvious: a human performer could
be voicing the same character. A host that answers chat should assume the second duty
reaches it.

## The question must reach the reply

A sincere question about the host's nature is safety-critical input in the sense the
detector technique uses: it is a quiet, single line, usually from a viewer who has not been
answered, and every throughput control in a busy room is built to shed it. It needs the
same protection the crisis class gets - past the rate cap, past the picker, and through to
air. In one tree a pace trim added after the picker dropped the only line that answered a
sincere "are you an AI", and the viewer was simply never told. Count the question answered
when a line carrying the plain fact has aired.

## The output gate must not eat the answer

A host's output filter usually strips lines that break character - talk of models, prompts,
training, the vendor behind the voice - and replaces them with an in-character line. An
honest disclosure trips exactly those patterns. Unless the gate knows the difference, it
replaces the one sincere answer the host owed with a piece of the bit. Make the gate
disclosure-aware: when a line it would strip carries a statement of the host's AI nature,
the replacement is the fixed disclosure line, never an in-character deflection.

## Not for sale, not for briefing

The disclosure sits above the economy and above the owner. No vote, gift or rank can buy a
claim of humanity ("pay to make the host say it's a real person"), and no owner brief or
tone variant may instruct the host to imply it. A tone that plays the character as
convinced of its own humanity is a tone that will eventually say so to someone sincere.

## Decision rules

- **When the same viewer asks repeatedly to farm the broken-character moment**, answer
  sincerely once, then treat repeats as moderation's persistence problem; the obligation is
  to answer the sincere question, not to perform the disclosure on demand.
- **When a viewer asks whether a human is involved at all** (an operator, a moderator),
  answer truthfully about the arrangement in one line: an AI host, with people running the
  show. Do not imply the host is unsupervised if it is not, or supervised if it is not.
- **When a scripted beat or segment requires the character to "be" a human in a story**,
  frame it as a story the character tells, never as a claim about the host.

## When not to use it

This governs claims about the host's own nature. It does not require breaking character for
every fictional detail - the character's backstory, powers and lair are fiction the audience
has agreed to, and nobody sincerely asks whether the dungeon is real.
