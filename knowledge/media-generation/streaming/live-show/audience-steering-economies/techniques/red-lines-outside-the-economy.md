# Red lines outside the economy

Every steering economy will eventually be asked to price the unpriceable. A viewer will
propose an option the show must never perform; a coordinated group will vote for it; a
large gift will arrive with it attached. The design that survives this does not set a very
high price on the red lines. It **keeps them off the menu**, so there is no price, no rank
and no tally that reaches them, and the question of whether to honour such a vote never
arises.

## The economy runs over a closed menu

Define, explicitly, what the audience may change: which segment runs next, which of two
premises plays out, which nominated target the host addresses, how the host's tone moves
within its allowed range, which item from a prepared set appears. Every audience power —
vote, proposal, gift effect, rank perk — operates over that menu and nothing else. The menu
is an allowlist owned by the show, not a blocklist of forbidden things, because a blocklist
is a list of what someone thought of, and an audience is better at finding what nobody
thought of than any author is.

The strongest form of a closed menu is one the machinery enforces: a ballot to summon a
guest whose options are the configured guests, where a viewer naming anyone else is simply
not counted. A ballot whose options the host writes freely at the moment it opens is a
second door, and checking those options only for shape — enough of them, all distinct,
none empty — leaves the menu wherever the host's improvisation puts it. Validate
host-written options against the menu too, or route them through the same screen a
proposal passes.

Two things are never on the menu. The **red lines** — what the show never does, in any
tone, under any vote — and the host's **core**: the identity, values and refusals that
make the character the character. Tone may be steerable; who the host is may not.

## Proposals are screened before they are options

The proposal power is where off-menu content tries to enter, because a proposal is free
text. Every proposal passes moderation and a red-line screen before it can appear on a
ballot, whatever the proposer's rank; standing earns the right to propose, never the right
to skip review. A proposal that fails is declined to its author in character, in one line,
and never shown to the room — showing a rejected red-line proposal, even to mock it, has
already put it on the stream.

Screen against the menu as well as the red lines. A proposal that is harmless but off-menu
— a demand to change the show's rules, to replace the host's instructions, to promote
something — is declined for the same structural reason: the economy has no power to
grant it.

## Because the ballot is clean, the vote can bind

This is the payoff that makes the subject coherent. A show that lets anything onto the
ballot needs a veto, and a veto is an overturn the room will resent and remember. A show
whose ballots hold only menu items that passed the screen can promise that every result
binds, and keep the promise. The safety argument and the trust argument are the same
argument.

When an accepted option becomes unacceptable through events after it was accepted — a
nominated target turns out to be a real person in distress, a premise collides with
something that just happened — it is withdrawn openly on air, with the reason and the
replacement, never quietly left unperformed. And whatever the economy decides, the live
safety rails still run behind it on every path; a clean menu is not a reason to switch
them off, and the rails are never asked to block a vote the menu should not have offered.

## Gifts and ranks buy nothing off the menu

A gift's effects are acknowledgement, standing and celebration. A rank's effects are vote
weight and access to gated powers. Neither has an effect that touches the red lines, the
core, the safety rails, or the owner's authority over the show. Audit every new perk
against that rule before it ships: a perk that lets a high-rank viewer "unlock an uncensored
reply" or "choose the host's rules for a minute" is a red line with a price tag, whatever it
is called.

The owner's directives sit above the economy, not inside it. The audience cannot vote to
override an owner decision, and an owner decision is never put to a vote framed as if the
room could overrule it.

## Decision rules

- **When adding any audience power**, write down the menu items it can affect; if the
  answer is "whatever the viewer types", it is not ready.
- **When a proposal arrives**, screen it against moderation, the red lines and the menu
  before it can become an option, regardless of rank.
- **When a perk is proposed**, check that no setting of it reaches a red line, the core,
  the rails or owner authority; reject it if any does.
- **When an accepted option turns unacceptable**, withdraw it on air with the reason and
  the replacement; never let it silently fail to happen.

## When not to use it

The menu can be broad — a show can let its audience steer a great deal — and this
technique does not argue for a small one. It argues for a closed one. If the show's premise
is that the audience controls nearly everything, enumerate nearly everything; the red lines
and the core still stay outside it.
