# Gift as in-world tribute

When a viewer gives to a live show, the thing they are buying is mostly the moment of
being seen giving. The acknowledgement is the product. A show that reads gift names off a
list in a flat voice has wasted that moment; a show that lets gifts purchase control over
what the host says has sold something it must never sell. The design that works sits
between them: the gift is translated into the show's own fiction, and the host receives it
there, in character, by name.

## Translate the gift into the world

Every show with a premise has a natural shape for tribute. A villain receives offerings
for the cause; a game-show host banks contributions to the jackpot; a cosy host is handed
a cup of tea. Map each kind of gift to an in-world act and let the host respond as the
character would to that act. The viewer is not a customer completing a purchase; they are
a participant who threw a coin on stage, and the character reacts to the coin.

The translation does three jobs at once. It keeps the acknowledgement in the show's voice,
so the stream does not lurch into a sponsor read every time someone gives. It gives the
host something to play — a villain can be gracious, greedy or suspicious about a tribute,
and each is a bit. And it frames what the gift is: a gesture inside the world, not a ticket
to change the world's rules.

## Scale the acknowledgement, and cap it

Bigger gifts deserve bigger moments, but the scale grows **sub-linearly** and stops at a
ceiling. A gift ten times larger earns a noticeably larger reaction, not ten times the
airtime; the very largest gifts earn a set-piece, not the next quarter-hour. Acknowledgement
that scales linearly with spend converts the host's attention into an auction, and the
non-giving majority watches the show become a conversation with the wealthiest few. How
much of the host's attention any one viewer may hold over a window is governed by the
absorption caps; this technique decides the shape of the tribute within that allowance.

One thing the caps must never do is swallow the acknowledgement itself. A heavy giver is,
almost by definition, a viewer who talks a lot, and a per-viewer rate cap tuned against
chatty viewers will drop the very message that carries their largest gift unless gifts are
exempt from it. The rule is that every gift receives its acknowledgement; what the caps
bound is the airtime around it — the follow-up banter, the second and third callbacks —
never the tribute.

Bursts collapse. When several gifts land within seconds — a celebration chain, a raid of
giving — the host acknowledges them together, naming the largest or the first and the
count, rather than delivering a dozen near-identical lines.

## Gifts given to others

A viewer who buys standing for other viewers has done two things, and both are
acknowledged: the giver is credited — they paid for a moment and it is theirs — and the
receivers are welcomed, by name where the room is small enough for names, as a group where
it is not. The most common mistake is to celebrate the gift and forget the receivers, or to
greet the receivers as if they had given. **Who gave** and **who received** are read from
the event, never from the host's impression of the chat: misattribution here costs the
giver their moment in public, which is worse than no acknowledgement at all. This is the
case a generative host gets wrong — measured, a smaller model failed it on more than
twice as many streams as a larger one, by ignoring the gift or naming the wrong
giver — which is why the names belong in the host's context as data rather than in the
chat it has to reread.

The event means the platform's record of the gift, not a chat line that describes one. A
message that merely says it gave five gifts is text any viewer can type; treating it as a
gift lets anyone claim a tribute, skip the caps, and be thanked for money never spent.
Where only chat text is available, acknowledge the claim lightly and reserve the full
tribute, and any rise in standing, for gifts the platform records.

## What a gift buys, and what it never buys

A gift may buy acknowledgement, a rise in standing — on a curve that, like vote weight,
pays sub-linearly — and an in-world celebration. It may never buy content outside the
show's menu of steerable things, a break of the host's character, a crossing of any red
line, or priority over a viewer in distress. "Gift and the host will say anything" is the
design that ends shows. A viewer who attaches a demand to a gift receives the tribute
graciously and the demand declined, in character, in one line.

## Never ask with guilt

The host may make giving feel good; the host may not make not-giving feel bad. No targets
spoken as debts, no singling out of viewers who have not given, no pressure aimed at viewers
who seem young or vulnerable, no sulking when a stretch passes without gifts. A villain
host may demand tribute as a running bit only where the bit is unmistakably part of the
fiction and nobody is pressed individually.

## Decision rules

- **When designing acknowledgements**, map each gift kind to an in-world act the host
  responds to in character; never a flat list.
- **When sizing a reaction**, grow it sub-linearly with the gift and cap it; collapse bursts
  into one acknowledgement with a count.
- **When gifts are given to others**, credit the giver and welcome the receivers, both read
  from the event.
- **When a gift carries a demand**, accept the tribute, decline the demand in character, and
  never let the gift unlock anything off the menu.

## When not to use it

A show without a fiction — a plain talk stream, a tutorial — has no world to translate
into, and a sincere, warm thank-you in the host's own voice is the right acknowledgement.
And the in-world framing never applies to a gift accompanied by a sincere message of
distress: that viewer is answered as a person, and the bit waits.
