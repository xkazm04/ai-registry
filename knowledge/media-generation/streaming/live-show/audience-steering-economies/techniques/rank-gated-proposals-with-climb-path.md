# Rank-gated proposals with a climb path

A vote chooses among options; a proposal **writes** one. The difference is the difference
between pressing a button and holding the pen. A proposal is free text from a viewer that,
if accepted, is shown to the whole room as something the show might do. It needs more
trust than a vote, it costs more to screen, and in a busy room it arrives faster than any
host could read. So authorship is a power, it is gated by standing, and every gate comes
with a visible way through it.

## What to gate, and where

Grade each power by what it lets a viewer put in front of the room. Casting a vote on
vetted options needs no standing at all; it is the room's basic right on the show.
Proposing an option, nominating a target for a segment, naming a challenge — anything
whose text may be shown or spoken — is gated. Higher-impact powers, such as proposing a
change to the running order, sit higher still.

Set the proposal gate at a rank a regular reaches within a few streams of honest
participation. Too low and the gate filters nothing: a new account proposes within minutes
of arriving and the screen drowns. Too high and proposing becomes the private hobby of a
dozen veterans, the ballots stop reflecting the room, and the climb looks pointless to
everyone below it. The right height is the point at which a viewer has shown up, been
answered, and voted — evidence of good faith that a throwaway account would have to spend
real time to fake.

The gate is checked by code against the recorded rank, at the moment of the attempt.
A viewer's claim of standing in chat is not standing, and neither is the host's impression
of who is a regular.

A ballot restricted by rank is a different instrument from a ballot with weights. It is
legitimate — a vote of the inner circle is a perk worth climbing for, and it makes good
theatre — but it must be named as what it is when it opens, so that a viewer whose vote is
not counted knows why. A rank floor quietly applied to a ballot the room believes is
theirs is the same snub as a dropped proposal, multiplied by everyone below the floor.

## The refusal is the first rung

A low-rank viewer who tries to propose has just told the show they want to take part. A
cold refusal — or worse, silence — tells them the show is a club and they are outside it.
The reply that works is warm, in the host's voice, one line, and **carries the path**: what
rank proposing needs, and the concrete way to get there. "Ambitious. Proposals open at the
third rank — stay, vote, get noticed, and you'll be writing my ballots by Friday." The
refusal becomes an invitation and the viewer leaves with a goal.

Never drop the attempt silently. An unacknowledged proposal looks to its author like a
broken feature or a deliberate snub, and to anyone watching like a host who ignores the
room. The refusal is a state the viewer can see, not an absence.

Do not over-answer. In a large room, repeated low-rank attempts are answered once per
viewer with the path, then counted quietly; the host does not deliver the same refusal
twenty times in a minute. How often the host may speak to any one viewer is the absorption
caps' concern.

## The climb must be climbable without paying

The climb path the refusal names must exist for a viewer who spends nothing. Participation
— being present, voting, being answered, returning — has to move a viewer up the ladder at
a pace a regular can feel. Spend may shorten the climb; if it is the only route, the refusal
becomes a sales pitch, and the room hears it as one.

## After the gate

Passing the gate makes a viewer eligible to propose; it does not make the proposal an
option. Every proposal still clears moderation and the red-line screen before it can
appear on a ballot, and a proposer is limited in how many open proposals they may hold so
that one eager veteran cannot fill the ballot. When a proposal becomes an option, credit
it to its author by name — a proposal is authorship, and authorship is what the proposer
climbed for.

An accepted proposal needs somewhere to go. A gate that admits proposals into a queue the
show never reads — counted, stored, never surfaced — has converted the reward for climbing
into a void, and the climber finds out on the first night their proposal disappears. Every
accepted proposal reaches one of three ends the proposer can see: it becomes an option, it
is held for a named later ballot, or it is declined in character with a reason.

## Decision rules

- **When a power lets viewer text reach the room**, gate it by rank; when it only selects
  among vetted options, leave it open to everyone.
- **When setting the gate**, place it where a regular arrives within a few streams of
  participation alone, and re-check it against the real rank distribution: if almost
  nobody can propose, lower it; if new accounts are proposing, raise it.
- **When refusing**, one warm in-character line with the rank needed and how to reach it;
  once per viewer, never silently.
- **When a proposal passes the gate**, screen it as if it had not; rank earns the right to
  propose, never the right to bypass review.

## When not to use it

On a very small show, where the host knows every chatter, a rank gate is ceremony: let the
host take suggestions conversationally and decide openly. And never gate a power that
should belong to everyone because the show is busy — throughput problems are solved by
absorption, not by turning a right into a rank perk.
