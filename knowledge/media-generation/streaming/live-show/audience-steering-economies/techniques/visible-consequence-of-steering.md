# Visible consequence of steering

A vote can be honoured and still be wasted. If the room cannot see the line from its
choice to a change in the show — because the change is hidden, late, or unattributed — the
experience is identical to not having voted, and the room stops bothering. The payoff of
steering is the moment a viewer watches the show do what they chose, and hears the host
say so. Design for that moment.

## Steer what the room can see

Choose steerable things whose change is perceptible: the next segment, the target of a
bit, which of two premises plays out, which ending a scene gets, the tone the host takes
for the next stretch within its allowed range. Avoid ballots over parameters the audience
cannot perceive — a hidden difficulty level, an internal weighting, a background choice
nobody will notice. If a reasonable viewer could not tell from watching which option won,
the ballot is decoration.

The two outcomes of a ballot must also be meaningfully different. A vote between two
options that play out almost identically is a vote with no consequence, and the room can
tell.

## Soon, and in the same breath as the result

The consequence lands close to the result: within the same segment, ideally within a
minute or so of the close. Every second between "the room has spoken" and the change
weakens the causal link the viewer draws. When the consequence must wait — the chosen
segment starts after the current one ends — the host says so at the close and calls back
to the vote when it begins: "you voted for this, twenty minutes ago; here it is". The
announcement and the enactment are joined by a "so": you chose this, so this happens.

## Attribution comes from the record

Every claim the host makes about a result is a claim about data, and it is read from the
data. Who won and by what margin come from the tally. Whose proposal it was comes from the
proposal record. Which viewers voted for the losing side, if the host wants to tease them,
comes from the ballot. A generative host that composes these from its impression of the
chat will misattribute — thank the loudest viewer for a quiet viewer's proposal, call a
narrow result a landslide, say "everyone" over a dozen votes — and each of those errors
tells somebody their participation was invisible. Credit the room when the room won, the
proposer by name when a proposal won, and never a guessed individual.

The record matters most at the moment of the announcement, because that is when the host
is improvising and the vote is several turns behind it. Put the winner, the margin and any
author into the host's context at the close, by name, so the line it composes is a reading
rather than a recollection. A judge reading a host transcript will find the recollection
failing in exactly the way described: the right option announced, the wrong viewer
thanked for it.

The screen is a second announcer and is held to the same rule. A bar marked as the winner, a
margin drawn as a bar length, or a tag saying the room disagrees with the host is a claim
about the tally. The screen reads it from the decision the tally made: the winner, the tie,
the per-viewer count. It never recomputes it from the raw numbers by a rule of its own. A
renderer that recomputes keeps whichever parts of the rule its author copied. It crowns
both sides of a weight tie the runtime broke by heads, or the earlier option after the
runtime started calling level ties honestly, or it counts a repeated message as a second
vote. The room then watches the screen and the host name different winners for one ballot.
Where the decision is not in the record yet, the tally should carry it. Until it does, one
shared function that mirrors the deciding rule, with tests, is the floor.

Margins carry their own honesty. "By a hair" and "by a mile" are both good television when
they are true and both corrosive when they are not. If the host cannot read the margin, the
host does not characterise it.

## The show remembers

A result is not finished when it is enacted. Callbacks — "the night you made me read the
complaints aloud", "last week's ballot put the trap door in; tonight it opens" — turn a run
of results into a history the room authored, and that history is what brings a viewer back
to vote again. Keep a record of past ballots, winners and notable proposers that the host
can cite, and cite it rather than remember it.

## Never fake a consequence

The worst version of this technique is the counterfeit: a vote whose "consequence" would
have happened anyway, or a show that claims the room chose something it was always going
to do. The room finds out — a clipped segment, a viewer who voted the other way and saw the
same result last week — and from then on every visible consequence is suspected of being
staged.

## Decision rules

- **When choosing what to put to a vote**, pick something whose change a viewer will see
  and whose options play out differently; reject ballots over invisible parameters.
- **When a ballot closes**, announce the winner and enact it in the same stretch, joined by
  cause; if enactment must wait, say when and call back to the vote when it lands.
- **When crediting**, read names and margins from the record; credit the room for room
  wins and the author for proposal wins; never credit by impression.
- **When a screen draws a ballot**, it shows the tally's decision (winner, tie, one vote
  per viewer) and never re-derives it; a second copy of the winner rule is a second result.
- **After the stream**, keep the ballot history where the host can cite it next time.

## When not to use it

Some steering is meant to be slow — a long-running ballot that shapes the next season, a
cumulative tally across a month. Its consequence cannot be immediate, so the technique
shifts from immediacy to progress: show the running count at the start of every stream so
the room watches its choice accumulate. And on a tiny show, where the host can thank each
voter personally, the ceremony of announcing margins can be replaced by conversation.
