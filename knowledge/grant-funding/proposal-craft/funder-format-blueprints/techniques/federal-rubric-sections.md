# Federal rubric sections

The concern: a federal (or national-agency) notice of funding opportunity
publishes review criteria with **point allocations**, and panel reviewers
score the application by working down that rubric — every sub-criterion in
the notice reappears on the scoring sheet. The canonical narrative
dimensions are *statement of need*, *approach*, *organizational capacity*,
and *evaluation plan*, plus a budget with justification. The structural
rule: these are **separate sections, never one crammed narrative** — a
single narrative blob forces the reviewer to hunt for each scored
dimension, and points not found are points not awarded.

## The four sections and what each must contain

**Statement of need.** The problem and its magnitude with cited data; the
target population and geography defined precisely; the gap this project
fills made explicit. Reviewers score need **on evidence, not adjectives**
— lead with verifiable numbers (the baseline, the gap, the consequence)
and use at most one short human example to make the scale concrete. Where
a figure is missing, a bracketed placeholder beats an invented statistic;
a reviewer who catches one fabricated number discounts every other.

**Approach.** Goals decomposed into measurable, time-bound objectives;
the activities and methodology that meet them; the work plan and timeline;
and the rationale for why this design will work (evidence base, prior
results, model fidelity). Tie every objective back to the stated need —
reviewers explicitly check the need→objective chain, and an objective with
no anchoring need reads as scope creep.

**Organizational capacity.** Proof the organization can execute: relevant
experience and past performance, key staff and their qualifications,
partnerships with letters of commitment, and the infrastructure and
systems in place (financial management, data systems, oversight).
Capacity commonly carries a substantial share of total points — it is a
scored argument, not a boilerplate "about us".

**Evaluation plan.** Process and outcome measures tied to the objectives
one-for-one; data sources and collection methods; who conducts the
evaluation; and how findings feed continuous improvement and reporting.
A measures table whose rows are the approach section's objectives is the
strongest possible shape, because it makes the alignment scoreable at a
glance.

## Decision rules

- **The published rubric is the outline.** Read the review criteria before
  writing anything; if the notice weights a dimension at N points and
  lists three sub-questions, the section answers all three, in order, in
  the notice's own vocabulary.
- **A simplified notice still scores by rubric.** National grantmakers
  have been shortening notices and rewriting them in plain language —
  fewer requested elements, less legalese — without changing panel review
  itself. Read a thinner criteria section with the same discipline: terser
  sub-criteria still reappear on the score sheet, and a plainer notice
  never licenses a looser narrative.
- **Allocate length by points.** A dimension worth a quarter of the score
  does not get a tenth of the words. Word bands per section keep the
  allocation honest at both drafting and review time.
- **Every objective appears three times.** Once in approach (stated), once
  in the timeline (scheduled), once in evaluation (measured). A reviewer
  cross-checks; a dangling objective in any of the three costs points in
  the other two.
- **Quantify or placeholder — never pad.** The register is evidentiary and
  concrete. Adjectival intensity ("critical", "urgent", "innovative")
  scores zero; it is the numbers and citations that move points.
- **Regional and municipal government notices follow the same shape.**
  When a sub-national government call publishes no distinct structure,
  structure the response as a federal rubric application — the reviewer
  culture is inherited, even when the rubric is thinner.

## When not to use it

Not for foundations, trusts, or arts panels — the evidentiary register
that wins rubric points reads as cold and impersonal to a trustee, and
the four-dimension structure over-answers a two-page form. Not for
supranational award-criteria calls, which score a different criterion set
under different names; the families overlap in spirit (both are
rubric-driven) but not in form.
