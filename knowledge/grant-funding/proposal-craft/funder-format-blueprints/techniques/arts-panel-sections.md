# Arts panel sections

The concern: public arts funders — arts councils, cultural agencies,
endowment programmes — convene panels that score a project-grant triad:
**the artistic work itself**, **who it reaches**, and **whether it will
actually happen**. The blueprint is three sections plus budget: artistic
quality and ambition, public engagement and audience, management and
feasibility. Panels are typically composed of practitioners and sector
peers, which shapes the register: they can tell when an application talks
*about* art without describing any, and adjectival self-praise is the
fastest way to lose them.

## The three sections and what each must contain

**Artistic quality & ambition.** Describe the work — the vision, the
artists involved and their practice, and why the project is ambitious and
distinctive *for these artists at this point*. Ground it in the work, not
adjectives: what will be made or performed, in what form, and what makes
it excellent and timely. The strongest quality sections read like a
practitioner describing a real project — repertoire, collaborators,
process — and let ambition emerge from specifics. Attach or reference work
samples where the form allows; for many panels the samples carry more of
the quality score than the prose.

**Public engagement & audience.** Who the work reaches and how: the
audiences and participants, how you will develop and diversify them,
access and inclusion provisions, and the difference the work makes for
them. Include a **concrete reach figure or target** — a number of
audience members, participants, or sessions — because "the public" is not
an audience and panels discount engagement sections that never commit to
one. Participation (people making or shaping the work) scores differently
from attendance; say which you mean.

**Management & feasibility.** How the project gets delivered: timeline
and key milestones, the team and partners, venues and production
arrangements, risk management — and, increasingly in this family, the
environmental impact of the activity. Panels have seen ambitious projects
collapse on logistics; a realistic schedule with named venues and
confirmed partners outweighs a visionary one with neither.

## Decision rules

- **The work first, instrumentally-framed benefits second.** Where a
  programme's published criteria weight artistic quality highest, do not
  lead with social outcomes; where a participatory or community-arts
  stream weights engagement highest, invert the emphasis — read the
  stream's own criteria and mirror their order.
- **Specifics are the register.** Name the artists, the form, the
  repertoire, the venues. Every sentence that could describe any project
  is a sentence the panel skims.
- **Commit to a reach number.** One honest figure with a basis ("three
  performances at a 200-seat venue plus a schools tour of eight
  workshops") beats a vague large one.
- **Feasibility is evidence, not assertion.** Confirmed partners, held
  dates, and quotes behind budget lines; a panel scores deliverability on
  what is already secured.
- **Match the band.** These sections run shorter than rubric-competition
  narratives — a quality section past several hundred words is usually
  padding the work with adjectives.

## When not to use it

Only when the funder is an arts panel or the call is an arts stream. An
arts project applying to a community fund uses the trust/lottery form
(need, activities, difference) — the same project, restructured around
community outcomes. An arts organization answering a federal cultural
agency's rubric-scored notice follows the published rubric, which may
resemble this triad but must be mirrored from the notice itself.
