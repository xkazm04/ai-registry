# Problem-population opening

The opening of a funding narrative decides whether a tired reviewer keeps
reading with interest or scans for reasons to score low. The technique is to
open with **the problem and the people it affects** — specific, documented,
local — and to earn the organization's entrance only as *the answer to that
problem*, never as the subject of the story.

## Procedure

1. **Name the problem as a specific condition, not a topic.** "Youth
   disengagement" is a topic; "students in a named district who read two
   grade levels behind by fourth grade and lose access to the district's
   only tutoring program at the end of this school year" is a problem. The
   test: could the reader restate what is wrong, for whom, where, and with
   what urgency, from the first two sentences?
2. **Make the population concrete before making it large.** Who exactly:
   age, place, circumstance, the barrier that keeps existing services from
   reaching them. A precise small population outscores a vague large one —
   reviewers fund tractable problems, and precision signals the applicant
   actually knows these people.
3. **Document, don't emote.** The most pervasive weakness reviewers report
   is a problem described but never documented. Every empirical claim in the
   opening carries a figure or a source — the applicant's own intake data,
   a public statistic, a published study. Where a figure would strengthen
   the case and none is verified, a bracketed placeholder for the writer
   beats a plausible invention; a wrong number the applicant signs is worse
   than a blank they fill.
4. **Connect the problem to the funder's stated priorities** — in the
   funder's own language where the solicitation provides it. Misalignment
   with priorities is the single most common rejection reason, and the
   opening is where alignment is either established or lost.
5. **Enter the organization as the response.** Only after the problem
   stands does the applicant appear — and immediately as mechanism ("X was
   built to close this gap"), not as biography.

## Sizing

A standalone need statement runs tight — on the order of two hundred to two
hundred fifty words: problem and population first, the program's approach as
the bridge, and the request amount connected to a concrete fundable outcome
aligned with the stated priorities. Inside a longer narrative the same
material is the first movement, proportionally sized to the genre's budget
(and, under a scored rubric, to the need criterion's point value).

## Decision rules

- When the draft's first sentence contains the organization's name, invert
  it — lead with the condition, close the paragraph with the org — because
  the reader's first question is "what is wrong", not "who are you".
- When a claim in the opening has no source, either attach one from the
  verified fact set or soften the claim to what is defensible; never let an
  unsourced statistic stand as fact.
- When the population description would fit any city and any demographic,
  it is not yet a population; add the qualifiers that make it *these*
  people.
- When the funder's own materials name the problem, echo their framing and
  vocabulary — the reviewer scores against the funder's language, not the
  applicant's.

## When not to use it

- **Arts-panel narratives.** The arts genre leads with the work and the
  artistic vision, not a deficit statement; forcing a problem-first opening
  onto an artistic-excellence application reads as genre misfit
  (see [genre-register-classification](./genre-register-classification.md)).
- **Post-award reporting.** A report to a funder who already gave opens
  with what was done and what changed, not with re-arguing the need.
- **Advocacy framing.** The opening still names a problem, but a *systemic*
  one with a power analysis — who holds power over the condition — rather
  than a service-gap statement (see
  [theory-of-change-framing](./theory-of-change-framing.md)).
