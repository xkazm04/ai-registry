# Trust and lottery sections

The concern: charitable trusts and lottery-distributed community funds ask
short, plain questions and are read by trustees or community assessors —
not scoring panels working down a points rubric. The dominant form across
this family is three or four questions: **the need**, **what you will do**
(your activities), **the difference it will make**, and — on the fuller
community-fund forms — **about your organisation**, plus a simple budget.
The register is the technique's core: a clear, honest, community-grounded
case beats scale, and rubric-competition language ("evidence-based
intervention", "logic model") reads as not knowing who you are talking to.

## The sections and what each must contain

**The need.** Who is affected, where, and the evidence for it — but
evidence in this family includes local data, consultation, and lived
experience, not only published statistics. The distinctive expectation is
the **community's voice**: show the need is real *and that the people
affected shaped the response*. Community funds increasingly assess
co-design explicitly; a need statement written entirely from the
organisation's viewpoint is the family's most common weak opening.

**Your activities / what you'll do.** What the grant actually funds — the
activities, who delivers them, where and when, how people take part. Be
concrete and realistic for the funding period, and be specific about what
*this* funder's money makes possible: trustees want to picture their grant
at work, not fund an undifferentiated slice of a programme. Tie each
activity back to the need.

**The difference it will make.** The outcomes for the people and
communities involved, with a way to know you achieved them — a number or a
clear indicator, stated plainly. Distinguish short-term outputs (sessions
run, people reached) from the lasting change. Funders in this family often
report back against exactly this section, so write it as the promise you
will later account for.

**About your organisation** (fuller forms). Track record, the people and
volunteers who deliver, relevant partnerships, governance — and
**safeguarding**, which is a hard expectation whenever the work touches
children or vulnerable adults. Have the supporting documents ready:
governing document, recent accounts, bank details, safeguarding policy;
this family verifies organisational basics rather than scoring capacity
prose.

## Decision rules

- **Shorter is stronger.** Most trusts read hundreds of applications with
  no professional review layer; answer the question asked and stop. The
  lighter trust form drops the organisation section entirely — need,
  activities, difference, budget.
- **The modest register is the safe register.** Grounded, first-person,
  specific to the community served. Claims of being unique or
  transformational cost credibility; a clear honest case over scale is
  what trustees say they fund, and observably what they do fund.
- **One indicator per outcome, not a measurement framework.** "We'll know
  because N people will X" is the right altitude; an evaluation-plan
  apparatus over-answers the form.
- **Name the change their grant buys.** Where the grant is a fraction of a
  budget, attribute honestly — trustees fund differences, not deficits.
- **Portal wins over instinct.** Where a specific community-fund portal
  publishes its own question set, follow it verbatim, even where it
  deviates from this family shape.

## When not to use it

Not for rubric-scored government competitions — the modest register and
three-question structure forfeit points a federal reviewer is required to
allocate elsewhere. Not for supranational calls, arts panels with
published criteria, or movement funders, each of which reads for content
this form never elicits. This family shape is the right *default register*
when the funder is unknown, but not the right *structure* once a scoring
family is identified.
