# Movement funder sections

The concern: funders of advocacy, organizing, and systems-change work read
through a distinct lens — **power**. Their program officers assess not
whether a service will be delivered but whether a campaign can win, and
whether the people most affected are leading it. The blueprint is four
sections plus budget: **the problem and who it harms**, **strategy and
theory of change**, **base, coalition and leadership**, and **how the
change will be measured** — where "measured" means power-building
indicators, not service outputs. A service-delivery application
transplanted into this family reads as fundamentally misaddressed: it
answers "what will you do for people" where the funder asks "what will you
win with them".

## The four sections and what each must contain

**The problem & who's affected.** Name the injustice or policy problem,
who it harms and how — with evidence — and, distinctively, **the systems
or decision-makers that hold it in place**. A problem statement without an
actor is a weather report; this family expects the analysis to identify
where the power to change things sits. Center the people most affected and
*their* analysis of the problem, not just the issue in the abstract.

**Strategy & theory of change.** The specific change sought; the strategy
and tactics to win it (organizing, advocacy, litigation, narrative work);
the targets and decision-points where pressure lands; and why this
approach can move them **within the grant period**. The theory of change
is a causal argument — "if we do X, then Y shifts, because Z" — not a
diagram or a mission restatement. Honest strategy sections name what must
be true for the plan to work; program officers in this family are former
organizers more often than not, and they read for strategic realism.

**Base, coalition & leadership.** The power behind the work: the base and
how it is built, the coalition and partners, the **leadership of
directly-affected people** (governance, staff, campaign leadership — not
just testimony), and the track record of campaigns moved. This is the
family's capacity section, but the asset being audited is constituency
and legitimacy, not systems and staffing.

**How you'll measure the change.** How you will know you are winning:
campaign milestones and **power-building indicators** — leaders developed,
base grown, coalition breadth, policy and narrative shifts — how they are
tracked, and how strategy adapts from what is learned. Campaigns lose and
still build power; the measurement section should make that legible,
because a funder who only sees the policy outcome will misread a
capacity-building year as failure.

## Decision rules

- **Name targets and decision-points.** A strategy that never names who
  decides, and when, is not scoreable in this family.
- **Affected-community leadership is structural, not decorative.** Show it
  in governance and staffing, with proportions where they are true and
  known — never invented.
- **Win-plausibility over win-certainty.** Overclaiming a guaranteed
  policy win reads as naive; a credible path with named contingencies
  reads as experienced.
- **Measure power and policy, both.** Outputs (actions held, doors
  knocked) support but never replace indicators of grown capacity.
- **Adaptation is a feature.** This family funds strategy under
  uncertainty; describing how the plan changes when conditions change
  scores as maturity, not vagueness.

## When not to use it

Not for service-delivery applications, even to progressive funders — if
the call scores need/approach/capacity/evaluation, use the rubric form.
Not for public-agency funders: government sources rarely fund lobbying
and this framing can create compliance exposure; the same organization
approaching a government source restructures around the permissible
activities. When genre is uncertain — an organization that both organizes
and delivers services — default to the generic form rather than guessing
this one; a power frame misapplied is harder to walk back than a modest
frame under-applied.
