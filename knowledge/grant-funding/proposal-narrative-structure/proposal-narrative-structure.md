# Proposal narrative structure

A funding narrative is not a description of an organization; it is an
**argument addressed to a specific reader** — a program officer or review
panel deciding, under time pressure, whether this money moves. The naive
reading of this subject is "write compellingly about the mission". The
principal reading is that a fundable narrative has a load-bearing skeleton —
**problem → population → model → evidence → what this funder's dollars
unlock** — and that the skeleton is *rendered in a different register per
funder genre*, because a federal reviewer, a foundation program officer, an
arts panel and a movement funder are reading for different things and scoring
against different rubrics. One generic template misfits all of them.

## The argument, link by link

Each link answers the reader's next objection, in order:

1. **The problem and the population** — not the organization. The opening
   names a specific problem, the specific people it affects, and evidence
   that it is real, before the applicant's name matters at all. Reviewers
   consistently flag "a problem described but never documented" as the most
   pervasive narrative weakness; an opening about the org instead of the
   need is its twin ([problem-population-opening](techniques/problem-population-opening.md)).
2. **The model** — what this organization specifically does about it. "We
   provide programming" is a category, not a model. The narrative must let
   the reader reconstruct the intervention: who does what, for whom, how
   often, through what mechanism the change happens
   ([program-model-specificity](techniques/program-model-specificity.md)).
3. **The evidence** — why anyone should believe the model works: the
   applicant's own measured results where they exist, the published evidence
   base for the intervention class where they do not, each claim carrying a
   source. Reviewers are trained to hunt claims without evidence
   ([evidence-base-citation](techniques/evidence-base-citation.md)).
4. **The unlock** — what *this funder's dollars specifically* make possible
   that would not otherwise happen. Not "support our work": the marginal,
   concrete difference between funded and unfunded, sized to the request
   ([funder-dollars-unlock-close](techniques/funder-dollars-unlock-close.md)).

The links must actually connect. The most common structural failure is the
**circular narrative**: the need section asserts a problem, the model section
describes activities with no stated mechanism, and the outcomes section
promises change the activities were never argued to cause. The discipline
that prevents it is a causal spine — *if we do X, Y changes, because Z* —
threaded identically through need, design and evaluation, so the same logic
holds the whole document together
([theory-of-change-framing](techniques/theory-of-change-framing.md)).

## Genre decides the rendering

The skeleton is constant; almost everything else — length, section order,
labels, register, even what counts as "the outcome" — comes from the funder
family, because [the funder sets the form](../_laws.md#the-funder-sets-the-form).
Four genres cover most of the field:

- **Private-foundation** — the modest default. A compact narrative, roughly
  four to six hundred words, in a specific, credible first-person-plural
  voice: problem and population, the model, the evidence, the unlock. No
  jargon, no "leverage", no mission-speak.
- **Government/agency solicitations** — longer, roughly seven hundred to a
  thousand words, and organized **to the reviewer's scoring rubric**, not to
  the applicant's preferred story. The published notice states the review
  criteria; the narrative mirrors them as labeled parts — need, approach,
  organizational capacity, evaluation — using the funder's own terminology,
  addressing every sub-criterion explicitly, and allocating space in
  proportion to point values. A reviewer scoring against a rubric awards
  points to what they can find; a buried argument loses points it earned.
- **Advocacy/movement funders** — framed as a theory of change, not service
  delivery. These funders fund *power and change, not headcount*: name the
  systemic problem and who holds power over it, the strategy (organizing,
  coalition, policy levers), the constituency mobilized, and the concrete
  systemic change sought. "Population served / units delivered" framing
  actively signals genre-misread here.
- **Arts and culture panels** — artistic vision first. The work itself, what
  makes it singular, the artists and community it serves, why *this*
  institution and why now, in the institution's own distinctive voice. Arts
  panels reject boilerplate faster than any other reader; generic
  nonprofit-speak is disqualifying, not merely weak.

Genre must be *detected*, and detected conservatively: switch registers only
on distinctive signals, and default to the modest foundation register when
unsure, because a misfired exotic register does more damage than a slightly
generic safe one ([genre-register-classification](techniques/genre-register-classification.md)).
Applicant type shapes voice orthogonally to funder genre — a university, a
municipality and a small business each carry their own credible register, and
none of them is nonprofit mission-speak.

## Truth discipline: the narrative's non-negotiable floor

The cardinal risk of assisted or template-driven drafting is a
[fabricated figure](../_laws.md#never-fabricate-a-figure). A narrative under
pressure to be "specific and measurable" will grow invented statistics,
invented past results, invented named partners — each one a landmine the
applicant signs their name to. Three rules make honesty structural:

- **A verified fact set is the only figure source.** Numbers the
  organization has confirmed may be stated as fact; everything else is a
  bracketed placeholder the writer fills, never a plausible-sounding
  invention. A blank the applicant completes is worth more than a wrong
  number they adopt.
- **Push for measurability only where data exists to back it.** "State a
  measurable outcome" is good guidance *when grounded*; issued against an
  empty fact set it produces placeholder bloat that increases the writer's
  rework instead of reducing it. The nudge is conditional on grounding.
- **Voice exemplars transfer voice, never facts.** An organization's own
  past winning application is the best available style reference — reviewers
  already rewarded that voice — but it is a *style* source only: match its
  voice and structure, copy no fact, name or figure out of it into a new
  context where it may no longer be true.

## Failure modes of the naive reading

- **The org-first opening.** Three paragraphs of institutional history
  before any reader learns what problem the money addresses.
- **The undocumented problem.** Urgent adjectives, zero evidence; the need
  section reads as opinion.
- **The category model.** "We provide services to the community" — a model
  so generic any applicant could have written it, which reviewers read as
  no model at all.
- **The circular logic.** Outcomes restated from activities; evaluation
  measuring attendance and calling it change.
- **The genre misfit.** A rubric-shaped compliance narrative sent to an arts
  panel; a lyrical vision statement sent to a federal reviewer; headcount
  metrics sent to a movement funder.
- **The generic ask.** A close that requests "support" instead of naming
  what these dollars, at this size, unlock now.
- **The confident fabrication.** A specific, wrong number stated as fact —
  worse than any weakness above, because it survives into the signed
  submission.
