# Funder-dollars-unlock close

The last thing a reviewer reads before scoring is the ask — and most asks
waste it: "your support will help us continue this vital work". That
sentence gives the funder nothing to buy. The technique is to close on the
**marginal difference**: what *this* funder's dollars, at *this* size,
make possible *now* that would not otherwise happen.

## Why marginality is the whole point

A program officer is not deciding whether the applicant's work is good;
they are deciding what their specific money changes. Three questions sit
behind that decision, and the close must answer all three:

- **Additionality** — what happens with this grant that does not happen
  without it? "Continue our work" answers: nothing changes, the money is
  substitutional. The fundable answer names the increment: a second
  cohort, a new site, the evaluation capacity that turns a pilot into an
  evidenced model, a staff role that removes the bottleneck.
- **Sizing** — does the increment fit the amount? An unlock too large for
  the request reads as fantasy; too small reads as padding. The claimed
  increment must be plausibly purchasable at the requested figure, and
  consistent with the budget's own lines.
- **Timing** — why now? A window that makes the grant consequential at
  this moment: a matched opportunity, a demand spike, a proven pilot ready
  to scale, capacity that lapses if unfunded.

## Procedure

1. **State the increment as a concrete deliverable**, in the population's
   terms where possible: not "expand capacity" but "add [a specific
   number] of places for [the population] at [the site/format]". Use
   verified figures where they exist; a bracketed placeholder where they
   do not — the close is precisely where a generator or a hurried writer
   invents a satisfying number.
2. **Tie the increment to the request amount explicitly.** The strongest
   closes read as a purchase order: this amount funds these components,
   which produce this increment. It also pre-answers the budget review —
   the close and the budget must tell the same story.
3. **Align the increment with the funder's stated priorities**, in their
   vocabulary. The close is the final chance to make the reviewer's
   summary sentence easy: "this grant buys X, which is exactly what we
   fund".
4. **End on the outcome, not the gratitude.** The final sentence belongs
   to what changes for the population, stated with the measurable target
   where the fact set supports one.

## Decision rules

- When the close could be pasted under any other applicant's narrative,
  it is generic; rewrite until it names this program's specific increment.
- When no honest increment exists — the grant genuinely sustains existing
  operations — say so *as the case*: name what stops or shrinks without
  it. Honest continuation framing ("this funds the staff role that keeps
  the program's only site open") beats fake expansion framing.
- When the request range is unspecified, argue increments in structure
  ("each unit funds one cohort of…") rather than committing to totals
  that may misfit the eventual award.
- When multiple funders are approached for the same program, the unlock
  must be partitionable — never promise the same increment twice.

## When not to use it

- **Post-award reports** close on accountability — what the money *did*
  unlock, variances included — not on a new ask; smuggling the next
  solicitation into a report reads as tone-deaf.
- **Rubric-driven applications** may have no free-form close at all: the
  unlock logic then lives inside the sections the rubric provides
  (typically approach, capacity and budget justification), because the
  reviewer's attention goes where the points are, not to a peroration.
- **Advocacy genre** keeps the shape but changes the currency: the unlock
  is stated in strategy and change terms — what campaign capacity, what
  coalition step, what policy window this funding makes winnable — not in
  units of service delivered.
