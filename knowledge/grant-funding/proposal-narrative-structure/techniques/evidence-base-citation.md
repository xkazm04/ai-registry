# Evidence-base citation

Reviewers are trained to hunt claims without evidence. The evidence link of
the narrative answers "why should anyone believe this model works" — and the
craft is knowing **which kind of evidence is available, being honest about
which kind is being offered, and never manufacturing the kind that is
missing**.

## The evidence ladder

Three rungs, in descending strength, each legitimate when labeled as what
it is:

1. **The applicant's own measured results.** Prior cohort outcomes, tracked
   over time, with the data source named (intake logs, attendance records,
   assessment scores). Strongest, because it evidences both the model and
   this organization's capacity to run it.
2. **The published evidence base for the intervention class.** Where the
   organization is young or the program new, the model can stand on
   external research: studies, meta-analyses, recognized practice
   registries showing the intervention class works. This is honest and
   often sufficient — provided the narrative claims "this model class is
   evidenced", not "we have achieved these results".
3. **A credible evaluation plan.** Where neither exists, the honest move is
   prospective: named metrics, baselines, collection instruments and a
   measurement timeline. Reviewers score a real evaluation design far above
   a vague retrospective claim — a weak evaluation plan (no metrics, no
   baseline, no methodology) is among the most cited proposal weaknesses.

The cardinal sin is rung inflation: presenting rung-2 research as rung-1
results, or dressing rung-3 hopes as either. One discovered inflation
poisons every other claim in the document.

## Procedure

1. **Inventory the verified fact set first.** Before drafting, know which
   figures the organization has confirmed. Those — and only those — may be
   stated as fact, and every one carries its source. A figure whose source
   cannot be shown is a candidate, not a fact.
2. **Attach provenance in the prose.** "Ninety-two percent of last year's
   cohort completed the program (attendance records)" reads stronger, not
   weaker, than the bare number — provenance is persuasive because it is
   checkable.
3. **Fill gaps with placeholders, never plausible numbers.** Where a
   statistic would strengthen the case and none is verified, a bracketed
   placeholder hands the decision to the writer. This matters doubly in
   assisted drafting: a generator pushed to "be measurable" against an
   empty fact set will invent — so the measurability push itself must be
   conditional on grounded data existing.
4. **Pick the outcome that is actually an outcome.** Attendance and
   retention are outputs; the outcome is what changed for the population.
   When verified data supports it, state at least one measurable result —
   a named metric with its value or target — and refuse to let an output
   impersonate it.
5. **Cite partners only with proof.** A named partner is a claim reviewers
   verify (they expect the letters). Name partners from the verified
   inputs, never from pattern-plausibility.

## Decision rules

- When the draft states a number, ask "which input is this from?" — no
  answer, no number.
- When the honest evidence is thin, argue the evaluation plan hard rather
  than inflating the past; reviewers forgive a young program, not a
  dishonest one.
- When external research is cited, tie it to *this* model's mechanism, not
  to the field in general — evidence for "mentoring works" is not evidence
  for this design unless the design matches the evidenced practice.
- When quantitative pressure and grounding conflict, grounding wins:
  suppress the push for figures rather than satisfy it with inventions or
  a thicket of placeholders that just relocates the work.

## When not to use it

Do not pad genres that weight evidence differently. Arts panels read for
artistic vision and track record of the work, not effect sizes; a citation
apparatus there signals misread genre. Advocacy funders read for strategy
credibility and past wins in the change domain (turnout, coalitions,
hearings, policy shifts) rather than service-outcome studies. The truth
discipline is identical everywhere; the *kind* of evidence offered follows
the genre.
