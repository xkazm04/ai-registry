# Honest misses disclosure

The lessons-learned section is the part of a post-award report program
officers read most closely, because it is the hardest part to fake. Anyone
can list activities; only an organization that actually reflects can say what
fell short, why, and what it will change. Funders are not buying flawless
execution — they know programs hit friction — they are buying an
organization's capacity to notice and adapt. A report that discloses a miss
*demonstrates* that capacity; a report that is all wins merely *asserts* it,
and assertion in this section reads as its opposite.

## The asymmetry the technique rests on

A disclosed miss costs less than writers fear and pays more than they expect.
Program officers compare reports across a portfolio; against that baseline, a
grantee whose lessons section says "recruitment ran forty percent below
target in the first cohort; the referral channel we assumed did not
materialize; we shifted to partner-based intake and the second cohort
filled" is *more* fundable than one that says "the program exceeded
expectations." The first is checkable, specific, and shows a team that
steers. The second is spin, and spin discounts everything else in the
document — including the true parts. Meanwhile an *undisclosed* miss the
funder later discovers (from a site visit, a budget reconciliation, the next
report's baseline) converts a program problem into an integrity problem, and
integrity problems end relationships.

The disclosure rule is the disclosure-side twin of
[never fabricate a figure](../../_laws.md#never-fabricate-a-figure):
fabrication invents what didn't happen; a scrubbed lessons section deletes
what did. Both present the funder a fiction.

## Structure of a disclosed miss

Show learning, not deflection. Each miss carries three parts, and the third
is the one that does the work:

1. **The miss, with its number.** Named plainly, quantified against the
   target it missed. Not "some challenges with enrollment" — "enrolled 61 of
   a planned 100."
2. **The honest why.** The team's actual read on the cause, including
   assumptions that proved wrong. Blaming externals exclusively ("the
   pandemic", "the economy") is deflection wearing a why's clothes; the
   credible version includes what the org itself misjudged.
3. **The change.** What the organization now does differently. This is what
   converts a miss into evidence of capacity. A miss with no change attached
   is a confession; a miss with a change attached is a lesson.

## Enforcing it structurally

Because writers under deadline pressure drift toward spin, the discipline is
worth enforcing at review time, not just exhorting: a lessons section that
contains no challenge language anywhere — nothing missed, nothing that fell
short, nothing that surprised, nothing that would change — is flagged as
suspect. A vocabulary screen for miss-shaped language ("fell short",
"below target", "would change", "surprised", "setback") is a crude detector
with real false-negative risk, so calibrate its severity honestly: it is a
reviewer's-attention flag, not a submission blocker. A period can be
genuinely smooth; the gate's job is to make pure spin impossible to ship
*unexamined*, not to mandate a synthetic confession.

Symmetrically, drafting guidance — for humans or models — should ask for the
miss explicitly: "be honest about the misses, not just the wins." A
generation prompt that only requests achievements will get only
achievements, and the gate will then flag its own pipeline's output.

## When not to use it

Do not manufacture a miss to satisfy the pattern — an invented challenge is a
fabricated figure with a humble face. And scope the candor to the lessons
section's job: operational and programmatic learning. Disclosures with legal
or regulatory dimensions (a safeguarding incident, suspected fraud, a
compliance breach) follow the award's formal notification clauses and
counsel's guidance, on their own timeline — a narrative report's lessons
paragraph is not the channel for them.
