# The four-section report model

Whatever template a funder hands out, a narrative report answers four
questions, in the order a program officer reads them: **what did you do**
(activities), **who did it reach** (beneficiaries), **where did the money go**
(finances), and **what did you learn** (lessons). Fixing these as the report's
skeleton buys three things: writers always know what remains; a drafting
pipeline gets one guidance block per section instead of a vague "write a
report"; and completion becomes computable instead of vibes.

## Per-section craft

- **Activities** (roughly 300–500 words): what the organization actually did
  during the period — cohorts run, sessions held, partnerships formed,
  milestones hit *and missed*. Specifics beat platitudes; quantify wherever
  the data allows. This section reports the period, not the whole program's
  life — resist the recap.
- **Beneficiaries** (roughly 150–300 words): the count served, the
  demographic shape, and — the part most writers omit — what portion is
  directly attributable to *this* grant versus general operations. A funder
  reading five grantees' reports is trying to locate their own money's
  effect; conflating grant-funded reach with total reach reads as inflation
  even when innocent. Name the data source (intake logs, attendance
  records): a figure whose provenance is stated invites verification instead
  of suspicion — [provenance per field](../../_laws.md#provenance-per-field)
  applied to outcomes.
- **Finances** (roughly 200–400 words): spent versus budgeted by category,
  burn rate against plan, and an explanation of any variance beyond roughly
  ten percent of a line — offered before the funder asks. Variance is
  normal; unexplained variance reads as sloppiness or concealment. Prose
  with figures inline usually survives portals better than pasted tables;
  know the destination medium before formatting.
- **Lessons** (roughly 200–400 words): what worked, what surprised the team,
  what would change next time. Governed by its own discipline —
  honest-misses-disclosure — because it is the section funders read most
  closely.

The word bands are reading-attention bands, not compliance rules: below them a
section is almost certainly under-specified; far above them the program
officer skims. When the funder's template states its own lengths, the
funder's lengths win — [the funder sets the form](../../_laws.md#the-funder-sets-the-form)
holds after the award exactly as before it.

## The completion contract

A report is a completion-tracked artifact, and the tracking must be
placeholder-proof: a section counts as filled only above a minimal substance
floor (a few dozen characters defeats "TBD" and "TODO" without judging
quality). Two properties matter more than the exact threshold:

- **Every section is funder-facing.** Unlike a proposal workspace, a report
  has no scratch section — everything written will be read by the funder, so
  the completion denominator is all four sections, no exemptions.
- **One threshold, one owner.** Whatever substance floor the operation uses,
  it is defined once and imported everywhere it is checked. Two independently
  maintained thresholds eventually disagree, and then "100% complete" means
  different things on different screens.

## Mapping onto funder templates

When a funder's form has more or different headings, map — don't abandon.
Their "progress toward objectives" is activities plus beneficiaries; their
"budget narrative" is finances; their "challenges" is lessons. Write against
the four-section model internally, then distribute into the funder's headings
at filing time. The inverse — drafting free-form against each funder's
idiosyncratic template — forfeits the completion contract and the reusable
per-section guidance, and produces reports whose quality varies with the
template instead of with the work.

## When not to use it

Financial-only filings (a standardized expenditure form with no narrative)
and metrics-table reports don't need narrative sections at all — forcing
prose onto them adds noise. And for a funder whose template is genuinely
orthogonal (some public agencies score against their own logic-model
headings), draft directly against their structure; the four questions still
locate the substance, but the skeleton must be theirs.
