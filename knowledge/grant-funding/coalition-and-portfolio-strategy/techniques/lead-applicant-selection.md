# Lead applicant selection

The concern: every multi-organization application has exactly one recipient of
record, and choosing it is the highest-stakes structural decision in coalition
assembly. The lead is not a figurehead. In nearly every funding regime the lead
organization is **legally and fiscally accountable for the entire award** — for
the appropriate expenditure of funds by every member, for consolidated reporting,
for compliance and audit exposure across the whole coalition, and for executing a
written subaward agreement with each partner. When a member misspends, the funder
comes to the lead. The lead is who the funder underwrites; the coalition's
credibility is capped by the lead's administrative credibility.

## The default rule: capacity carries

The defensible default is to nominate the **highest-capacity member** — in
practice, the largest by annual revenue — as lead. The reasoning is mechanical,
not deferential: fiscal accountability at award scale demands accounting systems,
audit history, grants-management staffing, and cash-flow depth that correlate
with organizational size. Subawards are typically reimbursement-based, meaning
the lead floats money and absorbs timing risk; the member that can carry a
payroll through a slow reimbursement cycle without crisis is the member that
should be in that position. A small founding member made lead out of courtesy
becomes the coalition's single point of failure the first time a drawdown is
late.

Revenue is a proxy, and the technique is honest about that. When better signals
are available, they refine or override the default:

- **A completed independent audit** outranks raw size — it is direct evidence of
  the financial controls the role requires.
- **Prior awards from this funder or program** outrank both: a lead the funder
  already trusts converts directly into review-stage credibility.
- **Eligibility asymmetries override everything.** If the program restricts who
  may apply — by type, geography, or certification — the lead must be drawn from
  the members who pass that gate, whatever their size. A hard gate is not
  negotiable by capacity.

The decision rule: start from highest-capacity, adjust for audit history and
funder relationship, and let eligibility gates veto. Never select by seniority,
founding role, or who discovered the opportunity — those are recognition
questions, and the application can recognize them in the narrative without
putting the wrong organization on the accountability line.

## Decide before drafting, and write it down

The lead must be named **before drafting begins**, for three compounding reasons.
The application is written *from the lead's perspective* — its registrations, its
financials, its track record fill the applicant fields; changing lead mid-draft
is a rewrite, not an edit. The subaward agreements — the written instruments
defining each member's scope, budget, deliverables, and reporting duties — hang
off the lead's identity and take real calendar time to negotiate and sign. And
partners deserve to consent to the structure they are joining: an organization
willing to be a subrecipient may be unwilling to be a passenger of *this
particular* lead.

The selection is a proposal until every member has agreed to it in writing. A
memorandum of agreement naming the lead, each member's role, and the money terms
(see proportional-subgrant-split) is the artifact that converts a scored
suggestion into a partnership. Systems that select leads should present the
choice with its reasoning attached — "largest member, most able to carry
compliance and reporting" — because a nomination whose logic is visible gets
accepted or intelligently contested, while a bare name gets resented.

## What the lead role costs, and pricing it in

Leading is expensive: consolidated reporting, subrecipient monitoring, audit
scope covering everyone's spending, and administrative labor that partners do not
see. Two consequences for the assembly pipeline. First, the lead's share of the
award should reflect the burden — an administrative allocation on top of the
proportional split is standard practice, not greed, and proposing it explicitly
prevents the lead from silently subsidizing the coalition. Second, an
organization asked to lead may rationally decline; the pipeline needs a
second-choice path, not an assumption that the top-ranked member accepts.

## When not to use

Do not apply the capacity default when the funder prescribes the lead type —
many programs require a specific institution class (a government entity, an
accredited institution, a designated regional body) to hold the award; there the
lead is determined, and the technique's job collapses to verifying a qualifying
member exists. And do not use lead selection to resolve a fit problem: if no
member has the administrative capacity to lead at the award's scale, the honest
output is that this coalition cannot responsibly pursue this grant — naming the
least-undercapitalized member as lead converts a screening failure into a future
compliance incident.
