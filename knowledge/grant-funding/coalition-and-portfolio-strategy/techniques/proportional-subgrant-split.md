# Proportional subgrant split

The concern: a coalition award is one pot of money that must become several
member budgets, and *when* and *how* that division is decided determines whether
the coalition survives its own success. The failure mode is well-worn: partners
assemble around an opportunity, defer the money question as divisive, win, and
then negotiate shares at the moment every member has maximum incentive to fight
and the funder is watching. The technique's core rule: **propose the split at
assembly time, derive it from a stated and verifiable measure, and write it into
the partnership agreement before submission.**

## The default rule: shares proportional to capacity

The defensible default allocates each member a share proportional to its
capacity — in practice, its share of the coalition's combined annual revenue:

    share(member) = member revenue / combined revenue

Why this measure, of all candidates:

- **It is verifiable.** Revenue appears on filed financial statements and public
  disclosures; no member can inflate it in negotiation without contradicting
  their own filings. A split derived from verified figures inherits their
  authority — and a split derived from invented or asserted figures is exactly
  the fabricated number this domain exists to keep out of funding documents.
- **It approximates absorption.** A member's revenue bounds how much new money
  it can responsibly spend in a program year. Proportional-to-revenue shares
  keep every member's slice inside its own capacity, which a per-capita or
  equal split does not.
- **It is symmetric with the capacity story.** The coalition exists because
  combined revenue clears the award floor; splitting by contribution to that
  combination is the allocation the assembly logic already implies.

Present shares rounded to legible precision (whole percentage points), lead
listed first, every member's figure and the derivation visible. The visibility
is the point: an allocation whose formula is stated gets negotiated *around* —
"we're taking on the evaluation work, we need five points more" — while an
allocation that arrives as bare numbers gets fought *about*.

## A proposal and an anchor, not a verdict

The proportional split is the **opening anchor**, not the final word, and
systems producing it must say so. Real budgets are built from scopes of work:
the member running the most expensive program component may be the smallest
organization. The negotiation sequence that works: (1) anchor with the
proportional split, (2) adjust for scope — who delivers what, at what real cost,
(3) add the lead's administrative allocation on top of its programmatic share
(consolidated reporting and subrecipient monitoring are real labor; leaving them
unpriced makes the lead subsidize everyone), (4) record the final split, with
its deviations from the anchor and their reasons, in the signed partnership
agreement. A deviation with a reason is a negotiation; a deviation without one
is a precedent someone will regret.

## Decision rules

- When a member's proportional share falls below what its scope of work costs,
  fix the scope or the share before submission — a member funded below its
  delivery cost will underdeliver, and the lead answers for it.
- When one member's share exceeds roughly half the award, question the coalition
  shape: a dominant member with satellites may serve the funder's consortium
  preference in letter only, and some programs check subaward distribution.
- When the funder's rules constrain the split (caps on subaward size, minimum
  shares for certain member classes, limits on administrative rates), the
  funder's constraints bind first and the proportional rule allocates the
  remainder. The funder sets the form of the budget, not the coalition.
- Never leave the split implicit in a budget spreadsheet only. The controlling
  artifact is the written agreement each member signed; the budget must
  reconcile to it.

## When not to use

Skip the proportional default when the program dictates allocation structure —
some funders require equal partner shares, prescribe percentage bands per role,
or fund named budget lines per member; there the technique reduces to compliance
plus documentation. And do not apply revenue-proportionality to coalitions whose
members' revenues are incomparable in kind (a hospital system partnered with two
grassroots organizations): the formula would allocate nearly everything to the
largest member and read as tokenism to exactly the reviewer the coalition was
built to persuade. In those shapes, anchor on scope-of-work budgets from the
start — but still before submission, still written, still signed.
