# Portfolio balance across difficulty

The concern: an organization's writing capacity is finite and its matched
pipeline is not, so the application slate is a constructed portfolio whether
anyone constructs it or not. Left unconstructed, it defaults to deadline
proximity — the nearest due date wins the next writing slot — which optimizes
urgency and ignores odds, payoff, and concentration. The technique replaces that
default with deliberate construction across difficulty tiers under an explicit
capacity budget.

## The unit of difficulty is the program, not the funder

The first discipline is granularity. Within a single funder, individual
programs' award rates routinely spread by an order of magnitude — the same
grantmaker runs a 40%-award-rate capacity-building fund and a 4% flagship. A
funder-level difficulty label averages those into a number true of neither, and
every downstream decision inherits the error. Difficulty attaches to the
specific program and cycle being entered. Funder-level intelligence still
matters — relationships, requirement profiles, payment behavior are
funder-level — but *odds* are per-program.

Two honesty rules govern the difficulty inputs. **Rates over thin samples stay
out of the model**: an award rate computed from a handful of decided outcomes is
noise wearing a percentage sign, and the honest treatment is suppression — an
unrated program is pursued on qualitative judgment, not on a fake number.
And **segment the rate to the applicant's own bracket where the data allows**:
overall award rates hide the fact that many programs' odds differ several-fold
by applicant size, and the rate that predicts *your* outcome is your bracket's.

## The construction: tiers under a capacity budget

State the capacity budget first — how many serious applications the team can
produce per cycle at full quality. This is a hard constraint: an application
that steals hours from another application is not free, and the most common
portfolio failure is quietly exceeding capacity so that every submission is
80%-quality. Then allocate slots across three tiers:

- **Base** (approachable: strong fit, honest odds well above the field, award
  sizes the organization absorbs easily). This tier pays the bills and keeps
  the win-rate morale loop alive.
- **Core** (moderate: competitive programs at the organization's natural scale,
  where fit and track record give a real edge).
- **Reach** (difficult: transformative award sizes, single-digit award rates,
  or first-time funders). Entered deliberately and rarely, with the expectation
  of losing most of them.

A defensible starting shape weights the base heaviest and caps reaches at one
to two per cycle — a barbell, not a bell curve. The two degenerate portfolios
are both common: all-reach (a lottery ticket habit that produces a year of
zeros) and all-base (a treadmill of small awards that caps the organization at
incremental funding forever). The middle-clustered slate is subtler but also
weak: every application is competitive, none is either reliable or
transformative.

Balance also runs on a second axis: **concentration**. A slate of six
applications to one funder family, or six decisions all landing in the same
quarter, is one adverse event away from a zero cycle. Spread across funder
families and decision timing is part of construction, not a bonus.

## Expected value, honestly computed

Ranking within a tier uses expected value — award size × win probability —
divided by effort, but each term deserves suspicion. Win probability starts
from the segmented program rate and adjusts for fit; award size uses the
realistic figure for the applicant's bracket, not the published maximum; effort
includes relationship work and post-award reporting burden, not just writing
days. And the ordering constraint from eligibility work carries through: only
grants that passed every hard gate enter the portfolio at all. EV computed over
an application the organization may not submit is a category error.

## Coalitions and signals in the balance

Coalition opportunities enter the portfolio as their own positions with
distinctive economics: coordination cost raises the effort term severalfold,
but funder-side signals can raise the odds term more. Where a program is
known — from published data or accumulated intelligence — to favor consortia
heavily over solo applicants, a solo application to it belongs in a *worse*
tier than its headline rate suggests, and a coalition application in a better
one. This is the portfolio-level payoff of funder intelligence: signals change
tier assignments, not just footnotes.

## Decision rules

- When the pipeline exceeds capacity (it always does), cut from the middle
  first: the base tier is protected revenue, the reach slots are protected
  strategy, and mid-tier applications are the fungible ones.
- When two candidates tie on EV, prefer the one that diversifies — new funder
  family, different decision quarter, or a program that builds a relationship
  the current portfolio lacks.
- When last cycle's results skew — all reaches lost *and* all base won — read
  it as calibration data on the difficulty model before reading it as strategy
  failure; a base tier that never loses may be under-reaching.
- Re-balance when the pipeline changes materially or a cycle turns, not per
  new opportunity; a portfolio rebuilt weekly is deadline-chasing with extra
  steps.

## When not to use

Portfolio construction presumes a pipeline larger than capacity. An
organization with three viable matches a year has a sourcing problem, not a
selection problem — applying tiering there dresses scarcity as strategy. It
also presumes the difficulty inputs exist; in a domain with no published award
data, build the qualitative tiering first and let measured rates earn their way
in later, rather than inventing percentages to feed the formula.
