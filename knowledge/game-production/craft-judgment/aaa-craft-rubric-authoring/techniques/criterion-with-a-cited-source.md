# A criterion is a bar with a named source

Each criterion in a craft rubric is one sentence stating what must be true of the
artifact, followed by the named published work that establishes why that is the bar: a
conference talk, a textbook, a broadcast or delivery specification, a discipline
standard, a documented studio methodology. The citation is load-bearing. It converts
the criterion from something an examiner may weigh against their taste into something
a practitioner can contest on the merits — and contesting it means arguing with the
source, which is a productive argument, or replacing the source, which is a visible
edit.

## The shape of an entry

A well-formed criterion has four parts and no more:

- **The bar, positively stated.** What must be present, not what must be absent.
  "Every transition between locomotion states has an authored blend with a contact
  frame on the ground" — not "no popping".
- **The source, named.** Title and author, or the standard's designation. Not "industry
  best practice", not "widely accepted". If you cannot name it, you have a preference
  and should say so explicitly rather than dress it as a standard.
- **What in the stored artifact answers it.** The specific place an examiner looks.
  This is what makes the criterion checkable rather than aspirational.
- **The failure form.** What the artifact looks like when the bar is not met, in one
  clause, so the examiner recognises it rather than reasoning toward it.

Nine to twelve criteria is a working rubric for a medium. Below seven the instrument
is too coarse to separate a competent piece from a strong one; above about fourteen
examiners start averaging rather than answering, and the marginal criteria stop
discriminating.

## Where the sources come from

- **Discipline conference talks and postmortems** for craft rules that live in
  practice rather than in specification — silhouette reading, telegraph timing,
  encounter pacing, the anatomy of a hit.
- **Textbooks and long-form references** for the fundamentals a criterion is a
  specialisation of. These age slowly and are the best anchors for criteria you expect
  to keep for years.
- **Published technical specifications** wherever the medium has one: loudness and
  true-peak standards for audio, colour and transfer standards for imaging, subtitle
  and caption specifications for text on screen, accessibility conformance levels.
  These are the strongest sources because the bar is a number and the source defines
  its unit.
- **Documented studio methodologies** for process criteria — stage gates, review
  cadences, definitions of done. Name the methodology; process criteria without a
  named method drift into whatever the current lead prefers.

Prefer, in order: a specification with a number, a textbook, a named talk, a
documented methodology. A criterion sourced to a specification is arguable only about
whether it applies; a criterion sourced to a talk is arguable about whether the talk
is right. Both are better than unarguable.

## Decision rules

- **One source per criterion.** Two sources means the criterion is really two, or that
  it is being propped up. Split it or pick one.
- **The bar in the criterion and the bar in the source must be the same bar.** If the
  source says one thing and the criterion says a stricter thing, the criterion's real
  source is you; say that. A criterion that quietly tightens its source is how a rubric
  acquires unattributed house style and then defends it as external authority.
- **Where the source gives a number, the criterion carries the number and its unit.** A
  criterion that says "loudness is within broadcast tolerance" is not checkable; one
  that names the target figure, the unit, and the measurement basis is.
- **Adapt cross-discipline sources explicitly.** Importing a standard from film,
  broadcast, or print into an interactive medium is legitimate and common — but the
  criterion must state what changed in the crossing, because the interactive case
  usually has a constraint the source never faced.
- **The criterion and the instruction given to the producer share one source text.** If
  the bar an examiner scores and the bar a generator is asked to hit are maintained
  separately, they drift silently and every resulting grade misattributes a
  specification failure to craft. Author once, project into both surfaces.
- **A criterion nobody has ever failed is not discriminating.** Review it: either the
  bar is below the floor of anything you produce, in which case delete it, or it is a
  disqualifier in the wrong list, in which case move it.

## When not to use it

- **For the disqualifier list.** Disqualifiers are not weighted criteria and do not
  need the same treatment; they need to be recognisable at a glance. A source is
  welcome but the entry stays short.
- **For genuinely proprietary house style.** A studio's own signature — a specific
  palette discipline, a specific readability rule for its own camera distance — has no
  external source and should not be given a borrowed one. Label these as house
  criteria in their own block, so a reader can see which part of the bar is the market's
  and which part is yours.
- **When the citation would exceed the criterion in length.** The entry is a bar with a
  provenance, not an essay. If the source needs explaining, the explanation belongs in
  a companion note the rubric points to, not in the scoring surface an examiner reads
  under load.

## The reason this holds a rubric together

Criteria without sources decay in a specific way. Each review cycle someone objects to
one, the objection is taste against taste, the loudest party wins, and the bar moves a
little. Six months later nobody can reconstruct why the rubric says what it says, so
nobody defends it, and it is replaced by a fresh unsourced rubric that will decay the
same way. A sourced criterion survives that cycle because the argument has an
adjudicator that is not in the room. It also survives the examiner: when the bar and
the reason for the bar reach the examiner together, the criterion is applied as
written rather than reconstructed from the examiner's own priors.
