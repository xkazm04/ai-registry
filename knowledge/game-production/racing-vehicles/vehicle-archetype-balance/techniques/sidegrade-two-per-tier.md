# Sidegrade, two per tier

The concern: a progression that replaces each vehicle with a strictly better one asks the
player nothing. The technique puts at least two vehicles of different archetypes in every
tier. Progression raises the *budget*; the choice inside a tier is a *trade*. A player who
moves up chooses which trade to carry forward, and a player who stays is not stuck with a
worse game.

Status of claims: the roster layout and tier budgets are **authored**. Whether the choices feel
like choices is **unmeasured** with people; the simulation shows only that neither class wins
the tier outright.

## Procedure

1. Name the tiers and give each a budget, rising by a curve the economy owns. Record the budgets
   in data beside the rating weights.
2. Fill each tier with at least two vehicles built from different archetypes — one pairing
   a light, technical class with a heavy, straight-line one is the canonical sidegrade — plus
   any specials. Two is a floor chosen for a count-limited roster; larger tiers need more
   archetypes, not more copies.
3. Give each archetype a stated skill expression: where it wins, what it pays, what it asks of
   the driver. Light classes win on corner exit and clean air and die to contact; heavy ones
   win lane choice and shoving and lose on tight tracks. The expression is a design sentence
   the harness later tests.
4. Hold the within-tier rating at the budget (the rating technique) and the identity pair on
   every vehicle (the pair technique).
5. Verify the sidegrade in simulation: in each tier, neither class wins the mixture past the
   gate, and each is best on a course type the other loses.
6. Let the shop and unlock path offer both: a ladder where only one of the pair is reachable
   at the tier is a straight ladder in costume.

## Why two, and what two costs

Two vehicles per tier give the player a binary trade they can hold in mind, and give the
roster-level check just enough shape to test. They also make the statistics harder: a two-peer
tier cannot support a sample deviation or a peer mean excluding the subject (a population of
one), which is why the pair check uses an authored band and why the peer-population note
reports "not enough peers" instead of passing. Ten vehicles over five tiers is already
twice the authoring and tuning load of a straight ladder; budget for it.

A tier budget that rises with tier is not a speed multiplier. The higher budget is a broader
capability allowance, spent as bigger strengths and bigger weaknesses, not as a higher value on
every stat; otherwise the tier above is a straight upgrade after all.

## Decision rules

- When a tier has a clearly better vehicle in play, the ladder has collapsed. Check winner
  share and per-course bests before touching a stat.
- When two archetypes in a tier overlap on every course type, they are one vehicle. Move a
  stat to widen the trade, then verify the pair gate.
- When a tier boundary is crossed by a rating overlap (a high-tier vehicle's rating inside the
  tier below's tolerance), the budgets are too close; widen the curve.
- When specials exist, keep them in a tier's budget unless they are priced separately; an
  unbudgeted special is a hole in the single authority.
- When a new vehicle is added, add it to a tier with a partner, not alone; a vehicle alone in
  a tier is below the peer-population floor and cannot be judged.

## Common failures

A sidegrade pair whose weakness is on a stat the course mix never taxes. A budget curve so
steep that tier N's weakest vehicle outruns tier N-1's best, making every upgrade mandatory. A
special tier that dodges the rating and quietly becomes the optimal pick. A career path that
unlocks vehicles in a single order, so the choice never occurs.

## When not to use it

Rosters without a progression: a flat pick-your-vehicle lobby needs the pair and the winner
share but not the tier structure. Rosters where vehicles are customised from parts rather than
chosen: the sidegrade lives in the parts, and the technique moves one layer down.
