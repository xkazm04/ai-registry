# Score each element of the concept, from the concept's camera

The named concern: a scene is judged against a concept image, and the judgment has to be a
list of named gaps a team can act on rather than a mood. The scorecard is that list. It is
written once, scored every round from one camera, and read element by element.

## Decompose once, before scoring

Take the concept apart into the ten to twenty elements a viewer reads in it. Fewer than ten
and each element is a region of the frame rather than a thing, so its score cannot point at
a lever; more than twenty and the list starts scoring brushwork. For each element write one
row: what the image shows, what it is in the product, and how it will be built. The build
column matters later, because it is the first draft of which stage will cap the element.

Freeze the list before the first score. An element that is hard tends to be redefined into
something easier, or dropped as out of scope, during exactly the round that exposes it. A
revision to the list is allowed, but it is an explicit, dated change, and the scores before
and after it are not compared as one series.

## The scale is ordinal and short

Score each element on four levels from one capture at the concept's camera: 0, absent;
1, present but reads as a placeholder; 2, reads as the concept's element; 3, matches it. A
finer scale invites precision the eye does not have, and two judges who disagree by one
step on a ten-step scale have not disagreed about anything. Keep the levels described in
words in the scorecard itself, so a later reader knows what a 2 claimed.

An element that is not built yet scores 0 and stays in the list. Leaving it out because it
is planned later turns a missing layer into a smaller denominator, and the total then
describes a different, easier concept.

## The result is the list, by name

Report every element with its score, the capture it was read from, who read it, and one
sentence of what still differs. Then name every 0 and every 1 again, in a sentence of their
own, below the table. The total is permitted as a summary and is never the headline: a
respectable total can sit on four absent elements, and those are often the layer the concept
was painted to sell.

Each score carries its basis: the grader, the capture, the camera pose and the build it was
taken from. A builder's reading of their own round is labelled as a builder's reading. It
predicts the judge and does not replace them.

## Estimate a ceiling per element, and know what the estimate is for

Before building, write a ceiling per element: the score it can plausibly reach with the
team, tools and device in hand, and the cap that stops it there. After each round, put the
measured score beside the estimate.

A ceiling estimate is good for one thing: **ranking levers**. It says which elements have
the most headroom and which stage stands in front of each, and that ordering is usually
right. It is bad at another: **predicting the total**. The reason is structural rather than
a matter of care. An element's score is the minimum of what every stage delivers to it, and
an estimator who knows one stage well estimates that stage's ceiling and quietly assumes the
others deliver a match. Five such estimates, each right about its own stage, sum to a scene
nobody will build. In one run, several stage-specialist estimates put the reachable total in
the high thirties of 45, and one build day measured the high twenties; the one estimate that
conditioned its total on the weakest stage actually delivering, and said that stage had not
yet shown it could, was the one that held.

So the honest total is conditioned: estimate each element as the lowest of its stages'
demonstrated output, not their promised output, and state which stage that is.

## Procedure

1. Write the element list from the concept, once, with the build column.
2. Fix the capture: pose, field of view, resolution, post-processing, device. Record them
   with the scorecard.
3. Before building, estimate a ceiling per element and name its cap.
4. Capture, score every element 0 to 3, and write what still differs.
5. Name every 0 and 1 below the table.
6. Put the estimate beside the measured score and note which stage was wrong.
7. Hand the list to cap attribution before the next round is planned.

## Decision rules

- **When an element is not built, it scores 0 and stays in the list.** Planned later is a
  reason, not a score.
- **When two estimates of the reachable total disagree, believe the one conditioned on the
  weakest stage's demonstrated output.** The optimistic one has assumed another team's work.
- **When the builder's reading and the judge's differ, the judge's is the score** and the
  difference is recorded as a fact about the builders.
- **When the list changes, the series restarts.** Comparing totals across a list revision
  compares two different concepts.

## When not to use this

- When the reference can be measured by an instrument, the comparison is a parity gate and
  should be numeric and registered rather than read by eye.
- When the concept is a mood board rather than one framed image, there is no camera to
  score from; decompose into style rules instead.
- For a single asset in isolation; there is no composition for the stages to divide.
