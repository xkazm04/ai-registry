# A blind panel with the starting frame as a control

The named concern: the score a scene receives is a reading taken by an instrument, and the judge
is part of that instrument. Change who judges, what they were told, or which frames sit beside
the one being judged, and the number moves without the scene moving. Over a long series of look
rounds those shifts compound. Each panel is briefed on the goal, sees the history, and knows the
team expects progress. The trail climbs toward the bar, partly on the scene and partly on the
judging. This technique separates the two.

## What leaks into a judge

Anything that tells the judge what the answer should be:

- the goal ("we are trying to reach 40");
- the history ("last round scored 36");
- the identity of a frame ("this is today's build");
- the builder's own reading;
- the order of the frames, when the newest is always last.

Every one of these is information about the expected score, not about the scene. A judge that
knows the goal and the history is scoring the project's story as much as the frame.

## The procedure

1. **Assemble a panel of at least three judges.** Give them no project context: the concept image,
   the element list with its scale in words, and the frames. Nothing else.
2. **Anonymise and shuffle the frames.** Neutral labels, a different order per judge, and no file
   names that carry dates or build numbers.
3. **Include the starting frame as a control.** It is the capture from before the series began,
   unchanged and unlabelled, scored element by element exactly like the others.
4. **Score every element of every frame, per judge.** Keep the per-judge marks, not only the means.
5. **Read the control first.** Compare its score from this panel with the score the series' first
   panel gave it. The difference is this panel's offset against the series. The scene did not
   change, so all of that difference is the instrument.
6. **Rebase the trail on the control.** The current frame's blind score is the honest level. The
   earlier panels' scores are good for direction of travel only.

## What one blind panel showed

One project ran ten scored look rounds over two days with briefed panels. Their means climbed to
38 of 45 against a go bar of 40. A blind panel of three judges then scored three anonymised
frames: the current build on two products, and the starting frame as a control. The control
scored 10, where the series' first strict panel had given it 14. The two current frames scored
34 and 31, where the last briefed panel had given 38. The blind panel ran about 4 points lower on
the old frame and the new ones alike, so briefing had lifted the briefed panels by about 4
points. The progress was real: the same panel, on the same day, put the control at 10 and the
current build at 34. But the bar was not met, where the briefed trail said it nearly was.

The same series measured two more instrument effects:
- **Builders read high.** The builders' readings of their own rounds ran 3 to 7 points above the
  judges' (one builder read a round at 41 that its panel scored 36).
- **Independent judges spread about 2 points.** Three independent judges on one frame commonly
  spread across about 2 points of 45 each side of their mean.

## Decision rules

- **When a total decides go or no-go, it comes from a blind panel with a control.** A briefed
  panel's total is a progress signal, not a verdict.
- **When two panels' totals are compared, both must have scored a common control.** Without one,
  the comparison mixes the scene with the instrument.
- **When a round moves the total by less than the panel's spread, it has not moved the scene.**
  Two such rounds in a row are a plateau. Read the per-element marks for what changed, and do not
  celebrate or mourn the total.
- **When the control scores higher on a new panel than on the first**, the new panel is lenient.
  Lower the trail by the difference. The rule cuts both ways.
- **When builders' readings sit above the judge's**, label them as self-reports and record the gap
  as a calibration fact about the builders. Do not average it in.
- **When a judge is a model from the same family as the one that built or described the scene**,
  it is not independent of it. Use judges from different families, or a person.

## Cost

A blind panel costs one extra frame per judge (the control) and a briefing that says less. It is
cheap enough to run at every decision point, and too cheap to skip at the one that decides the
project.

## When not to use this

- **Within one round's tuning.** A builder comparing two versions of a light needs a fast reading,
  not a panel.
- **When there is no starting frame to keep.** In that case, keep the first frame the panel ever
  scores as the control from then on.
- **When the judge is the person the scene is for and their verdict is the requirement.** Their
  reading is the bar itself. Even then, show them frames blind when the question is "which is
  closer".
