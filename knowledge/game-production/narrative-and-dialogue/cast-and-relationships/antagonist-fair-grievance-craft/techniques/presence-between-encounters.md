# Presence between encounters

The named concern: make the antagonist's control felt in the hours between the scenes in
which he appears, so that by the time the player faces him they already know him through
what he owns, says and signs. In a game the antagonist is on screen for minutes out of many
hours; what he is in the remaining hours decides whether the climax meets a stranger or a
man the player has been living under.

## Proxies, not appearances

Presence is built from proxies, each cheap to produce and each met many times. His **voice**:
a broadcast, a recorded announcement, a message read aloud by someone else. His **signature**:
on receipts, contracts, permits, the posted ration. His **rules**: written up where the
player works, enforced by people who are not him. His **prices**. His **lieutenants**, who
quote him and defer to him in his absence. And the **reactions** of others: the conversation
that stops when his name comes up, the character who refuses to say it.

The signature on a receipt is the purest form. Every transaction the player makes in his
territory carries his name, so his control is part of the routine rather than an event; the
player does not decide to think about him, they simply cannot buy anything without him.

## Appearances are scarce and consequential

The opposite failure is overexposure. An antagonist who appears in person every few minutes
becomes familiar, and familiarity spends menace faster than any line of dialogue can rebuild
it. The rule is that personal appearances are rare and each one changes something the
player will have to live with: a term revised, an ally taken off the board, a door closed, a
price raised. An appearance that changes nothing teaches the player that his appearances
can be ignored.

Each appearance should also plant. One detail per scene that reads as generosity the first
time and as foreclosure in hindsight — he compliments the machine and not its owner, he
knows a serial number by heart, a space in his building is already marked with the player's
name — makes every earlier appearance a setup for the reckoning, so the climax feels
inevitable without having been announced.

Between appearances, proxies should evolve. A broadcast whose content shifts as the story
moves, a receipt whose terms quietly worsen, a posted rule that acquires an amendment — each
tells the player that he is acting even when unseen. The strongest evolution tracks *his*
state, not only the player's: when he loses something, his proxy voice changes — shorter,
colder, less amused — and the player learns that the man behind the channel can be hurt.
That gives an antagonist who barely appears an arc the player hears rather than sees.

## One appearance in the player's hands

In a game, the most persuasive form of presence is a single non-hostile appearance inside
play before the final encounter: he drives the lap of honour, leads the field as the pace
vehicle, stands on the platform the player must pass under. The player feels his mass, his
line, his speed, his indifference, with their own hands, so the final encounter is a
reckoning with something known rather than a first meeting. The risk is specific: players
will test whether he can be touched, and an invulnerable presence inside play reads as a
cheat unless the fiction states why he cannot be touched yet. A cheaper substitute is his
machine or his figure present in the scene's background.

## Proxies in a reactive game

Proxies are well suited to games because they can be state-driven. A broadcast line that
names the player's last result, a receipt that records the player's debt, a lieutenant who
comments on what the player just did: each makes the antagonist seem to be watching without
requiring him to appear. The craft constraint is that the watching must be selective. An
antagonist who comments on everything is a narrator; one who comments on the one thing that
mattered is attentive, and attention is a form of menace.

## Decision rules

- **When the antagonist's first personal appearance is also the player's first contact with
  him, add proxies before it** — at minimum his name on something the player uses.
- **When he appears in person more often than the story's acts change, cut appearances** and
  move their content into proxies.
- **When an appearance changes nothing the player must live with, give it a consequence or
  cut it.**
- **When a proxy comments on the player's state, choose the one fact that matters** and let
  the rest pass unremarked.
- **When a proxy line is reused across many visits, rotate or evolve it**, because a proxy
  that the player can recite has stopped being a presence and become furniture.
- **When he suffers a loss, change his proxy register** in the next broadcast or receipt.
- **When the final encounter would be the player's first time sharing play space with him,
  add one non-hostile appearance inside play**, with a stated reason he cannot be engaged.
- **When a proxy channel is borrowed from a loud, gleeful broadcast villain, keep the
  channel and drop the register.** The reasonable antagonist uses the same airwaves to state
  terms, not to entertain.

## When not to use this

An antagonist whose power depends on being unknown — a concealed manipulator whose identity
is the mystery — cannot sign receipts. Use presence through effects instead, and accept that
the signature move is unavailable. And a short game with few hours between encounters does
not need a proxy schedule; the encounters themselves carry him.

## Evidence status

Keeping a villain present through a running channel, and giving that channel an arc when the
villain suffers a loss, rests on a game lead writer's interviews read through journalist
summaries (medium-high in the research behind this subject); that precedent's villain is
loud and enjoys his villainy, so only the channel transfers to this antagonist, not the
register. Planting a detail in every appearance rests on a series creator's primary
statements about a planted betrayal (high), applied here by analogy. The non-hostile
in-play appearance, the scarcity rule for personal appearances and the signature-on-receipts
proxy are design inference and practitioner judgement. None of it has been tested in a
played game.
