# Rationed necessity in public

The named concern: give the antagonist's control an object — a necessity the community
cannot do without and cannot produce for itself — and stage his rationing of it where
everyone can see. Control in the abstract is a sentence in a briefing; control over water,
fuel, medicine or parts, handed out in a queue, is a scene the world performs every day.

## Choosing the necessity

A good necessity has four properties. It is **genuinely scarce** in the world, so that
rationing is defensible and some of his allocation is right. It is **not producible** by the
people who need it, so that the dependence is structural rather than lazy. It is **consumed
repeatedly**, so that the dependence renews and the leash is felt again and again rather than
once. And in a game it is **a resource the player spends**, so that the antagonist's power
lives in the economy the player actually handles — the price the player pays, the ration the
player receives, the credit the player is extended.

The last property is the one that converts narrative into play. When the antagonist's
control and the player's resource loop are the same object, every purchase is a small
encounter with him, and his authority accumulates without a line of dialogue.

## Why public

Rationing in private is a ledger; rationing in public is a ritual, and rituals are what hold
a community in place. Three things follow from staging it openly.

The dependence becomes **visible without exposition**: the queue, the counter, the posted
allotment and the people turned away explain the political economy faster than any speech.

The community becomes **complicit**. Everyone in the queue has accepted his terms in front of
everyone else, and no one wants to be the first to refuse. That shared acceptance is how one
man holds a town, and it is the thing the protagonist eventually has to break — not his
strength but the community's agreement to stand in line.

His **partial rightness becomes legible**. Scarcity is real, someone must allocate, and his
allocation keeps people alive. The player should be able to see that the system works, in
its way, and that it works by making obedience the price of survival.

Stage the distribution so the architecture says it. The necessity is released from a place
people must look up to — a platform, a gantry, a window above the counter — in quantities he
chooses, at times he chooses, and the release is a performance. He may even frame the
community's need as their weakness, a dependency they should be ashamed of, which completes
the inversion: the man who controls the supply presents himself as the one keeping them from
indulgence.

## Credit is the leash

The sharpest instrument a rationer has is credit against the necessity. He extends it
readily, on terms that are fair on paper — a stated rate, a stated term, his signature — and
the borrower's future is now his. Debt converts a single transaction into a continuing
relationship of control, and it does so with the borrower's consent, which is what makes it
reasonable and what makes it frightening. The antagonist never has to threaten a debtor; he
only has to be owed.

The distinction that makes credit a leash rather than a loan is **who controls the terms
after signing**. A debt with a fixed figure that falls predictably is a loan, and in a game
it is worse than a loan: the player does the arithmetic, sees the date they will be free,
and the threat evaporates at the moment they finish the sum. A leash is a debt whose
creditor also controls the unit it is counted in, the shop where the borrower spends, the
price of the repairs they cannot avoid, and the right to revise the rate. Each revision can
be individually reasonable and announced in the open; the aggregate is that the date of
freedom keeps moving. The historical shape is the company town paid in scrip spendable only
at the company's store, where many workers never cleared the store debt.

Write the terms down and show them, and show the revisions. A changed rate the player notices
on a ledger — small, explained, defensible — is more menacing than any hidden clause, because
it proves the figure was never the player's to finish.

## Decision rules

- **When the antagonist's power has no object the player touches, give it one** from the
  player's own resource loop.
- **When the necessity is hoarded out of sight, move the distribution into a public place**
  the player passes through.
- **When his rationing is plainly irrational, make it partly defensible.** A rationer who is
  only greedy is a thief; one who is also keeping people alive is an antagonist.
- **When credit is extended, make the terms visible and individually fair**, and make the
  aggregate inescapable.
- **When the player can compute the date their debt ends, give the creditor a lever over the
  terms** — the unit, the store, the repair price or the rate — and use it once, visibly.
- **When the economy design and the antagonist design disagree about the resource's
  scarcity, resolve it before writing scenes**, because the player will check the story's
  claim against what the economy actually does. The story's scarcity and the economy's
  tuning are two statements of one quantity, and a contradiction between them is a defect
  ([one-authority-per-quantity](../../../../_laws.md#one-authority-per-quantity)); the economy
  owns the number and the story reads it.
- **When the world has more than two or three necessities in play, cut to the ones that
  carry the politics.** A world legible in a few frames is one where everyone needs two or
  three goods and each has a known holder.

## When not to use this

A story whose antagonist operates by violence rather than allocation does not need a rationed
necessity, and inventing one turns a warlord into a shopkeeper. And a game whose economy is
generous by design should not claim scarcity in its story: a ration the player never feels
is a contradiction the player will notice before the writers do. The tuning of the debt
itself — its rates, its schedule of losses, how it is staged as a system — belongs to the
discipline that owns debt and loss staging; this technique owns only the antagonist's hand
on it.

## Evidence status

Rationing a necessity from an elevated place as visible control rests on a film's on-screen
staging (high) and a secondary reading of its architecture (medium). Economy-as-map rests on
the same work's on-screen world (high). The leash-versus-loan distinction rests on historical
sources about company scrip and company stores (high on the history); economic historians
dispute how universal the gouging was, so the claim used here is the institution's shape,
not its typical severity (medium on generality). The four properties of a good necessity,
the complicity of the queue and the player-resource binding are practitioner judgement. None
of it has been tested in a played game.
