# Fair grievance, wrong means

The named concern: split the antagonist's motive into two separately authored halves — a
grievance that is true and fair, and a set of means that are wrong — and keep each half
stable from his first scene to his last. The grievance is what makes him reasonable. The
means are what make him the antagonist. The story's moral argument lives in the gap between
them, and the protagonist's victory has to happen in that gap.

## Write the grievance as a stranger would tell it

A fair grievance has three properties, and each one alone is not enough.

It is **specific**: a named wrong done by named parties at a known time, not a general
complaint about the world. "The powerful exploit the weak" is a theme; "the consortium
closed the well my father dug and sold the water back to us" is a grievance.

It is **checkable inside the fiction**: some other character, some document, some ruin in
the world confirms that it happened. A grievance only the antagonist asserts is a grievance
the audience is invited to doubt, and doubt is the first step toward the secret-liar twist.

It is **fair to a neutral listener**: told by a stranger, with the antagonist's name left
out, it is a wrong the player would want righted. This is the test, and it is cheap to run —
hand the grievance paragraph to a reader with no context and ask whether the wrong is real.
If they hesitate, the grievance is not yet fair, and every scene built on it will be weaker
for it.

## Write the means as the line he crossed

The means are what he does about the grievance, and they are wrong in a specific, nameable
way: they put the cost of an old injustice on people who did not cause it. The most useful
formulation for a controlling antagonist is that he has answered powerlessness with
control — he was once at the mercy of a supplier, a creditor, a landlord, and he has become
one, on the theory that the only safe position is the one holding the leash.

Write down who pays for his means. If the answer is only the people who wronged him, the
means are revenge and the story will struggle to call them wrong. If the answer includes
people who were as powerless as he once was, the means are a repetition of the original
wrong, and that repetition is the argument. The most useful antagonists of this type are
running, with better manners, the exact system that broke them.

## The protagonist's side is complicit

The symmetry that makes the argument two-sided is not a mood of vague greyness; it is a
structure. The antagonist is right about the problem and wrong about the solution, and the
protagonist — or the figure the protagonist inherits from — is right about the method and
complicit in the problem: the peace the hero's side kept was a private accommodation with
the very power that produced the grievance. That complicity is what stops the hero being
a referee. The naive alternative to a strawman is its opposite, a both-sides balance so
even that nothing is at stake and no choice matters; complicity avoids both, because the
hero's side has something to answer for and still holds the better method.

## How the protagonist is allowed to win

The protagonist does not win by disproving the grievance. That move — the revelation that
the wrong never happened, or that he caused it himself — empties the story: everything the
player felt about the grievance is retroactively declared a mistake, and the antagonist
becomes a liar rather than an argument.

The protagonist wins by **accepting the premise and refusing the conclusion**: yes, the wrong
was real; no, it does not license this. In play that refusal must be enacted, not stated.
The protagonist's path to victory should demonstrate an alternative answer to the same
grievance — a means the antagonist said was impossible — or at minimum should show the
protagonist declining the antagonist's methods when they were available and would have
worked.

Because the grievance is true and his system partly works, the victory owes the audience
one more thing: an answer, however brief, to what runs the world after him. An ending that
removes the controller and leaves the scarcity, the roads, the supply he was keeping moving
unaccounted for has quietly conceded his point that only he could hold it together.

## How the grievance reaches the audience

The grievance is never delivered as a confession before the fight. A sad history narrated
to buy pity at the last moment reads as manipulation, because pity that arrives without a
cost is the writer asking. The strongest delivery turns it into leverage: he tells the old
wound while doing to someone else what was done to him, so the backstory and the menace
arrive in the same breath and the audience learns the history as a weapon. Failing that,
the grievance is carried by the world — geography, prices, who lives where — and
confirmed by other people, never only asserted by him.

## Decision rules

- **When a draft introduces a reveal that the grievance was false, cut the reveal.** The
  twist is buying surprise with the whole story's argument.
- **When a neutral reader calls the means proportionate, sharpen who pays.** Put a person
  the player likes on the bill, and make that person as powerless as he once was.
- **When the antagonist explains the grievance himself, have someone else confirm it.** His
  telling is advocacy; another character's telling is evidence.
- **When the protagonist's victory depends on the antagonist being wrong about facts, rework
  the victory.** He may be wrong about people, about what they will do when freed, about his
  own necessity — never about the original wrong.
- **When the grievance and the means can be swapped onto any villain, the grievance is a
  theme.** Make it specific enough that it belongs to one man.
- **When the hero's side has nothing to answer for, give it a complicity** — an old
  accommodation with the power behind the grievance — rather than greying the villain.
- **When the ending removes him and says nothing about what replaces his system, add the
  answer**, even in one beat.

## When not to use this

A story that needs a force rather than an argument — an invasion, a disease, an indifferent
machine — does not need a fair grievance, and grafting one on produces a sympathetic
monster whose sympathy goes nowhere. Use this technique when the antagonist is a person who
controls other people and the story intends to judge him. Do not use it to excuse
atrocity: a grievance can be fair and still be dwarfed by what is done in its name, and the
technique depends on the audience feeling both at once rather than on balancing them.

## Evidence status

The core rule — right about the problem, wrong about the solution, with the hero right
about the method and complicit in the problem — rests on primary statements by working
showrunners and head writers of prestige animated series, read directly and rated high
confidence by the research behind this subject; the reading of a setting reshaped so its
geography supports the grievance rests partly on a critic's secondary essay (medium). The
grievance-as-leverage delivery is a scene checkable on screen. The neutral-stranger test,
the who-pays rule and the what-runs-after obligation are practitioner judgement, the last
reinforced by a design inference in the research rather than by any source. None of this
has been tested in a played game.
