# Style plus grudge before taunts

The named concern: characterise a rival by two things before they say anything clever — the
way they do the contested thing, and the one thing they hold against the hero — and show a
slice of their life away from the contest in the same stretch. A taunt comes after, if at
all, and it is written out of the style and the grudge rather than instead of them.

## Why the order is the technique

A taunt is the cheapest characterisation available and the least informative. Trash talk is
generic by nature — it exists to unsettle, and nearly every line of it could be said by any
opponent in any story. A rival introduced by a taunt is introduced as a function, and when
that function later crosses sides the audience has nothing to watch change.

A style is the opposite. How a person does the thing they are best at is the most honest
portrait available of them: whether they protect a lead or attack from behind, whether they
take the risky line or the reliable one, whether they make room for an opponent alongside or
close the door every time. In a game this portrait is also *played*: the player learns the
rival by being passed, blocked, chased and out-braked by them, and that knowledge is more
durable than any line, because the player paid for it in their own run.

A grudge gives the style a direction. A rival without a grudge is a hard opponent; a rival with
one is a person who is hard *on you*, for a reason. The grudge must be specific — a debt, a
death, a lost place, a humiliation, a betrayal the rival blames on the hero or on what the
hero stands for — and it must be concrete enough to be the thing that later gets redirected.
A grudge stated as an attitude ("he hates newcomers") is a temperament, and a temperament
cannot be aimed somewhere else at the turn.

The slice of life is what makes the rival's later losses legible. One scene, or one recurring
image, of the rival among their own — a garage, a family table, a crew, a ritual before the
start — tells the audience what the rival has to lose. Without it, the cost of crossing has no
referent and the rival's culture after crossing has nothing to keep.

## Procedure

1. **Write the grudge first, as an event**, with who the rival blames and why the hero is a
   plausible target. Write the truth behind it separately: where the grudge really belongs.
2. **Write the style as three to five observable behaviours** in the contest's own terms —
   line choice, what triggers their aggression, how they defend, how they spend their
   weapons or resources, what they never do — and one **visible tell** that comes before
   their signature attack. Every behaviour should be something the player can notice without
   being told; the tell is what lets the player learn the rival, and later lets them read the
   same move as help.
3. **Make each behaviour say something about the grudge or the life.** A rival who never yields
   the inside line because yielding once cost their family the land is a style and a grudge
   in one; a rival who yields politely and then wrecks you in the last corner is a different
   person with a different history.
4. **Place the slice of life before the first contest**, short and unexplained. It is shown,
   not narrated, and it must contain at least one detail that later content can return to.
5. **Only then write the taunts**, and derive each from either the grudge or the behaviour the
   player has just felt. A taunt that names the block the player just ran into, or the
   specific thing the rival holds against the hero, is characterisation; a taunt that could be
   said to anyone is filler and should be cut.
6. **Make the grudge a state, not a mood.** It persists from contest to contest, changes how
   the rival drives against the player while it stands, and is resolved by an event rather
   than reset at the start of each race. A grudge that resets every race is a temperament
   again, and a multi-contest thread is what gives the later turn something to end. This
   holds for the *arc* grudge. A *contact* grudge, earned by ramming the rival in an
   ordinary race, should decay when the player stops antagonising them. One racing series
   shipped a contact grudge that never faded, players asked for it to be switched off, and
   the studio patched it to fade. Keep the two in separate states, and make each visible.
7. **Hand the style to the behaviour authors as prose**, with the reason for each behaviour,
   and ask for it to be checkable in play.

## The menu trap

The failure that looks most like this technique and is not: personality authored in a place
the player visits between contests — a menu, a lobby conversation, a stat card — and absent
from the contest itself. The player is busy during the contest, which is exactly where they
form their picture of a rival, and a rival whose character lives off the track is met on the
track as a generic opponent. Every trait that matters goes into how the rival competes first;
the off-track material may confirm it, never carry it alone.

## Decision rules

- **When a rival's first appearance is a line of dialogue, move a behaviour in front of it.**
  The player should have a picture of the rival before they have a quote.
- **When a taunt could be spoken unchanged by another rival, cut it or rewrite it against the
  grudge.**
- **When the grudge is a temperament, convert it into an event.** Something happened; someone
  is blamed.
- **When the style is declared and the agent drives like everyone else, the style does not
  exist.** A characterisation that no behaviour reads is a sheet of paper, and the player
  will meet the default opponent with the rival's name on it.
- **When there are several rivals, give each a style that is distinguishable at a glance in
  play**, because a roster whose members are told apart only by their lines is a roster of
  one opponent.
- **When the style is expressed as an offset on a difficulty setting or a vehicle, check it at
  the hardest setting and in every vehicle the rival drives.** A clamp can zero a late-braking
  offset at the top difficulty. A signature attack owned by the car class is shared by every
  rival in that car.
- **When a contact grudge never decays, give it a decay**, and leave the event-resolved rule to
  the arc grudge.

## When not to use this

- **For an opponent with no arc.** A one-race obstacle does not need a grudge or a slice of
  life; giving every opponent one dilutes the rivals who matter.
- **When the rival's mystery is the point.** A rival whose motive is deliberately withheld
  for a reveal still needs a style before a taunt, but the grudge may be shown as an effect
  without its cause — the audience must see that there is a grudge, not yet what it is.

## Evidence status

The claim that a combatant's behaviour is their characterisation rests on primary designer
statements about character-driven boss fights (high confidence), and the persistent-grudge
mechanic on documented racing and action games whose opponents remember and retaliate against
the player (high for the racing case, medium for the origin story of the action case). The
menu trap rests on one contemporary review of an early combat racer (high as a quotation, a
single source as a generalisation). The value of the slice of life before the turn rests on a
primary showrunner interview about retelling a rivalry from the former antagonist's side
(high). The strict ordering — behaviour, then persistent grudge, then lines that name the
behaviour — is the research dossier's synthesis rather than any one source's rule, and none of
it has yet been tested in a played game of this kind. The arc/contact split rests on a racing
series' own patch notes, which made a never-decaying grudge decay, and on a players' thread
asking for it to be turned off (medium: an official note quoted on a community board). The
difficulty and vehicle check rests on one shipped game's code (high as a reading of that tree,
one case).
