# Switching sides costs something

The named concern: crossing costs the rival something real, the old side visibly collects it, and
later content remembers it was paid. A crossing that costs nothing reads either as cowardice — the
rival moved to the safer side — or as authorial convenience, and in both readings the audience
stops believing in the turn.

## What counts as a cost

A cost is something the audience has already seen the rival value, which is why it depends on the
slice of life shown earlier. Standing among their own, a family member who stays behind, a place
they can no longer return to, a car, a name, a livelihood, a debt called in, a promise broken. The
best cost is the thing the refusal was protecting: the rival refused because crossing would cost
them their crew, and when they cross anyway, they lose the crew. The refusal then turns out to
have been honest, and the turn turns out to have been brave.

Cost must be **taken by someone**. A rival who declares that they can never go home has paid a
cost in a sentence. A rival whose old side comes to collect — strips their colours, burns the
garage, names them a traitor in front of people who matter to them — has paid it in a scene. The
old side's reaction is also how the audience learns the old side was real.

## The new side has a price too

The turn is not free on arrival either. A rival who did harm before crossing carries that harm
into the new group, and someone in the group should hold it against them for a while. Universal
welcome tells the audience that the harm had no weight, and it erases the people who were hurt.
The new side's suspicion is not a separate subplot to resolve quickly; it is part of the cost,
and its slow resolution is how the rival earns the belonging the neighbouring subject goes on to
build.

## A cost must be read, not only paid

The most common failure is a cost that is real in the turn scene and never again. The rival lost
their family to cross, and three chapters later is cheerful at the fire and never mentions them.
The cost was declared, and nothing downstream reads it
([declaring-an-input-is-not-consuming-it](../../../../_laws.md#declaring-an-input-is-not-consuming-it)).
Name the cost as a fact the content can branch on, and write at least two later moments that
read it: a race where the old side is on the grid, a line on an anniversary, a refusal to go
somewhere. A cost that is read twice is a wound; a cost that is read never is a plot point.

## In a game, the cost can be mechanical

A game has costs a film lacks. The rival may give up a part of their own car, or arrive without
the car that belonged to the old side. A faction's standing may fall when the rival is
recruited, closing a shop or a route. Mechanical costs are strong because the player pays a
share of them, and a player who paid part of the price believes the turn more.

The rule that keeps a mechanical cost from undoing the rival: **take the equipment, never the
skill.** A rival who loses their garage, their colours or a component has paid; a rival whose
driving gets worse on crossing has been demoted, and the player reads demotion as the story
breaking its promise. Pay the cost in the rival's own currency — a hoarder pays cash, a builder
pays a part, a proud driver pays with their name on the record — so that the price is itself a
piece of characterisation. They must be **honest about the
choice**: if recruiting the rival will close a route, the player should be able to see that
something will be lost, even if not exactly what, because a surprise penalty for a story choice
reads as punishment for being kind.

## Decision rules

- **When the rival loses nothing by crossing, take the thing the refusal was protecting.**
- **When the cost is only spoken, show the old side collect it.**
- **When the new side welcomes the rival at once and without exception, give one member a reason
  to object and let it stand for a while.**
- **When the cost is declared, write two later moments that read it**, and check they shipped.
- **When the cost is mechanical, signal before the choice that something will be lost**, and keep
  the player's share smaller than the rival's.

## When not to use this

- **When the old side is destroyed in the same beat.** If nothing is left to collect, the cost
  moves inward — guilt, survivor's debt — and must still be read later.
- **To punish the player for recruiting.** A cost meant to make recruitment unattractive is a
  balance decision wearing a story's clothes; decide the balance question separately.

## Evidence status

That a turn must cost the rival what they fought for rests on critical writing about a
prestige animated series' redemption arc — broad consensus, secondary sources (medium) — and on
showrunners of serial animation stating, in primary interviews, that endings must charge a felt
price (high, though about endings rather than defections). The suspicion of the new side and
the penalty games attach to recruiting a former enemy rest on documented party-based role-playing
games and on trope criticism of universal welcome (medium to low; the trope sources are
community-written). Paying the cost in the rival's own currency, taking equipment rather than
skill, and reading the cost later are the research dossier's proposals and this document's own
judgement; none of this has been tested in a played game yet.
