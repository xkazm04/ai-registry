# Turn on what they see

The named concern: the rival turns because they witness something their existing belief cannot
absorb — not because someone argues them out of it. The turn is a scene of seeing, and the camera
belongs to the rival.

## Why seeing beats being told

A belief that moved on argument is exactly as strong as the argument, and the audience can
always imagine a better counter-argument. A belief that moved on evidence the rival saw with
their own eyes cannot be argued back, because the rival is not the kind of person who unsees
things. That is also why the scene should be quiet. The more the hero talks during the turn,
the more the turn belongs to the hero, and the less the audience believes the rival did any
turning.

## What the rival can see

The thing seen is chosen against the rival's refusal reason, because it has to defeat that
reason specifically. A rival who refused out of loyalty to their old side sees the old side
spend them, or spend someone like them. A rival who blamed the hero for an injury sees who
actually caused it — a ledger, a confession not meant for them, an order in the wrong hand. A
rival who believed the hero was the thing they despise sees the hero, unobserved and unrewarded,
do the thing that proves otherwise: stop for a wreck, pay a debt, refuse an advantage.

Three qualities make an image of this kind work:

**It is unstaged by the hero.** If the hero arranged for the rival to see it, it is an argument
in another medium, and the rival — and the audience — will discount it. The best version is one
the hero does not know the rival saw.

**It is concrete and particular.** A body, a document, a single act, a face. Not a pattern
summarised by a narrator, and not a montage. One thing, seen once, that the audience can name.

**It aims the grudge rather than dissolving it.** The image tells the rival where their grudge
really belongs. The rival does not stop being angry; they start being angry at the right party,
and their anger now runs the same direction as the hero's goal.

**It is small.** The act that turns a rival is rarely heroic. A grand rescue can be read as
display; a small, costly kindness done when nobody is watching cannot. The smaller the act, and
the more directly it contradicts what the rival was taught — by the side that raised them, the
code they serve, the person who set them on the hero — the harder it is to explain away.

## Seeing happens in two stages

The single image cracks the belief; it rarely completes the turn. The rival drops the weapon,
stops the car, lets the hero pass — and then needs to see the image's meaning confirmed in the
world: the people the hero has helped, the effect of the hero's choices on others, the old side
doing the same harm again. Only after that does allegiance change. Witness lets the rival
convince themselves, and a rival needs more than one piece of evidence to overturn a belief
that took years to build. Collapsing both stages into one scene is what makes a witnessed turn
feel as sudden as a spoken one.

The image is also worth keeping. Brought back at the end of the arc — the same small act, the
same object — it now means the alliance rather than the doubt, and the turn is remembered
without a word of recap.

## Writing the scene

Give the rival the point of view. Hold on their reaction longer than feels comfortable. Let them
say little — a name, a question, nothing. The decision can be shown in the next scene as an act:
the rival turns up somewhere they should not be, takes a side in a race they were meant to throw,
returns something. The audience should infer the turn from an act before anyone states it, and
when it is stated, it is stated in a line of the rival's that refers to what they saw rather
than to what anyone said.

In a game, the thing seen can be *played*. The rival can witness something the player did — a
choice in the previous event, a car the player stopped for — and then the turn is earned by the
player in a sense no film can offer. When the game does this, the witnessed act must be a
recorded fact the scene reads, never a scripted act the player did not perform.

## Decision rules

- **When the hero speaks more than the rival in the turn scene, cut the hero.**
- **When the image was arranged by the hero to persuade, replace it with one the hero did not
  arrange.**
- **When the image does not answer the specific reason the rival gave in the refusal, change the
  image**, not the refusal.
- **When the turn is announced in dialogue before it is shown in an act, swap the order.**
- **When allegiance changes in the same scene as the image, add the confirmation stage** — the
  rival seeing the act's effect on others — between the two.
- **When the turn rests on something the player did, read it from recorded state**, and write the
  fallback for the player who did not do it.

## When not to use this

- **When the story's theme is persuasion itself.** A story about the power of an argument may
  legitimately turn a rival on words; then the words must be the rival's to weigh, and the scene
  must cost the speaker something.
- **When the rival is a child or a captive under coercion.** A turn under duress is a rescue,
  and seeing is not the issue; safety is.

## Evidence status

Turning by witness rather than argument rests on a prestige animated series' on-screen sequence,
in which a character raised to kill the hero watches him free an insect her order taught her to
crush, then travels among people he has saved before her allegiance changes; the sequence can be
checked directly (high), and it is the source of the two-stage reading. That a fight can stage a
turn by a refusal to finish an opponent rests on two works' documented finales (medium to high).
Making the witnessed act a recorded player choice, replayed at the turn, is the research
dossier's design proposal; no source shows it working in a game, and none of this has been
tested in a played game yet.
