# Keep the rival's own culture

The named concern: after crossing, the rival remains themselves — their speech, their rites, their
loyalties outside the group, their way of doing the thing they are good at, and their competence.
The new side gains a person, not a convert. The opposite outcome, in which the rival is absorbed
into the group's voice and quietly made weaker, is the most common way a well-built turn is
undone after it has landed.

## The two ways a rival is absorbed

**Assimilation.** The rival starts to talk like the group. Their idiom fades, their customs are
dropped without a scene, their outside loyalties are never mentioned, and they begin to agree.
It happens because the group's voice is the writers' default and the rival's voice takes work to
sustain. The audience reads it as the rival having been tamed, which is the opposite of what the
turn claimed — the turn said the rival chose, and choosing is something a person with a self
does.

**Demotion.** The rival is weaker as an ally than they were as an opponent. They lose fights they
would have won, their car is suddenly slower, their skill exists only when they are against the
player. It happens because a strong ally threatens the protagonist's centrality and the balance.
The audience reads it as a broken promise: the person who beat them in the second race was the
reason they wanted the rival on their side.

## What the rival keeps

Write a short list before the first post-turn scene, and check every scene against it:

- **Their speech**: the idiom, the dialect, the oaths, the words they refuse to say. The rival's
  lines must still be distinguishable from the group's without the name attached.
- **Their rites**: what they do before a race, after a loss, at a grave. Kept rites are the most
  economical way to show a culture, and the group noticing or joining one is how belonging is
  shown later.
- **Their outside loyalties**: the people on the old side they still love, the code they still
  keep. These generate friction inside the group, and the friction is a feature.
- **Their style**: the rival still drives the way they drove. The style that characterised them
  before the turn is the same one the player now benefits from.
- **Their competence**: the rival is at least as good on the new side as on the old, and in at
  least one thing better than the hero.

## Kept, and re-aimed

Keeping a culture does not mean freezing it. The rival's rites and vocabulary survive the turn
and are *addressed to the new side*: the ritual cry they once raised for their old master is
raised, at the decisive moment, for someone they now fight beside; the oath they swore against
the hero is sworn on the hero's behalf. The same act, re-aimed, is the most economical proof
that the person who crossed is the person who opposed. A useful construction: let the rival's
moral code supply both their taunts before the turn and their loyalty lines after it, so the
voice is visibly one voice on both sides of the crossing.

The signature move works the same way. The behaviour the player learned to fear when the rival
was against them is the behaviour the player now gets to call on. Past dread becomes present
delight, and it costs almost nothing, because the move already exists and the player already
reads it.

## Disagreement is how the culture shows

A rival who never disagrees with the group after crossing has been absorbed. The rival's culture
is visible chiefly in the decisions where it diverges: they will not race on a certain day, will
not take a certain kind of win, will not abandon a certain kind of person. These disagreements
are where the rival's values meet the group's, and the group's response is where the
neighbouring belonging work begins.

## A declared culture must be read

A design document that lists the rival's idiom, rites and loyalties and a line table that never
references them has declared a culture nobody consumes
([declaring-an-input-is-not-consuming-it](../../../../_laws.md#declaring-an-input-is-not-consuming-it)).
Make the list a set of tags that later lines carry, and check post-turn content against it: how
many of the rival's lines after crossing carry one of their speech markers, how many scenes
touch a rite, whether their outside loyalties are referenced at all. A count of zero in any row is
a finding.

## Decision rules

- **When the rival's lines after crossing could be spoken by another group member, rewrite them in
  the rival's idiom.**
- **When the rival's power drops on crossing, restore it**, and solve the balance problem in the
  encounter rather than in the character.
- **When the rival has not disagreed with the group within the first few scenes after crossing,
  write the disagreement.**
- **When a rite was shown before the turn, show it once after, addressed to the new side.**
- **When the rival had a signature move as an opponent, give the player a way to call on it.**
- **When a culture list exists, check post-turn lines against it**, and treat an empty row as a
  defect.

## When not to use this

- **When the rival's arc is a deliberate conversion.** A rival who crosses because they have
  rejected their own culture is a legitimate story; then the rejection must be shown as the
  rival's own act and must cost them, and the culture is lost on screen rather than by neglect.
- **For brief alliances.** A rival who fights alongside for one event and leaves needs only
  their style; a full culture list is wasted on a single scene.

## Evidence status

That a convert keeps their vocabulary and rituals, re-aimed at the new side, rests on a
post-apocalyptic action film's on-screen sequence and on critical and academic commentary about
it (medium-high; the commentary is secondary). That the feared move becomes the welcome one rests
on a roguelike in which a befriended former boss can be summoned into the player's fights,
documented only on a community wiki (low to medium), and on the research dossier's proposal. The rule against demotion and the check of post-turn
lines against a culture list are this document's own judgement, informed by trope criticism of
former enemies weakened after joining (low as a source, consistent with the critical consensus).
None of it has been tested in a played game yet.
