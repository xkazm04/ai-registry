# Rival to ally turn

A rival who is beaten and then joins is one of the oldest moves in serial fiction, and one of
the most reliably cheap. The cheap version has three beats and fits on one line: the rival
loses, the hero says something generous, the rival is on the team. Every reader has seen it,
and the reason it disappoints is not that it is familiar. It is that nothing in it changed the
rival's mind. Defeat changed the rival's *position* — they lost — and the speech offered them
a new one, and the story asked the audience to accept that a person who arrived with a reason
to oppose the hero has simply stopped having it.

This subject is the craft of making that crossing land: the work done before the first
contest so that the rival is a person and not an obstacle, the work done in the contest so
that losing produces respect rather than only humiliation, and the four beats around the turn
itself — the refusal, the thing seen, the price, and what the rival keeps after crossing. It
is written for games, where the contest is played rather than watched, and that single fact
changes several rules that the screen and the page can treat loosely.

## The one distinction everything descends from

**A turn is a change in what the rival believes. The change of side is only its consequence.**

The naive turn changes sides directly. It edits the roster and supplies a sentence of
motivation, and because the sentence is doing all the work, it has to be a good sentence —
which is why naive turns are speeches. A turn that lands changes a belief first: about the
hero, about whoever the rival was serving, about what the rival's own grudge was really aimed
at. Once the belief has moved, the change of side follows as the only thing a person who now
believes this could do, and the story barely has to mention it.

Two corollaries follow, and most of the techniques are one or the other. The first is that a
belief needs *material* to move: the audience must already know what the rival believed,
which means they must have seen the rival's life and grudge before the contest, not been told
them at the turn. The second is that beliefs move on evidence, not on argument. People in
fiction, as in life, are rarely talked out of a grudge; they are shown something they cannot
unsee. A rival who is persuaded by the hero's speech is a rival whose conviction was a
sentence long. The rule binds the *first* move, not every word: a speech may complete a turn
that something seen has already cracked, by naming what the rival saw. The best defence of the
speech turn is that it follows a contest in which the speaker saw something. What fails is a
speech with nothing seen behind it.

## A rival is not an antagonist, and the difference decides the whole shape

An antagonist opposes the hero's goal. A rival contests the same thing the hero wants — the
same title, the same road, the same standing — and is defined by being the hero's peer in it.
That is what makes a rival convertible and an antagonist mostly not: a rival and a hero can
both want the same thing and still end on the same side, once they discover that the thing
standing between them was never each other. The richest form of the turn is therefore a
*redirection* rather than an erasure. The rival's grudge survives the turn; what it learns is
its true target. A grudge that simply evaporates leaves the rival with no motive at all on the
new side, and a motiveless ally is furniture.

This also fixes the order of construction. The rival's grudge is designed first, against the
real source of the story's injury, and the hero is the rival's *mistaken* target — mistaken
in a way the audience can understand, because the hero really is the nearest face of the
thing the rival hates. The turn is the moment the aim corrects.

## The six obligations, in the order the audience meets them

Read the techniques as six obligations laid along the rival's arc, not as six features.

**Before the first contest:** the rival is introduced through what they *do* and what they
hold against the hero, before anything they say. In a racing game that is a driving style and
a grudge — the player learns the rival from the way they take a corner and the one thing they
cannot forgive, and only then from their mouth; when the rival does taunt, the taunt names a
behaviour the player has just felt. The grudge is a state that persists from contest to
contest until something resolves it, not a mood that resets at the start line. Two grudges
need two rules, though. The *arc* grudge is the injury the story will redirect, and it waits
for its event. A *contact* grudge, which the player earns by ramming or wrecking the rival in
an ordinary race, is fed by play and should fade when the player stops feeding it. A
contact grudge that never decays was shipped in one racing series and patched out as a bug. A
game that stores both grudges in one flag ends up with neither rule. A slice of the
rival's life away from the contest is shown in the same stretch, so that the audience has seen
what the rival stands to lose, because sympathy borrowed after the turn reads as a retcon. See
[style-plus-grudge-before-taunts](./techniques/style-plus-grudge-before-taunts.md).

**In the contest:** losing must produce respect, which is not the same as defeat. Respect is
earned when the hero wins *on terms the rival's own code honours*, or loses to them in a way
that shows the same thing, and it is earned in both directions: the two are excellent in
different ways and each has seen the other's excellence up close. The rival's reaction to
losing then reframes the contest just played — why they drove that way, and for whom — rather
than announcing friendship. A win the rival would call dishonest does not start a turn; it
starts a deeper grudge. See
[earn-respect-before-the-turn](./techniques/earn-respect-before-the-turn.md).

**At the first offer:** the rival refuses, for a want the audience understands. The refusal is
the story admitting that the rival has reasons, and it is what makes the later acceptance an
act rather than a reflex. The strongest refusals are for something the rival then gets, and
finds empty. What the refusal must do is fixed; when it happens and who voices the offer are
not. It may come before the deciding contest, as a refusal of help. A rival who has already
crossed may carry the offer, and the refusal may be wordless. It must state the rival's own
reason once, and the turn must then be the rival's initiative. The rule binds a rival whose
opposition was their own. A conscript or an unwilling enemy recruited in one conversation is
a recruit, not a rival turn, and genres that recruit that way do it without loss. See
[refuse-once-then-turn](./techniques/refuse-once-then-turn.md).

**At the turn:** the rival sees something — an act, a document, a body, a betrayal by their
own side, the hero doing something small, unobserved and unrewarded — that their existing
belief cannot absorb, and then sees enough of its effect on others to convince themselves.
The hero may be in the room; the hero must not be the one explaining it. See
[turn-on-what-they-see](./techniques/turn-on-what-they-see.md).

**On crossing:** switching sides costs the rival something real and visible — standing, kin,
money, a place, a car, a name — ideally the thing they were fighting for, and paid in their
own currency. A free crossing reads as either cowardice or a writer's convenience, and in both
readings the audience stops believing it. The cost may take the rival's equipment; it never
takes their skill. See
[switching-sides-costs-something](./techniques/switching-sides-costs-something.md).

**After crossing:** the rival keeps their own culture — speech, rites, loyalties outside the
group, the way they do the thing they are good at — and keeps their competence. Their rites are
kept and re-aimed at the new side rather than dropped, and the signature move the player
learned to fear becomes the one the player is glad to have beside them. The rivalry is part of
what is kept: where the ally still competes, they still keep score. Which side the rival is on
and whether they hold a grudge are then two separate states, and a grudge earned after the
crossing speaks differently from the one before it. The new side gains a person, not a
convert. See
[keep-the-rivals-own-culture](./techniques/keep-the-rivals-own-culture.md).

## What a game owes that a film does not

In a film the contest is shown and the audience accepts whatever result the script needs. In
a game the player produced the result, and the rival's later account of it has to agree with
what the player did. Three consequences hold across every technique here.

The **respect beat must be conditioned on the contest, or the contest must be built so that
every way of winning carries the quality the rival respects**. A rival who praises a clean
race the player won by ramming them into a wall has not shown respect; the game has shown
that it was not watching. Either the line reads a recorded fact about how the win happened,
or the contest is shaped so that only the respected kind of win is possible. A line that
reads a fact must also **claim nothing its condition did not read**. Suppose a line is gated
only on whether the rival finished, and then thanks the player for sparing them after two hits
into the wall. It has invented two facts. That is the same failure as the fixed line, one clause later.

The **refusal must happen to the player, not around them**. If the offer is a player choice,
the first acceptance the player tries is the one the rival refuses; a menu that lets the
player skip the refusal has deleted the beat that makes the turn worth having. A refusal
written as an exchange must also *play* as one. A line picker that treats numbered lines as
alternatives will show one of the four, and that one may not be the line that carries the
reason.

The **thing the rival sees can be something the player did**, which is the strongest turn a
game has and one no film can stage: the rival witnesses the player's own earlier choice and
turns on it. It must then be read from recorded play, with a written fallback for the player
who never made that choice, because a turn that credits the player with an act they did not
perform is worse than one that credits them with nothing.

The **cost and the culture must be read by later content**, not only declared at the turn. A
rival who announces that crossing cost them their family and is then never once affected by
it has paid a cost that exists only in the scene that mentioned it, and the player notices
the absence the next time the rival speaks
([declaring-an-input-is-not-consuming-it](../../../_laws.md#declaring-an-input-is-not-consuming-it)).

## The failure modes of the naive reading

The naive reading is that the turn is the scene in which the rival changes sides. It produces
six recognisable pathologies, and each is the missing half of one obligation.

**The strongest-side turn.** Defeat is the only reason given. The rival joins because the hero
proved stronger, which reads as a calculation, and the audience correctly expects the rival
to leave as soon as someone stronger appears.

**The speech turn.** The hero explains to the rival what the rival should believe now, and the
rival agrees. The belief moved on argument, so it is as fragile as the argument, and the scene
belongs to the hero rather than the rival.

**The instant turn.** The first offer is accepted. The rival had no reasons, or had reasons and
abandoned them in a line, and either way the audience learns that the opposition was never
real.

**The free turn.** Nothing is lost by crossing, and frequently the old side does not even
react. The rival's past — including whatever harm they did — is forgiven by default, and
everyone on the new side welcomes them at once, which tells the audience that harm had no
weight.

**The absorbed ally.** After crossing the rival speaks like the group, dresses like the group,
and is markedly weaker than when they were an opponent. The audience was promised the person
who beat them in the second race and got a shadow. The trap is often set by the cost itself,
when the price of crossing is paid out of the rival's ability rather than out of something
they own. The failure is the same role made weaker. It is not every loss of total power: a
former boss turned into a limited call-in of their signature move, still at full menace, has
changed role and kept competence, and players accept that.

**The taunt-first rival.** The rival is introduced by a line of trash talk that any rival in
any story could say. The audience has no picture of the person, so nothing that happens to
them later moves anyone.

## How much of this is known

The craft here is distilled from what writers and showrunners of serial animation and film,
and designers of character-driven games, have said about their own work, and from long
critical consensus about redemption arcs; the rules are strongest where a sequence can be
checked on screen and weakest where a writer's intent was relayed second-hand. None of it has
been measured in play for this genre — no playtest has yet shown that a refused-then-witnessed
turn is believed by players of a racing game where a cheap one is not — so each technique
states its evidence separately, and a team adopting the subject should treat the first
playtest of a turn as the first real test of it. One shipped game's code has been read
against it. The arc was written in full, and most of what failed failed between the script
and the code that chooses lines: a refusal that played one line of four, respect lines that
claimed facts no condition read, and a grudge flag doing three jobs. Before the playtest,
check that every line the arc depends on can actually be reached.

## Where this subject stops, and what stands next to it

**Against ally bond and found family systems.** That subject owns what happens once a
character belongs: how bond accrues across a campaign, what an ally does for and asks of the
group, how belonging is shown and how losing an ally is staged. This subject owns the
*crossing* — everything from the rival's first appearance to the first scenes after the turn
that prove the rival kept themselves. The rule for picking is the side line: if the character
has not yet crossed, or the question is whether the crossing was believable, it is here; if
the character is already on the side and the question is how the relationship deepens or
strains, it is the neighbour's. The one shared seam is retained culture. This subject owns the
commitment that the culture *survives* the crossing; the neighbour owns keeping it alive over
the long run, including the friction it causes inside the group.

**Against antagonist fair grievance craft.** That subject owns the opponent whose grievance is
fair and whose arc ends still opposed — the force the story argues with rather than recruits.
This subject owns the opponent designed to cross. The rule for picking is the designed ending:
a character the story intends to convert is a rival and belongs here, a character the story
intends to confront to the end belongs there, and a character the writer has not decided about
is a decision owed before either subject can help. The two meet constantly, because the
cleanest turn redirects the rival's grudge onto the antagonist's side; the antagonist's
grievance is the neighbour's to make fair, and the rival's grudge is this subject's to make
redirectable. A grudge is personal and aimed at a face; a grievance is structural and is the
story's argument.

**Against agent behaviour authoring.** That subject owns how a driven opponent decides: what it
knows, how it arbitrates, what it commits to, and the competence dials that make it beatable.
This subject owns none of the mechanism. It owns the characterisation brief that the mechanism
must express — in prose, as a person — and the rule that the player meets that style on the
track before they meet the rival's voice. The handoff is a sentence such as *she never lifts
through the long sweeper and closes the inside line late*: this subject writes it and decides
what it says about her; the neighbour turns it into numbers and proves the agent does it.

## The path, in order

1. **Design the rival's grudge first, against the story's real injury**, and make the hero its
   understandable but mistaken target.
2. **Write the rival's style and one slice of their life before any of their lines**, and put
   both in front of the audience before the first contest.
3. **Shape the contest so that the hero's win, or loss, shows the quality the rival's own code
   respects**, and condition the respect beat on how the contest actually went.
4. **Write the refusal**, with a reason that is the rival's and not the plot's.
5. **Choose what the rival sees**, and keep the hero from explaining it.
6. **Name the price** of crossing, show the old side collect it, and give later content a
   reason to read it.
7. **List what the rival keeps** — speech, rite, outside loyalty, competence — and check the
   first post-turn scenes against the list.
8. **Trace every line the arc depends on to the code that chooses it.** For each line, check
   that it can be reached, that it plays in full, and that it claims only what its condition
   reads.
