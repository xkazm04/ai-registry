# Ally bond and found family systems

A player does not love an ally because the script says the ally is lovable. A player loves an
ally the way anyone comes to trust a colleague: the person showed up, did something nobody else
could have done, did it in a way that was unmistakably theirs, and did not send a bill. Cutscenes
can name the bond; only play can earn it. This subject owns the systems that earn it — who the
allies are, what each one does for the player in play, how a bond grows and what it pays, how the
group is put under pressure without being broken, and how the finale cashes in everything the
player invested in the people beside them.

A found family is the strongest form of this: a group of allies who are not bound by blood or
rank but by what they have been through together, and who outlast the structure that was
supposed to hold them. It is one of the most reliable emotional engines a game has, and one of
the easiest to waste, because every lever that builds it can be pulled in a way that turns a
person into a burden or a shop.

## The load-bearing claim: an ally is judged by the ledger, not the lines

**Every ally carries a ledger in the player's head, and the ledger decides the bond before the
writing gets a vote.** On one side is what the ally has cost: a slot that could have held
something stronger, a pace slowed to theirs, a failure state the player has to prevent, a
resource diverted, a choice taken away. On the other side is what the ally has given, in play,
when it counted. Players keep this ledger whether or not the designer does, and an ally whose
ledger runs negative is resented no matter how well they are written — the writing then reads as
the game insisting on an affection the play has refuted.

The ledger is not a sum, and that is the part teams miss. Small recurring costs add up; an ally
who once actively sabotages the player — blocks the shot, blows the cover, nags them off their
own pace at the moment it mattered — does not add a debit, it poisons every beat the character
carries afterwards. One such moment can undo a campaign's worth of writing, which is why the
remedy is to bend the rules in the ally's favour rather than to simulate them honestly: an ally
the enemies do not see while the player is sneaking is a cheat, and it is the right cheat.

The naive reading treats attachment as a writing problem: give the ally a backstory, a joke, a
sad scene, a voice. All of that is necessary and none of it survives a negative ledger. The
opposite naive reading treats attachment as a reward problem: make the ally useful, attach a
perk to every rank of the relationship, and players will invest. That produces engagement with
the perk and indifference to the person, which is the vending machine. The craft sits between:
the ally is useful, the usefulness is unmistakably theirs, and the bond deepens what they do
rather than unlocking a menu of things they sell.

## Two failure poles

**The burden.** The ally who has to be escorted, protected, waited for or carried. Each instance
is small; the sum teaches the player that this person is a tax. Burden is not the same as
vulnerability — an ally can be in danger, can be hurt, can need rescue once as a story beat —
but the moment the player's success is gated on managing an ally's weakness as a recurring
chore, the ledger tips and does not come back. The rule is absolute in its direction: an ally
may ask something of the player in the story; an ally must never cost the player in play. See
[ally-never-costs-the-player](./techniques/ally-never-costs-the-player.md).

**The vending machine.** The ally whose relationship is a transaction loop: give gift, receive
approval points, cross threshold, receive perk. The loop is legible and players optimise it,
which is exactly the problem — once a bond is a price, the player is shopping, and the person
behind the counter stops being a person. The tell is that the optimal play pattern involves no
attention to who the ally is: the same item, given repeatedly, at the same moment, by a player
skipping the dialogue. See [bond-pays-in-play-not-vending](./techniques/bond-pays-in-play-not-vending.md).

Both poles fail the same test from opposite sides. The burden costs the player and gives
nothing back in character; the vending machine gives the player something and takes the
character out of the giving. There is a quieter third failure that the fear of vending
produces: the bond that pays nothing in play at all, which players correctly read as optional
and skip. The answer to the vending machine is a dividend shaped like the person, not the
absence of a dividend.

## Who pays for the family

A group becomes a family when its members pay to belong to it, and the craft question is who
pays. **The cost of belonging falls on the allies, never on the player.** The former rival who
strips a part off their own car to give it to the player is weaker on the track for it; the
informant who warns the player has burned a source; the crew who hide the player have put
their own standing at risk. Each such choice, made visibly for the others, is what turns
proximity into kinship, and it is the mirror image of the burden: the ally carries a cost so
that the player does not. A group whose members never give anything up to stay is a reward
screen, and a group that could not come apart was never tested as one — which is why the
pressure act below exists. A shared rule or ritual — a meal, a roll call, a phrase said before
the start — gives the group a form the player can watch it keep, and it is the cheapest
recurring evidence that the family exists between its big scenes.

## Design backwards from the finale

The most common structural failure of a crew is that it was recruited forwards: each ally was
added because a chapter needed a new face, and when the finale arrives half of them have
nothing to do. The finale then either parades them in a montage, which spends the bond on
spectacle, or forgets them, which tells the player their investment was decorative.

**Design what the finale needs from each ally first, then recruit for it.** List the
irreducible tasks the final confrontation contains — the lock nobody else can open, the line
nobody else can hold, the voice nobody else can speak with — and give each ally exactly one of
them. Then work backwards: each ally's recruitment establishes the capability, the middle of the
game deepens it, and the finale calls on it. An ally recruited with no finale task is a
character without a payoff, and the recruitment scene is spent on someone the story will not
cash in — the narrative form of an artifact that compiles and is never wired
([compiling-is-not-wiring](../../../_laws.md#compiling-is-not-wiring)).

The finale's call-on is **once each**. Every ally gets one moment where they are the answer,
named and visible, with the player choosing to make the call. Twice dilutes it and a pool of
interchangeable helpers erases it. The same scarcity works at a smaller scale across the
campaign: an ally the player may call in once per event to perform their signature move pays
every session without becoming a button, because once per event keeps each call a decision
about when. The strongest finales make the outcome depend on how the player prepared each bond
— not a single good-or-bad ending switch, but a finale whose casualties and successes are
traceable, ally by ally, to what the player did with each one.

One limit binds every call-on: **the allies prepare, contribute and hold the line; the player
performs the decisive act**, with the game's core verb. An ally who lands the final blow, makes
the merciful choice or solves the last problem in a cutscene has taken the ending away from
the person the whole game trained for it, and players read it as stolen agency however well
the ally earned it. And a contribution that changes the finale's rules is disclosed before it
is used — announced, named, visible — because an ally's help that arrives as an invisible
modifier is indistinguishable from the game cheating, in either direction. See
[design-the-finale-need-first](./techniques/design-the-finale-need-first.md).

## Allies pay in their own currency

When every ally's contribution is the same kind of thing — a percentage, a stat boost, a better
drop rate — the allies are one ally with several portraits. **Each ally pays in a currency that
expresses who they are.** The mechanic pays in parts and fixes; the informant pays in what the
player is told before a fight; the old driver pays in a route nobody else knows; the medic pays
in a second chance. The currency is the characterisation: a player who has learned that this
ally's help arrives as a warning, at the last moment, with a joke, has learned who the ally is
without reading a line of backstory.

Own currencies also do economic work: allies who pay in different kinds do not compete for the
same slot in the player's head, so the player does not rank them on one axis and discard the
bottom of the list. This subject decides the kind, the timing and the voice of each payout;
how large a payout is, and whether the aggregate keeps the economy inside its band, is the
economy's question and is stated here only in prose. See
[pay-in-their-own-currency](./techniques/pay-in-their-own-currency.md).

## Pressure: one wavers, the institution betrays

A crew that is never tested is a roster. A crew where everyone is tested equally is a soap.
**One ally wavers.** Under the pressure of the middle act — a threat, an offer, a loss, a fear
of what the finale will cost — one person in the circle hesitates, and the player gets the
scene that decides whether they come back. One is the number because it makes the waver an
event: the player sees the group hold around the person who slipped, which is what a found
family is. Two waverers make the group look brittle, none makes it look untested. The waver is
motivated in the ally's own terms, it is foreshadowed, and it never removes anything the player
invested without the player getting a scene to fight for it. A waverer hangs back; a waverer
never harms. The waver shows first in play — the ally who does not answer the call, the help
that arrives a beat late — before any line explains it, and it is the strongest available proof
that the bond is not a vending machine: a machine cannot refuse, and a person who could have
and came back is worth more than one who never could. See
[one-ally-wavers](./techniques/one-ally-wavers.md).

**Betrayal comes from the institution, not the friend.** When a story needs a betrayal —
and most stories about allies under pressure do — route it through the structure the allies
belong to: the team owner, the league, the employer, the authority, the family business. The
institution betrays the player and, in doing so, tests a friend: it offers the friend a deal,
a threat or a way out. The friend's answer is the climax of the bond. A friend who betrays
punishes the player for the very investment the game asked for, and teaches them to hold back
from the next ally; an institution that betrays converts that investment into payoff, because
the found family is exactly what survives when the structure fails. The friend may be made the
instrument of the institution's order — the one who has to pass it on — and that is often the
sharpest form of the test. Suspicion of the friend is a legitimate tool on the way: clues that
could read as betrayal, planted fairly, resolved as loyalty once the institution's offer is
revealed. It is resolved well before the finale, because suspicion left standing into the last
act becomes the distrust it was only meant to imitate. The tragic exception is
real and rare — a friend's betrayal as the story's central point — and it is telegraphed, it is
the whole design rather than a twist, and it is never paid for out of the player's mechanical
investment. See
[the-institution-betrays-not-the-friend](./techniques/the-institution-betrays-not-the-friend.md).

## The bond is state, and the state must be read

A bond is a variable the game holds about the player and an ally. The craft failure that
matters most at production scale is the bond that is written and never read: a trust value that
climbs across twenty hours and changes one line in the finale. A declared relationship that no
scene, no call-on and no ending consults is decoration that looks like a system
([declaring-an-input-is-not-consuming-it](../../../_laws.md#declaring-an-input-is-not-consuming-it)).
The audit is the same one any state contract gets — for every bond, the list of places that read
it — and an ally whose bond is read nowhere but the bond screen has no bond in play.

The opposite error is a bond so heavily read that it becomes the game's main switch, gating
content behind thresholds the player must grind. A bond read in a handful of well-chosen places
— how an ally speaks before a race, whether they answer a call, what they bring to the finale —
is worth more than one read at fifty thresholds.

## Failure modes of the naive reading

**"Write them better."** Characterisation cannot repair a ledger. An ally who costs the player
in play is resented in proportion to how hard the writing insists they are loved.

**"Reward the relationship."** A perk per rank turns the bond into a price list. The reward is
right; the shape is wrong. The bond should change what the ally does, not unlock what they sell.

**"More allies, more attachment."** Attachment scales with how much each ally matters, not with
how many there are. A crew larger than the finale has tasks for is a crew with passengers.

**"The twist is the friend."** The friend's betrayal is the cheapest shock available and the
most expensive one to pay for, because it retroactively taxes every hour the player spent on the
bond and makes them guarded with every ally after it.

**"Everyone gets a crisis."** Serial personal crises, one per ally, make the group a queue of
problems. Depth comes from one waver the group holds through, and from the others showing who
they are by how they respond to it.

**"The finale is the boss."** A finale that is a fight against the antagonist and nothing else
leaves the crew standing at the edge of the screen. The finale is where the bonds are cashed;
the antagonist is the occasion.

## The path, in order

1. **Write the finale's needs first**: the irreducible tasks of the last confrontation, one per
   ally, each one something only that ally can answer.
2. **Recruit for the needs**, establishing each ally's capability at recruitment so the finale
   call-on is a promise kept rather than a surprise.
3. **Give each ally their own currency** of help, stated in prose with its kind, its timing and
   its voice, and hand the magnitudes to the economy.
4. **Audit every ally's ledger** for costs the player pays in play, and remove each one or
   convert it into a story ask; bend the rules in the ally's favour wherever honesty would
   let them sabotage the player.
5. **Shape the bond so it changes what the ally does**, and list where the bond is read; an
   unread bond is a finding.
6. **Make the allies pay to belong**, visibly and for each other, and give the group one
   ritual it keeps.
7. **Choose the one ally who wavers**, motivate and foreshadow it, show it in play first, and
   give the player the scene that brings them back.
8. **Route the betrayal through the institution**, and let it test a friend whose answer is the
   bond's climax.
9. **Call on each ally once in the finale**, visibly, by the player's choice, disclosed before
   it changes any rule, with the decisive act left to the player and the outcome traceable to
   how each bond was tended.

## Where this subject stops

The sibling that shares the most ground is the rival-to-ally turn, and the seam is the moment
of crossing. That subject owns the arc that converts an opponent into someone on the player's
side — the grudge, the respect earned in the contest, the refusal, the thing seen, the price of
crossing, and the commitment that the rival's culture survives it. This subject owns everyone
once they are on the player's side, including those who were there from the start: what they
pay the player, how the bond grows and strains, who wavers, who is tested, how losing one is
staged, and keeping a convert's culture alive over the long run, friction included. The
picker: if the question is "how does this person come over, and was the crossing believable",
it is the turn; if it is "now that they are here, what do they do for the player and what does
the finale need from them", it is here. One word is shared and means two things: the sibling's
price of crossing is what the rival loses to the old side, paid in the rival's currency; this
subject's own currency is how the ally pays the player afterwards. A turned rival enters this
subject's roster at the moment of the turn, and the finale-first rule binds them like anyone
else — a rival whose turn has no finale task is a beautiful scene that pays nothing.

Game economy tuning is the second neighbour, and the seam is the number. This subject decides
that the mechanic pays in parts, once a race, unprompted, with a grumble; the economy decides
how many parts, against which band, at which progression point. Payouts appear here only in
prose, and a magnitude written into this subject's documents is a magnitude the economy cannot
see and will not balance against. When an ally's payout needs a number, hand the kind and the
timing across and let the economy own the value.

Learning curve and teaching design is the third, and the seam is the lesson. Allies are the
most natural teachers a game has — the old hand who shows the player a line, the mechanic who
explains what the new part does — and the schedule of what is taught, where and when it is
tested, belongs to that subject. This subject owns whether the teaching is in the ally's voice
and currency, and whether the ally remains someone after the lesson is over. The picker: if the
question is "has the player learned it", it is teaching; if it is "does the player care who
taught them", it is here. An ally reduced to a tutorial delivery mechanism is a failure both
subjects can see, and the fix lives here.
