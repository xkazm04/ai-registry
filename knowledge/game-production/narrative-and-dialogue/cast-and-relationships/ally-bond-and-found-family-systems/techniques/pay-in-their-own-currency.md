# Pay in their own currency

The named concern: each ally helps the player in a kind of help that expresses who they are,
distinct from every other ally's, delivered with their timing and their voice. The currency is
the characterisation; a roster paying in one currency is one ally with several portraits.

## The uniform-currency failure

The default production path gives every ally a bonus from the same table: a percentage to
speed, a percentage to damage, a percentage to income. It is cheap to balance and it erases the
crew. Players rank allies on the one axis that exists, keep the top of the list and never think
about the rest; the allies' personalities become flavour attached to numbers that could be
swapped between them without anyone noticing. The test is exactly that swap: **if two allies'
payouts could be exchanged and the player would not feel a wrong note, they are paying in the
same currency.**

## What a currency is

A currency has four parts, and the craft is to make each part carry character.

**Kind.** What arrives: a part, a fix, a piece of information, a route, cover, a second chance, a
contact, a lie told on the player's behalf. The kind follows from the ally's life — the mechanic
pays in metal, the informant in what the player is told, the veteran in a line nobody else
knows.

**Timing.** When it arrives: before the event, at the last moment, after a loss, only when the
player is losing. Timing is as expressive as kind; a warning that always arrives at the last
second, slightly too late to be comfortable, says something no backstory line can.

**Condition.** What it depends on: unprompted, on request, only if the player did something,
only on certain ground. Conditions are where the bond lives — the condition loosens as the bond
deepens, so a closer ally pays more readily in the same currency.

**Voice.** How it is delivered: the grumble, the joke, the silence, the note left on the seat.
The voice is the line attached to the payout, and it is the cheapest place to keep an ally
present in every session.

## The best payment costs the giver

The strongest currency is one the ally gives up rather than produces. A part pulled off the
ally's own machine, a source burned to bring the player a warning, a favour called in that the
ally was saving for themselves — each is visibly paid out of the ally's own stock, so the gift
is also a choice made for the player at the ally's expense. That is the found-family mechanism
in its smallest unit: the cost of belonging falls on the ally, and the player sees it fall. A
payout the ally produces at no cost to themselves is service; one that leaves them poorer is
loyalty, and it needs no line to say so.

When the payments are physical, let them **accumulate into one object** the player carries to
the end — a machine built from what each ally gave, a jacket with every crew's patch, a map
annotated in five hands. The found family is then on screen in every frame that object appears,
the finale recalls each ally without a word of recap, and losing or keeping the object becomes
a stake that belongs to the whole group.

## Why distinct currencies matter beyond characterisation

Allies paying in different kinds do not compete for one slot in the player's evaluation, so no
ally is the strictly worse version of another. The roster stops being a ranking and becomes a
set of tools for different situations, and the player's attachment spreads across the crew
rather than collapsing onto the best-numbered member. Distinct currencies also give the finale
its shape for free: each finale task is a large form of an ally's everyday currency.

## The seam with the economy

This technique decides kind, timing, condition and voice. It does not decide magnitude. How
many parts, how good the information, how often the second chance — those are economy values,
balanced against bands this subject does not hold. State payouts in prose here ("the mechanic
pays in salvage after every race, more after a crash, with a complaint about the crash") and
hand the magnitudes across. A number written into an ally's design document is a number the
economy cannot see and will not balance against, and it will drift from the one in the tuning
tables within a milestone.

## Decision rules

- **When two allies' payouts survive a swap test, give one of them a different kind.**
- **When a payout's timing is "whenever the player asks", look for a timing that says more** —
  a currency that arrives on its own schedule carries a person; one on demand is a button.
- **When the bond deepens, loosen the condition rather than raising the magnitude**, so the
  relationship shows up as the ally's readiness rather than as a bigger number.
- **When a payout needs a number, write the kind and timing here and let the economy own the
  value.**
- **When an ally has no obvious currency, the ally's life is under-specified**; find what they
  do for a living or what they are good at before inventing a bonus.
- **When a payout can be drawn from the ally's own stock instead of produced from nowhere, draw
  it from their stock**, and show what it left them without.
- **When an ally's payment makes them weaker as a competitor, keep the loss to their equipment
  or standing, never their skill**, so the person the player admired is still there.

## When not to use this

- **For a pure combat squad** in a genre where allies are interchangeable units by contract —
  the player hired soldiers, not friends. Even there, a named few above the line benefit from
  their own currency.
- **For a single companion game**, where one ally carries every kind of help. Distinctness then
  applies across the companion's moods or phases rather than across people.

## Evidence status

That allies should pay in a currency matching who they are ("a hoarder pays cash, a builder
pays a part") is a design proposal in the dossiers, derived from sourced patterns about bonds
paying in play rather than sourced itself. The payment that costs the giver, and payments that
accumulate into one object the player carries, are likewise proposals inferred from a
high-confidence pattern about found families costing their members something, which rests on
creators' statements about prestige animated series, not on games. The swap test and the
four-part anatomy of a currency are practitioner synthesis. No claim here has been tested in a
played game; the game the dossiers serve has not been played by anyone.
