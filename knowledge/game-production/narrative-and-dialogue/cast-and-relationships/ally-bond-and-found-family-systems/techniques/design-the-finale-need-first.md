# Design the finale need first

The named concern: before any ally is written or recruited, write down what the final
confrontation needs from each one — a task only that ally can answer — then build each ally's
arc backwards from that need, and call on each ally exactly once in the finale.

## Why forwards fails

A crew recruited forwards grows by chapter: a new region needs a local, a new mechanic needs a
teacher, a lull needs a joke. Each addition is locally justified. At the finale the designer
discovers a roster of eight with tasks for three, and has two bad options: a montage that
parades the rest (spending the bond on spectacle and giving the player nothing to choose), or
silence (telling the player their investment in five people was decorative). Both are visible
to the player as the moment the game stopped caring about the people it asked them to care
about.

An ally recruited with no finale task is the narrative form of an artifact that builds and is
never wired ([compiling-is-not-wiring](../../../../_laws.md#compiling-is-not-wiring)): the
recruitment scene ran, the bond accrued, and nothing reachable consumes it at the moment the
story is cashed.

## The procedure

**Write the finale's irreducible tasks.** Not the plot of the finale — its needs. The lock only
one person can open, the line only one person can hold, the route only one person knows, the
voice the antagonist will only hear from one mouth, the repair that has to happen at speed under
fire. Each task is something the player cannot do alone and no generic helper could do instead.

**Assign one task per ally, one ally per task.** The count of allies is the count of tasks; an
extra ally is a passenger and an extra task is an obstacle nobody was prepared for. Where a
task fits two allies equally, one of them is not needed or the task is not irreducible.

**Plant the capability at recruitment, then prove it in a trust beat.** Two beats per ally
before the finale, at minimum: the acquisition beat that brings them in and shows what they
can do, and a later trust beat where they do it for the player when it costs them something.
The finale then calls on a capability the player has seen twice and a loyalty they have seen
once. A finale capability revealed for the first time at the finale is a convenience, and
players read it as one.

**Deepen the capability in the middle.** The bond with the ally grows around what they can do
— the middle game is where the player learns what the ally can do at their best, and the ally's
own-currency help is often a smaller form of the finale task.

**Call on each ally once.** In the finale, each ally gets one moment where they are the answer,
by the player's choice, named and visible. Once, because the moment's weight comes from
scarcity — a call-on usable twice is a tool, used once it is a scene. Before the finale the
same scarcity works per event: an ally who may be called once per encounter stays a decision
about when, while an ally who can be summoned at will is an ability with a portrait.

**Leave the decisive act to the player.** The allies prepare the ground, hold the line, open
the lock, cut the lights; the player, with the game's core verb, does the thing the finale is
about. An ally who lands the final blow or makes the final choice — however earned — has taken
the ending from the person the game trained for it, and players name that precisely as stolen
agency. Design each finale task so that it enables the player's act rather than replacing it.

**Disclose any rule an ally changes.** Where a finale contribution alters the arena — a hazard
removed, a weakness exposed, a modifier applied — announce it before it takes effect, by name
and with the ally's face on it. An undisclosed contribution is an invisible boost, which reads
as the game cheating whether it helps or hurts, and it also erases the ally from the moment it
was meant to honour. A contribution that is presentation only, with no rule change, needs no
disclosure and carries no fairness risk.

## Preparation shapes the outcome

The strongest form ties the finale's result to how each bond was tended. A well-prepared ally
succeeds at their task; a neglected one fails it or arrives late; a wavering one who was never
brought back is absent. The outcome is not a single good-or-bad ending switch but a finale whose
successes and losses are traceable, ally by ally, to what the player did. The player reads their
own choices in the result, which is the point at which the bond system stops being a set of
meters and becomes a story they authored.

Two cautions keep this honest. The criteria must be **learnable before the finale** — a player
who loses an ally for a reason the game never hinted at experiences a punishment, not a
consequence. And the **failure branch must still be a scene** worth playing, because a neglected
ally's failure is the most memorable outcome the system produces and the most likely to be
underwritten.

## Decision rules

- **When adding an ally, name their finale task first.** No task, no ally — or a minor character
  who is not presented as part of the crew.
- **When two allies share a finale task, cut one or split the task** into two irreducible needs.
- **When a finale capability first appears at the finale, move its first appearance to
  recruitment.**
- **When an ally can be called on repeatedly, make the call scarce** — once in the finale, once
  per encounter before it — or accept that it is an ability and stop presenting it as a bond.
- **When the finale outcome depends on bond state, state the dependency to the player in-world
  before the finale**, through an ally's own worry, a planning scene or a warning.
- **When a finale task would have the ally perform the decisive act, rewrite it as the act that
  makes the player's decisive act possible.**
- **When an ally's contribution changes a rule of the finale, disclose it before it applies**;
  when it cannot be disclosed cleanly, make it presentation only.
- **When an ally's death is wanted for weight, put it on screen and tie it to a task**, or cut
  it; an ally killed off-screen to raise the stakes spends the bond and pays nothing back.

## Evidence status

Finale-first recruitment is the best-sourced claim in this subject: a lead writer's own
statement that a whole game was built around its final mission, with the team recruited and
made loyal for it and survival decided by that preparation, is a primary source rated high in
two separate dossiers, and an encyclopaedia summary of the same game confirms that survival
turns on loyalty, upgrades and role assignment. That the decisive act must stay with the
protagonist rests on a primary interview about an ending revised after players objected that
another character had made the crucial choice. The disclosure rule for rule-changing
contributions and the once-per-encounter scale are the dossiers' own design proposals, not
findings; the acquisition-and-trust-beat minimum is the dossier's rule, sourced to the same
primary statement. Nothing here has been tested in a played game, and the game the dossiers
were written for has not been played by anyone.

## When not to use this

- **In a game with no authored finale** — an endless or systemic structure — the finale-need
  becomes a recurring climax need, and the same backwards design applies per climax.
- **For allies who exist to be lost.** A mentor whose death is the midpoint has a different
  need — the midpoint — and the rule applies to that beat instead of the finale.
