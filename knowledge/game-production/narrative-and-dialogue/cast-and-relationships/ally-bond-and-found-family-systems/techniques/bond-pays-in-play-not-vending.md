# Bond pays in play, not vending

The named concern: a deeper bond with an ally must change what the ally does in play — how they
help, when, in what voice — without turning the relationship into a shop where the player
inserts attention and receives perks. The bond is rewarded; the reward is the person getting
more like themselves, not a price list getting longer.

## The vending machine, precisely

The vending machine has three parts: a **legible input** (a gift, a dialogue choice the ally is
known to like, a task done for them), a **visible meter**, and a **threshold perk** that bears no
relation to who the ally is. Players optimise it immediately, because legibility is an
invitation. The optimal pattern needs no attention to the character — the same gift, repeated,
with the dialogue skipped — and once the player is playing that pattern, the bond has become a
grind wearing a face.

Each of the three parts is fine in isolation. Legible inputs help players care deliberately;
visible meters reassure; perks reward. The failure is their combination into a closed
transaction loop whose output is detachable from the ally: if the perk could be sold by any
shopkeeper, the ally is a shopkeeper.

**The input is what closes the loop, more than the perk.** Some of the best-loved bond systems
in the medium pay a perk at every rank, and many of those perks would fail the shopkeeper test:
a combat bonus, a discount, a fusion boost. They hold because each rank can only be reached
through an authored scene with that ally, and the scene costs something scarce: a day of
in-game time the player could have spent elsewhere, a gift earned once a run, a won race. The
player optimises them and stays attached anyway, because the optimal path still goes through
the person. The machine forms when the input is cheap and repeatable, so a rank can be bought
without meeting the ally: the same item handed over again and again, a dialogue choice known to
please. So test the input first. A cheap, repeatable input makes any perk vending. A scarce
input that buys a scene makes even a generic perk tolerable, and the own-currency perk remains
the better one.

The overcorrection fails too. A team that fears the vending machine strips the dividend out and
leaves the relationship as optional conversation, and players skip it, correctly, because the
game has told them it does not matter. The naive failure has two forms — all effect, which is
ground and felt as nothing, and no effect, which is skipped — and the craft is the narrow road
between them: a dividend the player feels in play, shaped so it cannot be separated from the
person giving it.

## What paying in play looks like

**The bond changes behaviour, not inventory.** At low trust the ally helps when asked; at high
trust they help before being asked. At low trust their warning arrives late and vague; at high
trust it arrives early and exact. At low trust they hold back their best trick; at high trust
they use it for the player. The same capability, expressed with more commitment — so the player
sees the relationship in the action rather than in a menu.

**The bond is earned through play with the ally, not transactions at the ally.** Trust rises
from things done together — runs where the ally was used, crises the player showed up for, asks
the player answered — rather than from items handed over. A bond that grows from shared play
cannot be ground in a menu, because the grinding is the playing.

**The ally appears in play, scarcely.** The most direct dividend is the ally themselves: at a
deep enough bond, the player may call them in once per encounter to do the thing they are known
for — the move the player once feared from them as an opponent, now on the player's side.
Once per encounter is the shape that keeps it a moment rather than a button; the player
decides when, and the decision is about the ally.

**The ally talks about more than what they pay.** Keep the character's voice independent of
the payout. An ally whose every line concerns what they are about to give, or just gave, has
been reduced to the payout's announcer; an ally who talks about their sister, the weather, the
rival they hate and the race the player just botched is a person who happens also to help.
Optional conversation the player can take or leave while still playing — spoken while moving,
not in a menu that stops play — is the cheapest way to keep that voice present without
charging the player time for it.

**The bond's readings are few and well chosen.** A bond read at fifty thresholds becomes a
gating system; a bond read nowhere is decoration. The right number of places that consult it
is small and each is load-bearing: how the ally speaks before a decisive event, whether they
answer a call, what they bring to the finale, whether they are the one who wavers or the one
who holds. Every bond has a written list of its readers; a bond whose list is empty is a finding
([declaring-an-input-is-not-consuming-it](../../../../_laws.md#declaring-an-input-is-not-consuming-it)),
because a trust value that climbed for twenty hours and changed one line was declared, written,
displayed and never consumed.

## The meter question

Whether to show the bond is a separate decision, and the default is to hide the number and show
the behaviour. A visible meter turns every interaction into a measurement, which is the
condition under which players optimise rather than relate. When the bond must be legible — the
player needs to know an ally is drifting so they can act — express it in the ally's conduct and
lines first, and in a coarse state (cold, steady, close) rather than a number. A number invites
arithmetic; a state invites attention.

## Decision rules

- **When a bond threshold grants a perk, test the input before the perk.** If the rank can be
  reached by a cheap, repeatable input, the perk is vending whatever it is, so make the input
  scarce or put a scene in front of it. If the rank is reached only through an authored scene
  bought with something scarce, test whether any other character could grant the perk. If one
  could, prefer a change in how this ally helps; the generic perk is tolerable, not ideal.
- **When trust can be raised by an item or a menu action alone, add a play requirement or
  remove the path**, because an input that bypasses play is the grind the player will find.
- **When a bond is declared, list its readers before the bond is built.** No readers, no bond.
- **When a bond is read at many thresholds, collapse them to the few that change behaviour**,
  and move the rest to flavour lines that cost nothing to miss.
- **When the bond must be visible, show conduct first and a coarse state second**, never a
  numeric meter unless the game is openly about managing it.
- **When an ally's lines are all about their payout, write the lines that are not** before
  writing another payout line.
- **When a deep bond lets the player call the ally in, limit the call to once per encounter**
  and make the called move the ally's signature, not a generic effect.

## When not to use this

- **In a game whose genre contract is the transaction.** A management game where allies are
  openly staff with wages and morale is honest about being a shop, and the player accepted it;
  vending is the design. The own-currency rule still applies there.
- **For flavour-only companions** who carry no mechanical weight at all. A bond that pays only
  in dialogue is fine when nobody expects it to pay anything else — though such characters are
  rarely the ones a finale should call on.

## Evidence status

The two-sided naive failure — no gameplay effect and players skip the relationship, all effect
and the characters become tools — comes from the dossier's reading of a published comparative
analysis of one long-running series' bond system, which is a secondary source rated medium. The
optional, take-it-or-leave-it companion conversation delivered while moving rests on a primary
developer interview. The once-per-encounter summon of a former opponent is documented only on a
fan wiki and is low confidence as a description of any shipped mechanic, though the shape is
sound on its own terms. The hidden-meter and coarse-state preference, and the rule that every
bond lists its readers, are practitioner judgement, not sourced findings. The input-first test
was added on 2026-10-06, after two independent research lanes found perk-per-rank systems
loved despite generic perks. Each gated its ranks behind authored scenes bought with scarce
time or a gift earned once a run. One series' director said in a primary interview that
players need those ranks to power up even while caring about the story. A lead writer of
another series said, also primary, that its follower content had moved from gating by approval
to events whose conversation the approval only shapes. That supports the "change behaviour,
not inventory" rule. A practitioner talk at a major developer conference names the coin loop
"seducing a vending machine" and concedes it is the easier one to build. No source was found
showing that grinding such a system lowered attachment. None has been tested in a played game;
the game the dossier serves has not been played by anyone.
