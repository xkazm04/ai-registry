# Ally never costs the player

The named concern: audit every ally for the costs the player pays in play to keep them, and
remove each cost or convert it into something the story asks rather than something the play
charges. An ally may want things, need things, be in danger, be wrong; what an ally may not do
is make the player's run worse in order to exist.

## The ledger the player keeps

A player tracks, without being asked, what each ally has cost and what each ally has given.
The costs are rarely dramatic. They are a slot in the loadout that could have held something
stronger, a pace slowed to the ally's, a fail state triggered by the ally's health rather than
the player's, a resource diverted to keep the ally functional, a route closed because the ally
cannot take it, a decision taken out of the player's hands because the ally objects. Each is
small; the sum is a verdict. An ally whose ledger runs negative is resented, and the resentment
grows with the warmth of the writing, because the writing now reads as the game insisting on an
affection the play has refuted.

The ledger is asymmetric in time. A cost the player pays repeatedly is remembered at full
weight every time; a gift the ally gives once is remembered as one event. So the rule is not
"keep the ledger balanced" but **"keep the cost column empty"**, because a recurring cost
cannot be outweighed by occasional help.

It is also not additive at the top. An ally who once actively sabotages the player — gives
away their position, blocks the line at the decisive moment — has not added one debit; that
moment colours every later beat the character carries. The consequence for implementation is
counter-intuitive: **cheat in the ally's favour.** An ally who is invisible to enemies while the
player is not yet in combat, who cannot be the one who trips the alarm, who never takes the
racing line the player is on, is less realistic and much more loved. Honest simulation of an
ally is a choice to let them sabotage the player some fraction of the time, and that fraction
is the one players remember.

## What counts as a cost

The test is mechanical rather than tonal: **does the player's outcome get worse, or their
options narrower, because this ally is present?** If yes, it is a cost. Six kinds recur.

**Escort cost.** The ally moves slower, takes a worse line, or must be kept within range. Fix:
the ally keeps up, or the player is free to leave them and they arrive on their own.

**Protection cost.** The ally's death or defeat fails the player's objective. Fix: the ally
cannot be the reason the player fails; their danger is a story beat with a fixed outcome or a
state the ally recovers from by themselves.

**Slot cost.** Taking the ally occupies a place a stronger option could have held, so choosing
them is choosing to be weaker. Fix: the ally's slot is additive, or what they bring is of a
kind no alternative offers, which is the own-currency rule doing defensive work.

**Upkeep cost.** The ally consumes a resource the player would otherwise spend on themselves.
Fix: the ally is self-sufficient, or their upkeep is paid in a currency the player has in
surplus and the cost is converted into a reason to visit them.

**Veto cost.** The ally blocks an action or a route, removing the player's choice. Fix: the
ally objects, loudly and in character, and the player is still free; the objection becomes a
line and a consequence in the bond rather than a locked door.

**Pace cost.** The ally leads, hurries or nags — sets the tempo the player must follow, repeats
the objective, tells the player to get a move on. It costs the player authorship of their own
play, and it is the cost designers most often mistake for characterisation. Fix: let the player
lead and have the ally follow, comment once, and fall silent.

## Story ask versus play cost

The distinction that keeps this technique from sanding every ally into a servant: **an ally
may ask something of the player in the story, and must not cost the player in play.** An ally
who needs the player to show up for their brother's funeral, to back them in an argument, to
win a race that matters to them — these are asks. They give the ally wants of their own, they
are optional or chosen, and they are the material of a bond. An ally whose low health ends the
player's run is a cost, even if the scene around it calls it loyalty.

When a cost is load-bearing to the story — the ally really is injured, really cannot keep up —
convert it: the injury happens in a scene, is paid for in story, and the ally's play behaviour
does not degrade the player's run. The player experiences the ally's weakness as something to
feel about, not as something to manage.

## Decision rules

- **When an ally's failure state can fail the player's objective, remove the coupling.** The
  ally may be knocked out of a fight; the fight is not lost for it.
- **When an ally would occupy a slot, make the slot additive or the contribution unique**,
  because a slot that forces a stronger-or-ally decision loses the ally every time a player
  optimises.
- **When the story needs the ally to be vulnerable, stage the vulnerability once, in a scene**,
  and keep their play behaviour whole.
- **When an ally objects to a player action, let them object and let the player act.** The
  objection writes to the bond; it never locks the door.
- **When playtesters describe an ally with words like "annoying" or "in the way", audit the
  ledger before the dialogue**, because the complaint is almost always a cost and almost never
  a line.

## When not to use this

- **On a deliberate burden mechanic that is the game.** A design whose core is protecting a
  dependent — the dependent is the objective, not an ally — is a different contract, and the
  player has accepted it at the door. Even there, the dependent should be competent at
  something, or the protection reads as a chore. The same holds for a single escort event in a
  larger game: the escorted thing is declared as the event's objective, it is not a member of
  the crew, and a crew ally is never the thing escorted.
- **On a rival or neutral character.** Characters who are not on the player's side may cost the
  player freely; costing the player is part of what makes them opponents.
- **On temporary costs the player chose with full information.** A sacrifice the player elects —
  spending a resource to save an ally in a crisis — is a choice, not a tax. What fails is the
  cost the player never agreed to and pays every time.

## Evidence status

The claim that an ally must be cheated in the player's favour rests on a primary source: a
studio's published account of its companion AI, which makes the companion undetectable to
enemies outside combat and has her hand over supplies. It was re-read verbatim on 2026-10-06
as a chapter in a game-AI practitioner anthology. It gives the reason as the team's belief
that one sighting giving away the player would fracture the bond. That supports the
single-sabotage rule as a design judgement, not a measurement. The same chapter records that
rescuing the companion every time quickly became tedious, and that the team kept a rescue
requirement "intentionally and infrequently". That is the story-beat allowance above. A
second studio lead said in a preview that his game's companion never needs protecting because
that is not fun. A remake co-director of a game built around an escorted objective said the
opposite for that design: a dependent who takes care of themselves no longer feels protected.
That supports this technique's burden-mechanic exclusion rather than refuting it. The account of a companion redesigned
from leading and hurrying the player to following them, after playtesters disliked being nagged,
reaches the dossier only through a fan-wiki summary of developer commentary and is medium
confidence at best. That a single sabotage moment outweighs accumulated help is the dossier's
own inference stated as a rule, not a measured finding, and the six-way cost taxonomy is
practitioner synthesis. None of it has been tested in a played game of the kind this bundle
serves; the game the dossier was written for has not been played by anyone.
