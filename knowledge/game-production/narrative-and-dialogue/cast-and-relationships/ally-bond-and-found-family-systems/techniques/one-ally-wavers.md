# One ally wavers

The named concern: test the found family by having exactly one ally hesitate under pressure —
doubt, consider leaving, take an offer, freeze — and give the player the scene that decides
whether they come back. The group shows what it is by how it holds around the person who
slipped.

## Why one

A crew that is never tested is a roster: the player has no evidence that the bonds would hold,
so they are only assertions. A crew where every ally has a crisis is a queue of problems, and
the player becomes a counsellor processing them in turn. **One** waver is an event. The rest of
the crew responds to it — the loyal one who is angry, the quiet one who understands, the one who
goes after them — and each response characterises the responder more efficiently than their own
arc could. One waver tests the whole family; many wavers test only the individuals.

Two wavers in sequence also teach the player that the group is brittle, which retroactively
cheapens every bond, and two wavers at once compete for the same scene.

None is not safe either. A family that cannot come apart is a reward screen: the player has
been handed belonging rather than shown it held. The waver is also the strongest evidence the
game can offer that its bonds are not transactions — a vending machine cannot refuse, so an
ally who could have walked away, nearly did, and came back is proof the player was dealing with
a person all along.

## Choosing who wavers

The waverer is the ally with **the most to lose and a want the pressure can reach** — a family
outside the crew, a debt, a fear about the finale's cost, an old loyalty the antagonist or the
institution can invoke. The waver is motivated in the ally's own terms, so that the player
understands it even while resisting it. A waver the player cannot understand reads as the
writers needing a crisis.

Never choose the ally the player has invested in least, because a waver from someone the player
does not care about tests nothing. Prefer an ally the player has used and liked, whose waver will
be felt as a threat to something real.

## Shape of the waver

**Foreshadow it in witnessed steps.** The want the pressure reaches is visible earlier — a
line, a call home, a look at the offer — and the drift is staged as small acts the player sees
rather than as a reveal. A waver from nowhere feels like betrayal by the writers; a waver the
player watched approach, step by step, feels like something they could have prevented, which
is what gives the recovery scene its stakes.

**Show it in play before any line explains it.** The first sign is behavioural: the ally hangs
back, the help arrives a beat late, the call goes unanswered once. The player notices the
change in their hands and wonders, and the scene that names it lands on a question already
asked.

**Hang back, never harm.** The waverer withholds; the waverer does not sabotage. An ally who
crashes into the player or gives them away has stopped wavering and become an opponent, and
everything the subject protects about the player's ledger is lost with them.

**Make the pressure external.** The waver is caused by something done to the ally — a threat, an
offer, a loss — rather than by a sudden change of character. External pressure keeps the ally
sympathetic and points the player's anger at the source.

**Give the player the scene.** The player gets an active moment to bring the ally back: a
choice, a run, a confrontation, a gesture that draws on the bond. The bond's state is read here,
and it is one of the few readings that matters most.

**Never confiscate investment without a fight.** The waver may withhold the ally's help for a
stretch; it must not take away what the player paid into the ally without the player first
getting the scene. Losing an ally the player fought for and failed is a consequence; losing one
the player never had a chance to keep is a tax.

**Return changed.** An ally who comes back is not reset. They carry the waver — a debt to the
group, a sharper loyalty, a joke at their own expense — and the finale may call on exactly what
the waver taught them.

## When the player fails to bring them back

The branch where the ally leaves must be authored, not a hole. They may return late, at the
finale, without their task done; they may be absent and their task fall to a weaker answer; in
a story that can bear it they may end up on the other side. Each is a consequence the player can
trace to the bond, and the finale-first design should already know what happens to that
ally's task.

## Decision rules

- **When planning the middle act, pick one waverer and write the others' responses** to the
  waver before writing anyone else's personal crisis.
- **When the waver has no foreshadowing, add it in the act before**, as acts the player
  witnesses rather than information they are told.
- **When the waver's first sign is a line, move it into play** and let the line follow.
- **When a waver would have the ally harm the player, cut the harm**; withholding is the limit.
- **When the pressure on the waverer is internal, find the external hand on it** — usually the
  institution's — so the waver also serves the betrayal structure.
- **When the waver would remove the ally's help, time-box it and give the player the scene
  before the removal sticks.**
- **When the player fails to bring the ally back, the finale task must still resolve** in a way
  the player can read as the cost of that failure.

## When not to use this

- **In a game with one ally.** The waver of the only ally is the central relationship's crisis,
  which is a different structure; the one-in-a-group arithmetic does not apply.
- **When the betrayal plot already puts a friend under the institution's test.** The tested
  friend and the waverer are usually the same person, and splitting them makes two crises.

## Evidence status

The waver as a gameplay device — one ally hanging back under the antagonist's pressure, shown
in driving before any line, so allies are not vending machines and none sabotages the player —
is a design proposal in the dossiers, not a sourced pattern. Its supports are a
high-confidence pattern from prestige animated series that a found family must be able to come
apart, resting on creators' own statements, and a medium-high pattern about loyalty corroding
in witnessed steps drawn from a game's long arc via an encyclopaedia entry and a critical essay.
The "exactly one" arithmetic is practitioner judgement. Nothing has been tested in a played
game, and the game the dossiers serve has not been played by anyone.
