# The picture carries the card

The named concern: on a card with an image, the image is the primary channel and the text is
the second, and the two must carry different information. The eye reaches the picture first
and takes from it, in well under a second, everything that can be seen. Text that repeats it
arrives late with nothing new, and the card has spent its most expensive line on a duplicate.

## The division of labour

The picture carries **what is seen**: place, object, face, posture, weather, damage, who is
present and who is missing. The text carries **what cannot be seen**: what something cost,
who owes whom, what was said, what happens next, when time runs out, a name. The test is a
single question asked of every line: could a viewer have read this off the picture? If yes,
the line goes, unless one of the three exceptions below applies.

## When restating the picture is not waste

The duplicate test assumes a viewer who reads the picture fluently and can see it. Three
conditions break that assumption.

- **The still is ambiguous.** A covered car, an unnamed face or a pile of parts can be read
  more than one way. A few words that fix the reading (*your* car, *his* tag, *the
  courier's* gearbox) carry the one thing the picture cannot, which is which reading is meant. Keep the
  anchoring words and cut the description around them.
- **The reader is new to the picture.** Text that recaps a diagram is redundant for a reader
  who can already read the diagram. A novice may not understand the diagram without the text,
  and that is measured instructional research, not taste. A family room includes novices. The
  first card that shows a face or an object the player has not met may name it once, and
  later cards drop the name.
- **The reader cannot see the picture.** A player using screen narration gets only the text.
  A card meant to be narrated needs a separate description of what the picture shows, kept out
  of the visible lines. The wordless peak card needs it most, because without one it gives a
  narration user nothing at all.

The strongest pairing goes further than avoiding overlap: the text changes what the picture
shows. An image of a man smiling beside a car reads as a winner; the line "He sold the house
to build it" turns the smile into a bet. The picture was never wrong; the text gave it a
second meaning the eye could not supply. That is the target, and a card that reaches it reads
in one beat because the reader's eye returns to the picture to see it again, differently.

## At the peak, take the words out

The division of labour has a limit case, and it is the most important card in a sequence. At
the moment of highest emotional pressure — a loss, a betrayal, the end of a rivalry — words
invite the player to evaluate what the picture lets them feel, and the card is strongest with
one line or none. The silence is decided in advance and briefed into the picture; a wordless
card made by deleting lines from a full one shows its gaps, because the image was never asked
to carry the whole moment. Use it sparingly: a sequence where every card is silent has no
peak.

## Procedure

1. **Brief the picture before writing the final text,** and write the brief as what must be
   visible: the one object the card turns on, the face that must read, what must be absent.
   A picture briefed as a mood produces a mood, and the text is then forced to carry the
   facts the image declined to show.
2. **Write the text against the delivered picture, not against the brief.** Images drift from
   their brief. When the delivered image shows the damage, cut the line that described it;
   when it failed to show the object the turn depends on, either fix the image or move the
   object into the text.
3. **Run the duplicate test line by line.** Every line that a viewer could have read off the
   image is cut or replaced with something the image cannot say.
4. **Review the card rendered, at its real size, with its picture.** Not the text in a
   spreadsheet and the image in a folder.

## Why the review must be of the rendered card

A text review of a card judges the wrong artifact. It approves a line that duplicates the
picture, because without the picture the line looks informative, and it flags a line that
only makes sense with the picture, because without it the line looks cryptic. Both verdicts
invert on the real card. A card that has a picture, text in the right fields and a valid
layout has passed every structural check and still says nothing about whether the two
channels divide the work, which is
[structural proof is necessary and never sufficient](../../../../_laws.md#structural-proof-is-never-sufficient)
at the scale of one card: the perceptual rung — the card on the screen, seen as the player
will see it — is the only one that answers the question.

## Decision rules

- **When the picture is generic, the text must carry the specific, and the card is weaker for
  it.** Fix the picture first; text compensating for a vague image becomes a caption.
- **When the picture already shows the image the card turns on, drop the image line** rather
  than labelling the picture with it, and spend the freed line on the trade or leave it empty.
- **When history must reach the player, put it in an object the picture shows** — worn,
  damaged, given away, picked up again — rather than in a line of backstory. The object can
  carry a later callback; a line of lore cannot.
- **When the text contains an adjective that describes the image** — "battered", "lonely",
  "gleaming" — cut it. The image has already shown it, better.
- **When the card has no picture,** the first line does the picture's job and must be one
  visible noun, and the card loses a line for the trade and the turn. Budget accordingly.
- **When the same picture serves several cards** — a recurring portrait, a location plate —
  the text carries all of the difference between them, so each card's text must state what
  changed, never what is shown.
- **When the picture contains readable text** — a sign, a number, a name painted on a car —
  that text is part of the card's budget and must not be repeated below it.
- **When a picture is reworked,** re-read every card and callback it serves. A picture can
  carry a seed or a payment that no line mentions, and a new composition can delete it without
  any text changing.

## When not to use this

- **On cards where the picture is decoration,** such as a branded backdrop behind a rules
  card. There is no second channel to divide labour with; write the text as a standalone line.
- **When the image is unknown at authoring time** and will be selected at runtime from a
  pool. The text must then stand alone and avoid both duplicating and contradicting any image
  in the pool, which is a different and more conservative discipline.

## Evidence status

The division between what is seen and what is said is long-standing craft in comics (caption
versus panel), film title cards and picture books. It is consistent with instructional research
in which a diagram alone beat a diagram plus text that recapped it. The same research conditions
that result: a less experienced reader could not understand the diagram without the text (the
expertise reversal effect; primary, peer-reviewed). No effect size is relied on here. The
narration exception comes from a platform holder's accessibility guidelines, which ask that
non-decorative images carry a description read aloud by narration (primary). The training-data
lane reached the same two conditions independently, through redundancy boundary conditions and
the anchoring function of a caption. The rework rule comes from one game's shipped panels. There,
an art rework removed a seed from a picture while the picture that pays it kept the payment. The
peak-silence rule rests on animation directors speaking first-hand about wordless sequences
(primary, high confidence for the interviews read in full, medium for one quote known only
from a search excerpt). The object-carries-history rule draws on readings of animated series
that the collecting research partly marks as its own interpretation (medium). The rule that
review must happen on the rendered card follows from the bundle's law on perceptual evidence.
No card has yet been judged by a player.
