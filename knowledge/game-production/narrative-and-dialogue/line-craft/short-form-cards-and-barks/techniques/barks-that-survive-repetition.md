# Barks that survive repetition

The named concern: a bark's quality is decided on its tenth hearing, not its first, and the
writer controls how a line ages by matching its specificity to how often it will fire. This is
a writing concern before it is a selection concern. Selection machinery reduces how often a
line repeats; it cannot rescue a line that was never fit to be heard twice.

## Specificity is inverse to frequency

Lines wear out at different rates according to what they carry. A **specific** line — a pun, a
reference, a fresh observation, a named detail — delivers its value once, as surprise, and the
second hearing has nothing left to deliver except recognition that the game has run out of
things to say. An **attitudinal** line — a rhythm, a stance, a relationship voiced in a few
words — delivers its value as recognition, and recognition is what repetition produces. A
player who hears a rival's dry "Again?" for the eighth time hears the rival; a player who hears
a clever pun for the eighth time hears the pool.

So the rule is: **the more often a line fires, the less specific it may be.** Common events —
an overtake, a bump, a lap completed — get short attitudinal lines in a pool large enough to
vary. Rare events — a first win, a comeback from last, a crash on the final corner — get the
specific lines, because they will be heard fresh. Each pool may keep one standout, weighted
rare and given a long cooldown, because the memorable line is the one that wears out first.
The naive pool does the reverse: the writer spends the best jokes on the events they imagine
most vividly, which are the common ones.

There is one way to keep a specific *kind* of line on a frequent event: make the habit the
character. A rival defined by terrible puns can pun on every overtake, because the repetition
now reads as who he is rather than as the pool running dry. The device works for one character
in a cast; given to two, it is a house style, and the pool is back to wearing out.

## Variants differ in angle, not in wording

A pool of five synonyms — "watch out", "look out", "careful", "heads up", "mind yourself" — is
heard as one line said five times. Variants earn their place by taking a different angle on
the event: one notices the damage, one the rival, one the money, one says nothing but a
grunt. Vary length as well, so that repeats do not share a rhythm — a pool where every line is
eight words long has a cadence the ear learns in a race. And keep phrases out of more than one
pool: a distinctive phrase that two characters share, or that one character uses for two
events, is a repeat the selection machinery cannot see.

## Estimate the frequency, with its basis

A writer cannot apply the rule without a number, and the number must carry its unit and basis:
expected fires **per session**, under a **stated session length and play pattern**, for a
stated event. "Common" means nothing; "about forty hearings in a ninety-minute evening of four
races per hour, after the selection gates" is a number a writer can write to, and a
selection-table owner can check against what the game actually logs —
[a number carries its unit and its basis](../../../../_laws.md#a-number-carries-its-unit-and-basis).
The frequency comes from the condition table that fires the line; this technique consumes it.

Count **hearings, not events.** Where cooldowns, gaps and per-race caps already exist, the
player hears only a fraction of the events that could have fired a line. In one measured
racing campaign it was about one hearing in five and a half events, so a pool sized to the
event count would have been five times too large. Wear follows the heard count. Before the
gates exist, count events and label the figure as an upper bound.

From the frequency follow two figures: how many variants the pool needs so that any one line
recurs rarely enough to stay tolerable, and how specific each variant may be. A pool for a
forty-fire event needs more variants than a pool for a four-fire event, and its variants must
be plainer. When the arithmetic says the pool cannot be made large enough, prefer silence to a
repeat **of a rare-band line**. A missing line costs less than a repeated joke, because a
repeated joke tells the player the world is a recording. The rule does not extend to plain
attitudinal lines, which are written to be recognised and may repeat on their band's horizon.
Applied to every line, it silences most of a cast: in one measured campaign, a rule of no
line heard twice in an evening would have cut three barks in four.

## Procedure

1. **Get or estimate fires per session for each event**, with the session assumption written
   next to the number. Use hearings after the selection gates where the gates exist, and
   event counts, labelled as an upper bound, where they do not.
2. **Sort events into frequency bands** and assign each band its specificity ceiling: plain
   attitude for the most frequent, specific observation for the rare, unique lines for events
   that occur once.
3. **Write the pool to its band,** each variant from a different angle, at varied lengths, with
   at most one rare standout. Move any specific joke written for a frequent event to a rarer
   one, or cut it.
4. **Check the phrase ledger** so no distinctive phrase is shared across characters or pools.
5. **Run the repetition read:** read the frequent pool aloud, in random order with repeats, as
   many times as a session would fire it. Mark every line that grates. Grating on repeat is a
   finding about the line, not the listener.
6. **Hand the pool and its bands to the selection owner,** who sets the no-repeat window and
   cooldowns. The line's band tells them which lines may never repeat within a session.

## Decision rules

- **When a line is memorable, it is rare-band.** Memorable is the property that makes a line
  wear out.
- **When a line names a specific number, place or name, it is rare-band** unless that detail is
  the character's fixed attitude.
- **When a pool for a frequent event is short, write plainer lines, not more lines of the same
  kind.** Ten clever lines repeat their cleverness ten times faster than three plain ones wear.
- **When a taunt targets the player, keep frequent taunts about the act and rare taunts about
  the history.** A taunt about an old defeat that fires every race stops stinging and starts
  nagging; a frequent taunt about losing is the line players mute first.
- **When repetition is reported as annoying in play, check the band before adding variants.**
  Adding variants to a pool of specific lines multiplies the problem.

## When not to use this

- **On lines that fire once by construction** — scripted beats, unique story cards. They have no
  repetition to survive and should be as specific as the moment deserves.
- **On system callouts,** where identical repetition is the point.

## Evidence status

The inverse relation between specificity and wear is stated first-hand by game writers in
trade press, including the observation that firing frequency decides how fast a bark wears out
(primary, high confidence). The "repeat only after twenty or thirty loops" practice and the
volume-over-placement approach come from a game director's interviews (primary, high). The
standout-per-pool, length-variety and synonym-pool rules come from the research behind this
subject, rated high for the system and medium for its pool sizes. The report that players of a
racing game modded out a repetitive announcer comes from community sources (low). The band
procedure and the repetition read are this subject's own codification. A headless census of
one racing campaign's real line selector supplies the measured parts. It ran three sittings
of 34 races with an AI stand-in for the player. The census found that most hearings were
repeats, that the punchline lines were heard most, and that the hearings-versus-events ratio
and the cost of a blanket no-repeat rule are as stated above. It is one game with one
driver model, so the ratios belong to that game. No line has been judged by a player on a
sofa.
