# A bark carries character in one breath

The named concern: a bark is one or two sentences spoken in reaction to an event, and in that
space it carries exactly two things — **one fact the player cannot see, and one point of
view**. The event itself is already on screen; the player saw the crash. A bark that reports
it is a play-by-play caller on a loop. What the player cannot see is what the event means to
this particular person, and sometimes a fact only that person would know: who is coming up
behind, what the rival intends, what the last result cost. That is all a bark has room to say.

## What carries character at this size

At bark length there is no room for exposition, history or arc, so character is carried by
three things only. **What the character notices** — two drivers watching the same crash
remark on different things: one on the car, one on the driver, one on the money. **How they
say it** — rhythm, sentence length, the field their words come from, whether they finish
their sentences, what they call the player. **Whom they say it to** — the player, a teammate,
themselves, nobody. A bark that varies none of these is generic, and the test is mechanical:
cover the speaker's name and ask whether a reader could say who spoke. If the line could be
reassigned to any member of the cast without a rewrite, it carries no character.

Character is not carried by the character's name, a catchphrase, or a mood label. A
catchphrase is a single line repeated; it becomes the only thing the character says and the
first thing a player mutes. A mood announcement ("I'm furious!") states what a bark should
perform. Cleverness is not character either: a pun or a flourish that makes the line
memorable also makes it slower to parse, and at speed it buries the one fact the player
needed.

## The breath as the unit

A bark is said in one exhalation, which is a length budget written in the body: a short
sentence, or two very short ones, said without a pause to inhale. The breath is a better unit
than a word count because it also governs rhythm — a line that needs a breath in the middle
has two ideas in it and should be two barks or one.

Where the player's eyes are sets how much of the breath may be used. **Mid-action**, a bark is
heard over engine noise by a player watching the road, and it must land in well under the
length of a breath — a handful of words, a couple of seconds spoken. **Between rounds**, with
the player's attention free, a bark may use the whole breath. One budget for both produces
mid-action barks that are still talking when the next event fires.

Front-load the meaning. The first few words are the ones that get through noise, and a bark
may be cut off by a higher-priority line or the end of the round. "Behind you — and he's
angry" survives being cut after two words; "You know, I think he's coming up behind you"
survives nothing. A bark either makes sense when cut off halfway or has a deliberately short
variant for the moments when it will be.

State the budget as the intended size of the bark, not as a ceiling, because a generator or
a writer handed "up to twenty words" writes nineteen-word barks:
[a budget shapes the output, it does not only cap it](../../../../_laws.md#a-budget-shapes-the-output).
"One breath, meaning in the first three words, shorter mid-action" produces different lines
from "maximum twenty words", and the difference is visible across the whole pool.

## Procedure

1. **Write the character's angle on the event before writing any line.** One sentence, not
   for shipping: what does this person notice when this happens, and what do they want?
2. **Name the one fact the bark gives the player**, or decide deliberately that it carries
   only attitude. A bark with two facts is two barks.
3. **Write the bark from the angle, not from the event.** A bark written from the event
   describes it; a bark written from the angle reacts to it.
4. **Say it aloud in one breath.** If it needs a second breath, cut it or split it.
5. **Move the meaning to the front** and check that the line survives being cut off.
6. **Run the covered-name test** against two other members of the cast. A bark that survives
   being reassigned is rewritten.

## Decision rules

- **When two characters' barks for the same event could be swapped, the fix is the angle, not
  the wording.** Changing words while keeping the observation produces a synonym, not a second
  voice.
- **When a bark explains the event, cut the explanation.** The screen showed it.
- **When a taunt is aimed at the player, make it about what the player just did, in the
  rival's terms,** and never sneer at the player for failing. A generic insult is a sound
  effect; a taunt that mocks a failure the player already feels charges them twice, and on
  repeat it is the line players mute. A taunt that names the player's move as the rival sees
  it is a relationship.
- **When a bark must carry information** — a warning, a hint — put the information in the first
  words and the character in the rhythm; information buried after the attitude is lost.
- **When a line is voiced,** spell out numbers, avoid words whose pronunciation depends on
  meaning, and keep hesitation marks to one, because each of them is a place where a voice —
  recorded or synthesised — can go wrong.

## When not to use this

- **On system callouts** that must be identical every time, such as a lap count or a countdown.
  These are interface sounds in a voice, and character in them costs clarity.
- **On a narrator who is deliberately neutral.** A neutral voice is a choice; giving it an angle
  turns it into a character, which may not be wanted.

## Evidence status

The one-fact rule comes from game writers quoted first-hand in trade press (primary, high
confidence), as does the warning that cleverness makes barks less useful. The covered-name
test and the levers of voice draw on a screenwriting authority known here through summaries
(secondary). The breath as a unit of roughly six to fourteen words, and the short mid-action
figure of five to eight words or under two seconds, are working heuristics from the research
behind this subject and from voice-synthesis vendor guidance, not measured optima; the
cut-off rule and the no-sneering rule come from a talk on dynamic dialogue and a practitioner's
newsletter, the second weaker. None of this has been tested in a played game yet.
