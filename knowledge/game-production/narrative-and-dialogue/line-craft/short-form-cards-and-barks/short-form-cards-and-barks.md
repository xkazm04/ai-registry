# Short-form cards and barks

Most of the story a player actually receives arrives in units too small to be called scenes.
A card shown between two rounds: one picture, three lines. A rival's sentence shouted as he
passes. A taunt on the results screen. A line that, forty minutes later, turns out to have
been a promise. These are the smallest units of game story, and they carry more of it than
the cutscenes do, because they are where the player already is: between plays, inside play,
on the screen they look at while the next thing loads.

This subject owns the writing of those units. It is a craft of compression with a twist that
prose craft does not prepare a writer for: each unit is judged on a clock the writer does not
control. A card is read once, in the few seconds the player grants it, by a room rather than a
reader. A bark is heard over and over, by a player whose eyes are on something else. A line
that is excellent on the page fails both clocks routinely, and the failure is invisible in any
review that reads the text once, at a desk, at arm's length.

## Two units, two clocks

The **card** is a still moment: an image and at most three short lines, shown at a seam of
play — after a race, before an unlock, at a turn in a rivalry. Its clock is the glance. The
player has finished one thing and wants the next; the card has the interval between the two
and not a second more, and it shares that interval with a picture that the eye reaches first.

The **bark** is a moving moment: one or two sentences spoken by a character in reaction to
something that just happened — a crash, an overtake, a near miss, a win. Its clock is the
repeat. A bark that fires on a common event will be heard dozens of times in an evening, and
its quality is decided on the tenth hearing, not the first. The taunt is a bark with an
addressee: the line a rival aims at the player, which must sting without being a joke the
player can see coming.

The two units fail in opposite directions, which is why a single standard for "short game
text" serves neither. A card fails by saying too much: it explains the picture, names the
emotion, and spends its last line on a moral. A bark fails by saying something specific: a
pun, a reference, a fresh observation that is delightful once and grating on its fifth
outing. The card must earn its three lines by doing what the image cannot; the bark must earn
its repetition by carrying attitude rather than content.

Both units share one obligation that long forms can defer: **each pays inside itself.** A
card that only sets up a later card is a trailer, and a player who meets a trailer between
every round learns that the cards promise and never deliver. The reward — a turn, a price, a
point of view the player did not have — arrives inside the unit, even when the unit also
plants something for later.

## The card is image, trade, then a turn or a price

A card that works has a shape, and the shape is small enough to state as a rule. The first
line, or the picture itself, gives **one concrete image** — not a setting but a thing: the
borrowed jacket, the empty grandstand, the debt written on a napkin. The second line states
the **trade**: what someone gives, owes, wants or risks, in plain terms. The last line is a
**turn or a price**: either the fact that changes what the trade means, or what it will cost.
A card that ends on a price makes the next race matter; a card that ends on a turn makes the
last one mean something different. See
[three-line-card-image-trade-turn](./techniques/three-line-card-image-trade-turn.md).

The naive card has the same three lines and none of the shape: a sentence of scene-setting, a
sentence of feeling, and a sentence of summary. It reads smoothly and moves nothing, because
none of its lines is a fact the player did not already have.

## The picture carries the card

On a card, text is the second channel, and the most common failure is to spend it repeating
the first. A picture of a wrecked car beside a line saying the car is wrecked has wasted its
best line. The division of labour is a rule: the picture carries what is seen — place,
object, face, condition — and the text carries what cannot be seen — what it cost, who is
owed, what was said, when it ends. When the image changes, the text is rewritten, because a
card's text is written against its picture and not beside it. The rule reaches its limit at
the emotional peak, where the strongest card often has fewer lines or none: words invite the
player to evaluate a moment that the picture alone lets them feel, and the silence is planned,
not left over. This also decides how a card is reviewed: a card judged as text alone passes lines that duplicate the image and fails lines
that only make sense with it, which is exactly backwards. See
[picture-carries-the-card](./techniques/picture-carries-the-card.md).

## A bark carries character in one breath

A bark has room for exactly two things: one fact and one point of view. The fact is
something the player cannot see for themselves — a threat behind, a rival's intent, what the
last result cost — because a bark that reports the event on screen is a play-by-play caller
on a loop. The point of view is the character: not the character's name, not a catchphrase,
not an announcement of mood, but their way of seeing what just happened. Two racers watching
the same crash should produce barks that could not be swapped, because each notices something
different and says it in a different rhythm. The length is set by the breath and by where the
player's eyes are: a bark is said in one exhalation, front-loaded so that its first few words
carry its meaning, and a bark that plays mid-action is shorter again than one that plays
between rounds, because mid-action it is heard over engine noise by a player watching the
road. See
[bark-carries-character-in-one-breath](./techniques/bark-carries-character-in-one-breath.md).

## A bark must survive its own repetition

The rule that governs the bark pool is inverse: **the more often a line fires, the less
specific it may be.** A line that fires on every overtake carries attitude, rhythm and
relationship — things a listener enjoys recognising. A line that fires once in a season may
carry the precise joke, the reference, the named detail, because it will only ever be heard
fresh. A writer who assigns the best specific jokes to the most common events has written a
pool that is funniest in the first five minutes and worst for the rest of the evening. Variants
must differ in angle, not wording — five synonyms for "watch out" are one line heard five
times — and a repeat punctures the illusion of an attentive world faster than a silence does.
Selection machinery — cooldowns, no-repeat windows, variant weights — reduces how often a
line repeats; it cannot rescue a line that was never fit to repeat. See
[barks-that-survive-repetition](./techniques/barks-that-survive-repetition.md).

## A callback pays a seed

The cheapest depth available to short units is the callback: a later card or bark that
returns to a concrete detail planted earlier and changes what it means. A seed is a specific,
noticeable, unexplained detail — at its best one with an innocent first reading that hindsight
turns over; a callback is a later line that pays it, and the payment is a change of meaning,
not a repetition. A callback that means what the seed meant is a wink; one that has changed is
an arc. Because short units are scattered across play and many
fire only under conditions, a callback is also a correctness problem: a callback whose seed
the player never saw is a line that refers to nothing. Seeds and callbacks are therefore kept
as a ledger, audited in both directions — callbacks with no reachable seed, and seeds that no
callback ever pays. See [callback-pays-a-seed](./techniques/callback-pays-a-seed.md).

## The television sets the budget

A television at sofa distance is the most expensive surface text can appear on. The type is
large, so few characters fit on a line; the room reads at the pace of its slowest reader, who
in family play may be a child or the adult reading aloud to one; and a card that disappears
on a timer is unreadable in a way that a card on a desk monitor never is. So the budget is
stated before writing, per unit class, in a declared unit with its basis: characters per
line at a stated type size, lines per card, words per bark, seconds of dwell. The budget is an
instruction about the size of the thing, not a ceiling discovered at layout, and every unit
is read aloud before it ships, because read-aloud is how a household will meet it. Where a
line is both voiced and captioned, the caption matches the audio word for word; a caption that
paraphrases its own audio makes the room hear one line and read another. See
[television-read-budget](./techniques/television-read-budget.md).

## Stock phrases are banned by list, and the list is not enough

Short units attract stock phrases because stock phrases are dense: "little did they know",
"the stakes have never been higher", "only one can win", "this changes everything". Each one
reads as story and carries none, and a generator asked for dramatic short text converges on
them reliably. The ban list therefore holds three kinds of entry, not one: **phrases**, the
fixed clichés; **constructions**, the shapes that fake a revelation or a cadence whatever words
fill them — "it's not X, it's Y", the mirrored pair, the closing generalisation about life or
fate, a rule-of-three on every line; and **interior-state words** that narrate what a character
feels or realises instead of giving the fact that would make the player feel it. Run the list
as a check on every stored unit, let a hit reject the line unless a person overrides it with a
written reason, and keep the list and the check in one place so the style guide and the gate
cannot drift apart. Then know what it does not catch: a writer or generator steered off a
banned phrase reaches for its nearest synonym, and the synonym is just as empty. The list
removes the worst offenders; the shape of the card and the specificity of the image are what
replace them.

## Failure modes of the naive reading

The **captioned picture** describes the image under it, spending the card's most valuable
line on information the eye already took. The **trailer card** sets up and never pays. The **mood card** names a feeling ("tension is
high") instead of stating a fact that would cause one. The **moral ending** spends the last
line on a lesson, so the card closes instead of opening the next race. The **one-time bark on
a common event** is a fresh joke wired to an overtake, worn out by the second race. The
**generic bark** could be said by any character, because it reports the event rather than a
point of view, and the **clever bark** buries its one useful fact under flavour. The **orphan callback** refers to a seed the player's path never showed them.
And the **desk review** approves every one of these, because it reads each unit once, at arm's
length, without the picture and without the hundredth repeat — the conditions under which no
player will ever meet them.

## Boundaries

**Against short-form-narrative-structure (in the media-generation bundle).** That subject owns
a continuous piece of factual video — a schedule of curiosity debts opened, carried and paid
over thirty seconds to eighteen minutes, held together by causal links between beats, for a
viewer who can leave at any moment. This subject owns discrete units dropped into play, each
complete on its own, whose reader is not deciding whether to stay but is busy with a game. The
rule for picking: if the text is a runtime the audience watches from end to end, and its job
is to keep them watching, it belongs to the neighbour; if it is a unit that must land in one
glance or survive its hundredth hearing, it belongs here. The neighbour's causal grammar does
reach inside a card — trade then turn is a three-line "but" — and is not restated.

**Against condition-tagged-line-tables.** The sibling owns the table that decides which line
fires: the tags, the game-state conditions, the weights, the cooldowns, the fallbacks when
nothing matches. This subject owns the line itself and whether it deserves to fire. If the
question is "which line plays now", it is the sibling's; if it is "is this line good enough
to play now, and the fiftieth time", it is this one. Expected firing frequency is the seam:
the table produces it, and this subject writes to it.

**Against subtext-and-voice-differentiation.** The sibling owns how a cast sounds distinct
across scenes and what characters leave unsaid; it establishes a voice. This subject spends
that voice under a budget of one breath or three lines. When a bark could belong to anyone,
the defect is usually upstream — the voice was never differentiated — and the fix belongs to
the sibling; when the voice is clear in scenes and lost in barks, the compression failed, and
the fix is here.

**Against the neighbouring budget and voice subjects.** The cost side of a line — how many
translation units a pool creates, what a recorded variant costs — belongs to the text-budget
technique of branching-narrative-graph-validation and to game-dialogue-voice-pipeline. This
subject's budget is about the reader on the sofa, not the localisation invoice; the two are
stated separately because they are counted in different units and neither implies the other.
