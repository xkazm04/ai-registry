# Three-line story cards for a couch racing campaign — the Death Ride card format

*Resolved 2026-10-04 against the `firetv-deathride` tree (branch `deathride/main`, tip
`6efb1dda`). Paths below are root-relative to that tree. Every cited line was re-opened and
re-read on that date.*

Death Ride is a vehicular-combat racing campaign for Fire TV, played on a television from the
sofa with phones as controllers. Its story is delivered almost entirely in short units: a card
before and after each of roughly thirty-five races, rival taunts, an announcer, and ending
cards for beaten rivals. The card format is not yet built or played; what exists is research.
Two dossiers written for the game define the card, and they define it slightly differently,
which is why it is worth reading them side by side. Nothing here has been tested in play.

## The format as the dossiers state it

The prestige-series dossier turns its short-form pattern into a three-line rule with one job
per line:

- `docs/narrative/research/R1-prestige-series-craft.md:313 "a place or object, concrete, readable at TV distance"`
- `docs/narrative/research/R1-prestige-series-craft.md:314 "what someone wants from someone else, in the present. Not backstory."`
- `docs/narrative/research/R1-prestige-series-craft.md:315 "the thing that is now different. Never a feeling named outright."`
- `docs/narrative/research/R1-prestige-series-craft.md:316 "No line explains a line above it."`

Its worked card ends on a clean turn —
`docs/narrative/research/R1-prestige-series-craft.md:323 "You sign; the engine sounds the same, and isn't yours."`
— and its source pattern requires the reward inside the piece:
`docs/narrative/research/R1-prestige-series-craft.md:233 "start from a single image that holds the conflict"`
(rated High; primary interviews with a short-film director, sources 48 and 49 of that dossier,
https://80.lv/articles/alberto-mielgo-talks-specifics-of-creating-love-death-robots-short-episodes).
The trade line descends from that dossier's dialogue-as-trade pattern, quoted from a
showrunner first-hand (Collider, https://collider.com/castlevania-season-3-warren-ellis-kevin-kolde-interview/).

The dialogue dossier states the same card as situation, pressure and hook:

- `docs/narrative/research/R3-dialogue-craft.md:171 "a noun you could photograph."`
- `docs/narrative/research/R3-dialogue-craft.md:173 "aimed at the player, and ideally the shortest line."`
- `docs/narrative/research/R3-dialogue-craft.md:175 "Keep each line to about 12 words or fewer. Don't start two lines the same way, and never end on a moral."`
- `docs/narrative/research/R3-dialogue-craft.md:177 "Three lines of mood"` — the named naive failure.

It rates its own card rule honestly:
`docs/narrative/research/R3-dialogue-craft.md:184 "This is a common heuristic, reasoned from [8]."`
Source [8] is a dramatist's memo to a writers' room
(https://nofilmschool.com/2010/10/david-mamet-drama-a-memo-the-unit-writers).

## Reconciliation against the technique

**Confirmed.** One concrete image, a present-tense trade, a last line that turns or charges,
no moral ending, no line explaining another, and a reward inside the card. The dossier's tight
announcer line is a three-beat card in miniature:
`docs/narrative/research/R3-dialogue-craft.md:47 "Six cars. Four laps. One of them owes money."`

**Upward lessons taken into the technique.** "Not backstory" on the trade line (R1:314); "no
line explains a line above it" (R1:316); the dozen-word line, the shortest-last line and no
repeated openings (R3:173, R3:175); and the ban list's shift from phrases to interior-state
words (R1:316 bans "feel", "realise", "remember", "destiny", "betrayal") and to constructions,
taken from the dialogue dossier's blacklist —
`docs/narrative/research/R3-dialogue-craft.md:247 "It's not X, it's Y"` is the best-known
measured construction (corpus studies cited there as [9], [10], [11]:
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing,
https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15061v2, https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509) — and the override
discipline:
`docs/narrative/research/R3-dialogue-craft.md:380 "A hit rejects the line. A human may override it with a written reason."`

**Deviation 1 — the picture as caption.** The card rule says
`docs/narrative/research/R1-prestige-series-craft.md:317 "the text is a caption"`
and goes on to say the prose "only labels" the image. That falls short of the standard: a
label repeats what the eye already took. The same dossier's line 1 asks the text to name the
image while the art also shows it, so a Death Ride card built literally to R1 would spend its
first line duplicating its picture. The technique keeps the stricter rule — the picture
carries the image and the freed line goes to the trade (picture-carries-the-card).

**Deviation 2 — two shapes for one card.** R1 ends on "turn or price"; R3 puts pressure on
line 2 and a player-aimed hook on line 3. The R3 hook drifts toward a question for the race
to answer (R3:175, "Leave a question for the race to answer"), which, done carelessly, is the
trailer card. The technique resolves it by situation: before a race, end on a price; after a
race, end on a turn; either way, pay inside the card.

**Deviation 3 — the line count.** The game-narrative dossier proposes a shorter pre-race card,
`docs/narrative/research/R2-game-narrative-craft.md:234 "one card of 1–2 TV-legible lines"`,
against three lines in R1 and R3. Not a contradiction under the technique: three is the
ceiling, and a card whose picture carries the image needs only two.

**Status.** All three dossiers mark their game-specific proposals as inference —
`docs/narrative/research/R1-prestige-series-craft.md:367 "are design proposals inferred from the patterns, not facts."`
No Death Ride card has been rendered on a television or read aloud from a sofa.
