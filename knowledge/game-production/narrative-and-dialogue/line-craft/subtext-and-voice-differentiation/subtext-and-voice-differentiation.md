# Subtext and voice differentiation

Dialogue is the only part of a game in which characters act on each other with nothing but
words, and it is judged by a test a reader applies without knowing it: do these sound like
different people who want different things, or like one writer talking to themselves in
several fonts? The second kind of dialogue is grammatical, informative and on tone, and it
is the default output of every hurried writer and every unbriefed generator. This subject
owns the craft that produces the first kind.

It owns six properties of a line and one instrument. The properties: the line means more
than it says, and says it in few words; the speaker is identifiable without the name tag, by
rhythm, vocabulary and above all by what they will not say; the exchange is a trade between
two people who want something from each other, not a lecture with a listener; information
arrives because somebody uses it as a weapon or pays for it; threat sounds dangerous rather
than performed; and warmth and humour exist in a grim world as things the characters do to
survive it. The instrument is a voice bible per speaker, which turns all six from taste into
something a writer, a reviewer and a generator can be held to.

None of this is decoration on top of correct dialogue. A line that names its feeling, could
be spoken by anyone, and informs a listener who wants nothing is grammatical and on topic,
and it is still placeholder work: a lead would hand it back. That is
[grade against what ships, not on a curve](../../../_laws.md#grade-against-what-ships-not-on-a-curve)
at line scale, and it is why the properties here are acceptance criteria rather than polish.

## The unsaid is the content

A line of dialogue has two layers: what is said, and what the speaker is doing by saying it.
The second layer is the scene. A mechanic who says "the left rear will hold" to a driver he
has just watched crash twice is not reporting on a tyre; he is deciding not to say what he
thinks, and the player hears both the report and the decision. Write the decision into the
words and the line goes dead, because there is nothing left for the listener to do.

The naive reading treats subtext as vagueness — lines made oblique so they sound deep. That
fails the other way: a listener cannot infer what the scene never gave them the material to
infer, and an unreadable line is not subtle, it is noise. Subtext is a calculation the
listener completes. It needs a known want, a known pressure, and a line that visibly steps
around the obvious statement of either. Few words are the tool rather than the goal: the
shorter the line, the more of its meaning must be carried by context, and the more context
the scene must have planted before it. So a per-line word budget belongs in the brief, as an
instruction about what kind of line is wanted, rather than in a trim afterwards, which is
[a budget shapes the output, it does not only cap it](../../../_laws.md#a-budget-shapes-the-output).
In an interactive scene the menu carries subtext too: which replies are offered, which are
missing and which loop back say what the player character would and would not ask. See
[subtext-in-few-words](./techniques/subtext-in-few-words.md).

## A voice is a pattern of choices under pressure

The costume reading of voice is the most common and the least effective: give each
character an accent spelled phonetically, a catchphrase and a favourite oath, and call them
differentiated. Strip the costume and every one of them builds sentences the same way, asks
the same kind of question and concedes at the same moment. A reader feels the sameness even
when they cannot name it.

A voice that survives the costume test is built from structure. **Rhythm** — sentence
length and how it varies, whether the speaker finishes thoughts or abandons them, whether
they answer questions with statements or with other questions. **Vocabulary** — the domain
the words come from (a trade, a faith, a ledger), the register, the words they reach for
when angry. And **refusal** — the topics they will not raise, the admission they will not
make, the word they will not use, how they say no. Refusal is the strongest marker of the
three because it is invisible in any single line and unmistakable across twenty: a
character who never once says what they feel, or never once says the dead man's name, is
recognisable by the shape of the hole. Voices are lost first where they matter most: at the
peaks, where every character drifts into the same articulate emotional summary, although a
frightened person loses syntax rather than gaining it. The test is mechanical and cheap:
strip the speaker tags from a scene and ask a reader to attribute every line, and treat a
cast whose distinctness was never tested this way as untested rather than distinct, which is
[unmeasured is not a pass](../../../_laws.md#unmeasured-is-not-a-pass). See
[distinguish-by-rhythm-and-refusal](./techniques/distinguish-by-rhythm-and-refusal.md).

## Every exchange is a trade

Two characters in a scene each want something from the other — information, permission,
forgiveness, time, a lower price, a confession — and each line is a move in that negotiation:
an offer, a refusal, a counter, a concession, a bluff. When the scene ends, the balance
between them has changed, and the player can say how. Before writing, each side's want, what happens to them if they do
not get it, and why it must be settled now are stated in a line each; and once the trade
settles the scene ends, because a conversation that outstays its trade becomes a lecture.

The naive scene is a lecture: one character holds the information, the other asks the
question that releases the next paragraph. "What do you mean?" "Go on." "And then?" Every
such line could be deleted without loss, because the listener wants nothing and risks
nothing. Games are especially prone to it, because the player's dialogue options default to
prompts for the next block of exposition. Treat each option instead as a move with a price:
the player gives something — candour, a favour, a concession, face — to get something, and
the character answering has a reason to give less than was asked. See
[dialogue-as-a-trade](./techniques/dialogue-as-a-trade.md).

## Exposition rides the conflict

A world has to be explained, and the explanation has to arrive somewhere. The craft rule is
that a fact is spoken only when a character has a reason to say it to someone who has a
reason not to want it: as ammunition in an argument, as the price of a favour, as an
accusation, as the thing one person knows and the other is paying to learn. Delivered that
way the fact is remembered, because it arrived attached to a stake. Delivered as a briefing,
between two characters who both already know it, it is the oldest failure in dialogue and
the reader skips it. A second rule governs the dose: a scene carries one new fact the player
needs, delivered after the player has been made to want it, and the rest of the world is
withheld until a later conflict needs it. See
[exposition-inside-conflict](./techniques/exposition-inside-conflict.md).

## Menace is quiet, specific and conditional

Threat on the page fails toward parody faster than any other register. The tells are well
known — the announced threat, the laugh, the adjectives of evil, the explanation of the plan,
escalation by volume — and each one tells the listener the speaker needs to be believed,
which is the opposite of dangerous. The menacing line works the other way. It is ordinary in
its surface, specific in its detail (the speaker knows the listener's sister's shift times),
conditional in its form (a term, not a promise of pain), and it leaves the consequence
unsaid, because the listener's imagination is the more frightening writer. Menace lives in
any character who threatens — an enforcer, a rival, a creditor, an ally pushed too far — and
it can coexist with humour: talk about the kettle while the danger sits unnamed in the room
is a long and effective tradition. See [menace-without-parody](./techniques/menace-without-parody.md).

## Warmth is a survival behaviour, not a tonal break

A grim world written entirely grim is monotonous, and the player stops feeling it within an
hour. The naive cure is relief scheduled by the writer: a comic sidekick, a quip after every
hard beat. That cure is worse than the disease, because a quip that undercuts a loss tells
the player the story does not believe its own stakes.

Warmth that works is something the characters do, for reasons of their own. People in hard
places joke because joking is how they stay upright; they tease the ones they love because
saying it plainly costs too much; they share the last of something without remarking on it.
Humour therefore belongs to specific characters as part of their voice, aimed at specific
targets, and it is rationed: after a real loss, at least one beat sits unjoked, and the
first person to joke afterwards is making a choice the scene can see. A game adds one rule
the page does not need, because the player is in the scene: humour is shared with the
player and never aimed at their failure, since a reactive line that mocks a crash sounds like
the game mocking the person holding the controller. See
[warmth-in-a-grim-world](./techniques/warmth-in-a-grim-world.md).

## The voice bible is the instrument

Every property above is a judgement, and judgements drift: across writers, across months,
and across generations of a model that was never told the difference between two of the
cast. The voice bible makes them a reference. One entry per speaker: what they want and what
they will not admit; their rhythm stated in terms a reviewer can check; their vocabulary
domain and the words they never use; how they refuse, threaten, joke and show affection; how
their register changes toward each person they talk to; what pressure does to them; and
reference lines written or chosen by a person — on voice, each paired with the plausible line
they would never say. The two halves serve different readers. A generator steers best from
positive rows with their reasons and from the on-voice lines; the off-voice line serves the
reviewer, for whom it is the most useful row in the document, because it is usually exactly
what the generator wrote.

A voice bible counts only if it is consumed. A beautifully written bible that no authoring
prompt carries and no review consults is
[a declared input nothing reads](../../../_laws.md#declaring-an-input-is-not-consuming-it), and
it leaves the cast as undifferentiated as having none. It is also single-sourced: one entry
per speaker, read by the writer, the generator and the reviewer alike, rather than a
paraphrase in each, which is
[one authority per quantity](../../../_laws.md#one-authority-per-quantity) applied to a
character. See [voice-bible-per-speaker](./techniques/voice-bible-per-speaker.md).

## Machine authorship moves every default the wrong way

A language model asked for dialogue converges, without instruction, on the failures this
subject exists to prevent. Characters state their feelings because stated feelings are the
most probable continuation of an emotional setup. Every speaker shares the model's own
fluent, balanced, faintly therapeutic register. Scenes become polite lectures, because a
helpful assistant's idea of a conversation is a question answered fully. Villains monologue,
because the training data is thick with villains who monologue. And a generation line that
scores its output with a prose-quality grader will select for all of it, because each failure
reads well line by line. The correction is not a better adjective in the prompt. It is a
voice bible in the prompt, a budget that forces compression, a requirement that each speaker
want something stated, and a review that tests attribution blind rather than asking whether
the dialogue is "good".

## Failure modes of the naive reading

The **feelings narrator** has characters announce what they feel and why. The **costume
cast** differs only by accent and catchphrase. The **lecture scene** has one speaker who knows
and one who asks. The **as-you-know briefing** has two characters tell each other what both
already know. The **cartoon threat** announces itself. The **quip reflex** undercuts every hard
beat. The **obscure line** mistakes withholding for subtext and leaves the listener nothing to
infer. And the **shelf bible** is a voice reference that was written once and is read by
nothing that produces or judges a line.

## What this cannot decide

Whether the story is worth telling, whether a scene belongs in it, and whether the plot
makes sense are not voice questions. A perfectly voiced scene can be in the wrong place. Nor
does any check here prove a line will land when performed: subtext lives partly in delivery,
and a line that reads as restrained can be played as flat. Read-aloud and performance are a
separate rung of evidence above anything a page review reaches.

## Boundaries

**Against short-form-cards-and-barks.** That sibling owns the smallest units — the
three-line card shown between plays, the bark that fires on an overtake, the taunt — and
the compression, repetition and television-reading budgets they live under. This subject
establishes the voice those units spend. The rule for picking: if the defect is that a
character does not sound like themselves anywhere, or that a scene says too much and wants
too little, it is this subject's; if the voice is clear in scenes and lost under a
one-breath or three-line budget, or a line wears out on its tenth hearing, it is the
sibling's.

**Against antagonist-fair-grievance-craft.** That sibling owns the antagonist as a designed
character — the fair grievance, the wrong means, the protected thing, the witnessed act of
follow-through that funds his calm, and the stance his speech must take. This subject owns
how threat is written at line level for anyone who threatens, the antagonist included: the
rhythm, specificity, conditional form and silence that keep a menacing line from tipping
into parody. If the fix changes what he wants, holds or has done, it is the sibling's; if the
fix is a rewrite of the line for register, compression or what it leaves unsaid, it is this
one's.

**Against llm-dialogue-quality-control.** That sibling owns the instrument that judges
generated dialogue in a pipeline: what is checked mechanically, what an independent judge
grades, how verdicts are bound and recorded, and how a failing line is sent back. This
subject owns the craft standard such an instrument checks against, and the voice bible that
both the generator and the judge must read. The rule for picking: if the question is what a
good line for this speaker is, it is here; if it is how a pipeline finds out whether it got
one, it is there.
