# Shop and paddock exchanges as trades in a debt-racing campaign

This application reads dialogue-as-a-trade, with its neighbour exposition-inside-conflict,
against two research dossiers written for Death Ride, a top-down vehicular combat racer for a
television set-top device in which the player races to pay off a debt. The dossiers are
`docs/narrative/research/R1-prestige-series-craft.md` and
`docs/narrative/research/R3-dialogue-craft.md` in the tree at
`firetv-deathride` (both written 2026-10-04; paths below are relative to
that root). They are research distilled from interviews, memos and on-screen readings; the
game has not been played, so nothing here is a result seen in play. The exchanges in scope are
the dossier's
`docs/narrative/research/R3-dialogue-craft.md:5 "shop or pre- and post-race exchanges of three to eight lines"`.

## What the dossiers confirm

The core rule appears in both. R1 states it as
`docs/narrative/research/R1-prestige-series-craft.md:98 "each line must be an attempt to get something from the other person or to give them something"`,
with the cut rule `docs/narrative/research/R1-prestige-series-craft.md:98 "If no one in the exchange wants anything, cut it."`,
from Warren Ellis on writing Castlevania:
`docs/narrative/research/R1-prestige-series-craft.md:102 "These aren't lectures."`
(Collider, https://collider.com/castlevania-season-3-warren-ellis-kevin-kolde-interview/ ;
Bleeding Cool, https://bleedingcool.com/tv/castlevania-warren-ellis-talks-adapting-video-game-working-with-netflix-dracula-more-interview/).
R3 grounds the same rule in David Mamet's memo to the writers of The Unit
(https://nofilmschool.com/2010/10/david-mamet-drama-a-memo-the-unit-writers):
`docs/narrative/research/R3-dialogue-craft.md:15 "who wants what, what happens if they don't get it, and why now"`,
and in Robert McKee's framing via summaries,
`docs/narrative/research/R3-dialogue-craft.md:15 "each line should shift the balance of power or knowledge"`.
The naive failure matches the technique's lecture:
`docs/narrative/research/R1-prestige-series-craft.md:100 "Philosophical speeches where the theme is stated outright."`

Exposition as a trade is confirmed by
`docs/narrative/research/R3-dialogue-craft.md:116 "World facts arrive as ammunition or as the price of a deal."`,
with a Death Ride instance of the price form:
`docs/narrative/research/R3-dialogue-craft.md:118 "The Mechanic only names a region's hazards while haggling over tyres."`
The Mechanic's line `docs/narrative/research/R3-dialogue-craft.md:21 "Axle's in. It's not new. It's honest."`
is a trade in three fragments: he wants trust in a used part and is paying for it with candour.

## Upward lessons taken into the technique

Two came from these lines. The stake and the clock — what happens if a side does not get what
it wants, and why now — were added to the first step of the procedure from Mamet's three
questions at `docs/narrative/research/R3-dialogue-craft.md:15`. And the ending rule, that a
scene stops when its trade settles, came from
`docs/narrative/research/R1-prestige-series-craft.md:98 "And end every conversation once its trade is settled."`
R1's on-screen reading of a crime boss telling his childhood trauma while doing the same to a
subordinate — `docs/narrative/research/R1-prestige-series-craft.md:102 "The backstory is the leverage."`
— became the backstory-as-leverage paragraph in the trade technique and the strongest form of
ammunition in exposition-inside-conflict.

## Where the dossiers fall short of the standard

**The player's options are not treated as offers.** R3's banter structures include an
accept, reject and deflect set for choices, but neither dossier prices the player's options:
nothing says that a reply which costs the player candour, a favour or standing should buy a
different answer from a free question. In a campaign whose whole economy is debt, the shop
exchange is the natural place to make a reply cost scrip or a favour owed to the Mechanic, and
the dossiers leave that unused.

**No check that a scene changed the balance.** The rubric scores each line's action, but no
step states, after an exchange, who got what and what it cost, which is the cheapest way to
find a scene that did not happen.

## Evidence honesty

R1 rates the trade rule high for Ellis:
`docs/narrative/research/R1-prestige-series-craft.md:106 "High for Ellis."`, and lists it among
its strongest patterns, `docs/narrative/research/R1-prestige-series-craft.md:365 "dialogue as trade (Ellis)"`.
R3 rates its version high with the note
`docs/narrative/research/R3-dialogue-craft.md:23 "Mamet's memo is primary"`, while its McKee
material reaches it through summaries, which are secondary. Every example line in R3 is
original to the project,
`docs/narrative/research/R3-dialogue-craft.md:7 "Every example line was written for this project"`,
and none has been played, voiced to a player or tested.

## The Death Ride use

Each shop and paddock exchange is briefed with one line per side naming the want, the cost of
failing and why it must be settled before this race; each player option is written as a move
with a price; each needed world fact enters as a jab, a bargain, a correction or an object;
and after each exchange the writer records who got what and what it cost, cutting the exchange
if the answer is nobody and nothing.
