# Warmth in a grim world

The named concern: put warmth and humour into a hard world as things the characters do to
survive it, so that the world stays grim and the people in it stay human. Unbroken bleakness
numbs; a player stops feeling loss when nothing is ever not lost. Warmth is what gives loss
its weight, because the player has to have seen what there was to lose.

## Two naive cures, both worse

**The scheduled relief.** The writer notices the grimness and inserts comedy: a comic
character whose function is jokes, a light scene after every dark one. The relief is the
writer's, not the characters', and it reads as such — the comic character is a mood
regulator, not a person, and the world's grimness becomes a setting the story does not quite
believe.

**The quip reflex.** Every emotionally serious beat is followed by a joke that defuses it.
This is the more damaging failure, because it teaches the player that the story will always
let them off, and once learned that lesson cannot be unlearned: no later loss is allowed to
land. A generator produces the quip reflex readily, because a light closing beat is a
probable continuation of a heavy one.

## Warmth as behaviour

Warmth that works is done, not announced. People in hard places tease the ones they love
because saying it plainly costs too much. They share the last of something without remarking
on it. They keep small rituals — the same greeting, the same cup, the same argument about the
same song. They mock what frightens them. The player infers affection from these acts the
same way they infer anything through subtext, and inferred warmth is felt more than declared
warmth, which tends to sound like a greeting card.

Humour, likewise, belongs to particular characters as part of their voice. Who jokes, about
what, at whose expense, and when they stop joking are all voice choices. A character whose
humour is dry, deflecting and always aimed at themselves is a different person from one
whose humour is loud and aimed at authority, and both are different from the character who
never jokes — whose one joke, late in the story, is an event.

Competence can be care. A character who over-explains a repair, checks a strap twice or
leaves a spare part on the bench without comment is showing affection in the only
vocabulary they trust, and the player reads it as such.

## Humour is with the player, never at them

A game adds a hazard the page does not have: the player is in the scene. A reactive line that
mocks a failure the player just made sounds like the game mocking the player, and the
warmest character in the cast becomes the most irritating one by the third crash. Humour
aimed at the player's failure is reserved for a rival whose antagonism is the point, and even
then it stings without sneering. Everywhere else the joke is shared with the player — at the
situation, at the world, at the speaker's own expense. Two smaller rules travel with this:
puns belong in names and labels, not in a person's mouth, because nobody under pressure
speaks in puns; and every intended laugh is tested by asking whether a real person in that
position would say it, which is the test most generated humour fails.

## Placement rules

Ration humour by stakes. After a real loss, at least one beat sits unjoked — the player and
the characters are allowed to feel it — and the first person to joke afterwards is making a
choice the scene can see: it can be brave, cruel, or the first sign of recovery, and the
other characters should react to it as such. Gallows humour, joking in the face of the thing
itself, is legitimate and powerful when it comes from the person facing it; from a bystander
it is the quip reflex again.

Let warmth be at risk. Small kindnesses and running jokes established early are what a later
loss takes away, and the absence of a ritual — the greeting nobody says any more — can carry a
loss in fewer words than any eulogy. Plan warmth with that in mind.

## Decision rules

- **When a joke follows a loss within the same beat, cut it** unless the person joking is
  the one who lost, and the scene reacts to the joke.
- **When a character's role is to be funny, give them a want and a refusal** that have
  nothing to do with being funny, or they remain a mood regulator.
- **When affection is stated outright ("I care about you"), try it as an act or a tease
  first.** Keep the plain statement for the one moment it must be plain.
- **When the cast's humour all sounds alike, assign each speaker a humour target and
  register** in their voice reference.
- **When a world has had no warm beat for a long stretch of play, add one that is at risk**,
  not one that is merely pleasant.
- **When generating a scene that follows a loss, instruct the generator to end it unresolved
  and unjoked.**
- **When a reactive line fires on the player's failure, check who the joke is on.** If it is
  the player, rewrite it toward the situation or the speaker.

## When not to use this

A story whose point is unrelieved despair may withhold warmth on purpose, at the cost of
numbing, and should make that trade knowingly. Comedy-led material inverts the rationing:
there the rare serious beat is the event, and the same placement logic runs the other way.

## Evidence status

That humour and tenderness under pressure deepen rather than dilute a grim story is
established in drama and in games-writing practice, and the coexistence of laughter and
dread is documented in stage traditions that use both. The with-the-player rule rests on a
games studio's reported early-access audience response and a games writer's article on
reactive lines; the rules on puns and on the human test come from a games-industry
practitioner article. All are secondary or practitioner sources, rated high by the research
that collected them. The quip-reflex failure is a widely voiced criticism, and the claim
that models habitually add a light closing line is editorial observation, not a measurement. The rationing
rules are practitioner heuristics. None of this has been tested with players in a played game.
