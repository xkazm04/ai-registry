# Menace without parody

The named concern: write a threatening line so that the listener believes it, at line level
and for any character who threatens. Parody is what threat becomes when the speaker visibly
needs to be believed: they announce, explain, escalate, laugh, decorate. Menace is the
opposite posture — a speaker who does not need the listener's agreement, saying something
ordinary that the listener cannot stop hearing.

## The tells of parody

They are few and well known, which makes them checkable. The **announced threat**: "you will
regret this", "I will destroy you" — a promise of harm in general terms, which tells the
listener the speaker has not decided what. The **adjective of evil**: the speaker describes
their own cruelty or power. The **explanation**: the plan laid out, the reasons justified at
length, which is a request for agreement. The **escalation by volume**: louder and longer as
stakes rise, and its written form, the exclamation mark attached to a future threat. The
**laugh**, written or directed. And the **generic insult**, which could be
aimed at anyone and therefore lands on no one. Any one of these in a line is a signal to
rewrite; two in one line is almost always parody.

## How a menacing line is built

**Ordinary surface.** The line is about something mundane — the weather, the tea, a car's
paintwork, the listener's children's school. The danger is never named. The gap between the
banal words and the situation is where the listener's fear lives, and the listener's
imagination is the more frightening writer.

**Specific detail.** The speaker knows something particular: the listener's address, the
debt to the day, the name of the person they love. Specificity says "I have already looked",
which is a threat no adjective can make.

**Conditional form.** The threat is a term, not a promise of pain: if this, then that, and the
"that" is stated flatly or left unsaid. Terms imply a system, and a system is more frightening
than a temper.

**Shortening under pressure.** As stakes rise, the menacing speaker says less. The longest
line is early and relaxed; the last line before something happens is a few words, or a
question, or nothing.

**Questions they know the answer to.** "How is your mother's leg?" asked by the man who holds
her debt does more than any statement of power.

**Courtesy that is not needed.** Politeness from someone who visibly need not be polite reads
as control. The courtesy must not wobble; a menacing speaker who breaks into rudeness has lost
the scene.

**A frame the speaker believes.** The menacing speaker thinks they are the reasonable one —
the adult in the room, the one doing the necessary thing — and their lines are written from
inside that belief. The threat is routine to them, and the listener hears that it is routine.
A speaker who knows they are the villain performs villainy; a speaker who believes they are
keeping order simply keeps it.

**Direct the calm.** When a line is performed, its direction is part of the line. A threat
directed as anger turns the speaker back into someone who needs to be believed, and a voice
cast for calm and then directed to shout tends to break. Direct menace as flat, even and
unhurried, and let the words carry it.

## Menace and humour together

Threat and comedy coexist well, and the combination is one of the strongest registers
available: two people talking about something absurd while the danger sits unnamed in the
room. The rule is that the humour belongs to the situation or to the menacing speaker's dry
precision, and never to the speaker mocking their own threat. A joke that winks at the
audience — the villain who knows he is a villain — is parody by definition.

## What line craft cannot do alone

A line is credible only if the world has shown that this speaker's terms are real. Line craft
spends credibility; it does not create it. Where the speaker is the story's controlling
antagonist, the act that earns his calm is designed as part of his character, not as a
property of his lines, and a perfectly written threat from a speaker who has never followed
through still reads as hollow. For lesser threateners — an enforcer, a rival — the same holds
at smaller scale: one visible consequence funds a great many quiet lines.

## Decision rules

- **When a threat names the harm in general terms, replace it with a specific condition** or
  with silence about the consequence.
- **When a menacing speaker's lines get longer as the scene tightens, invert the curve.**
- **When the speaker describes themselves, cut the description**; let the listener supply it.
- **When a line would work shouted, rewrite it to work whispered**, and check it still lands.
- **When a generator writes a threat, give it the mundane subject, the specific detail the
  speaker knows, and a ban on announcing harm**, because it defaults to the announced threat.
- **When menace and comedy share a scene, check who the joke is on.** Never on the threat.

## When not to use this

Comedy that intends parody — a villain played for laughs — should use the tells on purpose;
the rule is that parody is chosen, never drifted into. Unhinged or chaotic threat, where
unpredictability is the menace, uses volume and incoherence deliberately and is a different
register. And material for young audiences may need the threat clearer than this technique
leaves it, because the gap between the words and the danger is precisely what a young
listener may not close.

## Evidence status

The mechanisms here — menace through ordinary talk, evasion, the unexplained threat and
comedy alongside dread — are documented in a mid-century tradition of stage drama known for
exactly that combination, described here from a secondary encyclopedic summary. The
believed frame rests on games writers' interviews about villains who think they are right,
reported second-hand, and rated high by the research that collected them; the dossier's own
list of precision, understatement, reasonable frame and unspoken cost is rated medium as a
synthesis, and the parody tells are practitioner synthesis. The warning that a calm voice
directed to shout misfires comes from a speech-synthesis vendor's documentation of its own
tags, a primary source for that one medium only. The shortening-under-pressure curve is a craft
heuristic, not a measured effect. None of it has been tested with players in a played game.
