# Exposition inside conflict

The named concern: deliver the facts a player needs about the world, its history and its
people inside exchanges where someone has a reason to say them and someone has a reason not
to want to hear them. A fact that arrives as ammunition, as an accusation or as the price of
a favour is remembered, because it came attached to a stake. A fact that arrives as a
briefing is skipped, by the eye on a page and by the thumb on a controller.

## The briefing and its disguises

The undisguised failure is two characters telling each other what both already know, for the
benefit of a listener who is not in the room. It has a familiar opening — "as you know" — and
writers who have learned to avoid the phrase often keep the structure: a new arrival who
needs everything explained, a convenient recording, a character who suddenly recites history
at a moment of calm. Each disguise is still a briefing, because nobody in the scene needs or
resists the information; it is addressed past them to the player.

## Ways a fact can enter with a stake

**As ammunition.** In an argument, each side reaches for the facts that hurt the other. "You
weren't there when they took the well" is history delivered as a blow, and the player learns
the history and the wound together.

The strongest form of ammunition is backstory used as leverage: a character tells the story
of what was once done to them while doing the same thing to the listener, and the history
arrives as a threat in the present tense rather than as a memory.

**As a price.** One character knows, the other pays to learn — in money, a favour, a secret
of their own. The fact is valued in the scene because it was bought.

**As a correction.** A character gets something wrong and another, with a reason to, puts
them right. The error makes the truth stand out, and the correcting character reveals what
they care about by which errors they correct.

**As a refusal.** A character pointedly does not say something, and the shape of what they
avoid tells the player there is something there. The fact itself can come later; the
existence of the fact arrives now.

**As an object.** A ledger, a scar, a posted rule, a name scratched off a wall. The world
states the fact and a character's reaction to it carries the meaning; nobody has to explain.

## Dose and order

A scene carries one new fact the player needs, rarely two. Facts beyond that slide off,
however well delivered. Deliver it after the player has been made to want it — after a
question has been planted by something they saw or something withheld — because a fact that
answers a question is kept and a fact that answers nothing is not. Then withhold the rest of
the world until a later conflict needs it; a world revealed in pieces, each when it starts
to matter, feels larger than a world explained at the start, and the parts that never
become necessary never need writing.

Know which facts the player must retain to play and which are colour. A fact the player needs
to make a decision must also be retrievable outside the scene — in a journal, a card, a
repeated line — because a fact buried in a heated exchange is easy to miss, and a decision
made in ignorance of it feels unfair.

## Decision rules

- **When both speakers already know the fact, one of them must have a reason to say it now**
  — to hurt, to remind, to bargain — or it is cut from the exchange and moved.
- **When a new character exists to have things explained to them, give them a stake** in the
  answer, or replace the explanation with an object.
- **When a scene introduces more than two new facts, split it** or move the rest to the
  conflicts that will need them.
- **When a fact gates a player decision, deliver it in conflict and again plainly where the
  decision is made.** Drama earns attention; the plain restatement earns fairness.
- **When a generator writes exposition, instruct it to name who resists the fact and why**,
  because it will otherwise produce the briefing by default.

## When not to use this

An opening that must teach controls or rules quickly is not the place to make every fact a
weapon; clarity wins, and the conflict can arrive one scene later. A deliberately expository
voice — a narrator, an announcer, a broadcast — is a briefing by design and is judged on its
own voice rather than on hidden conflict. And a world whose mystery is the point can withhold
past comfort; the rule about plain restatement applies only to facts a decision depends on.

## Evidence status

Hiding exposition in conflict, and the "exposition as ammunition" maxim, are standard
screenwriting teaching; the rule that an as-you-know line means the scene is broken comes
from a primary memo by a playwright to a writing staff, and the refusal of a narrating
explainer from game and film practitioners' interviews, reported second-hand. Backstory as
leverage is read from an animated series on screen, which the research rated high for the
scene's content. The one-new-fact-per-scene dose is a practitioner rule of thumb, not a
measured limit. The rule that decision-critical facts be
restated plainly is games-specific practice drawn from usability reasoning. None of this has
been tested with players in a played game.
