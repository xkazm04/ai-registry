# Dialogue as a trade

The named concern: make every exchange a negotiation between people who each want something
from the other, so that each line is a move and the scene ends with the balance changed.
The currency varies — information, permission, money, time, forgiveness, face, a promise —
but there is always a currency, both sides hold some, and neither gives it away for free.

## The lecture and why it is the default

The lecture scene has one character who knows and one who asks. The asker's lines are
prompts — "What do you mean?", "Go on", "And then what?" — and the knower's lines are
paragraphs. It is the default because it is the shortest path from a writer's notes to the
page: the writer has information to convey and a character to convey it to. It fails because
nothing is at stake in it. The asker wants nothing and risks nothing, so the listener has no
side to take, and every prompt line could be deleted without loss — which is the test.

Games fall into the lecture more than any other medium, because the player character is
structurally an asker. A dialogue menu of four questions, each releasing a block of answer,
is a lecture with a user interface. The player clicks through it because the game has given
them no reason to care which answer comes first.

## The procedure

1. **Name both wants, the stake and the clock.** Before writing, state in one line what each
   speaker wants from the other in this scene, what each is prepared to give, what happens to
   each if they do not get it, and why this has to be settled now rather than tomorrow. If
   one side wants nothing, either give them a want or cut the scene to a line; if nothing
   happens when they fail, the scene has no pressure; if it could happen tomorrow, it has no
   reason to happen now.
2. **Name what each withholds.** The trade needs friction: each side holds back something
   the other wants, and has a reason to.
3. **Write moves, not turns.** Each line is an offer, a refusal, a counter-offer, a
   concession, a bluff, a test or a change of the terms. A line that is none of these is a
   prompt and goes.
4. **Let the price rise.** Within a scene, the cost of the next piece of what one side wants
   should go up, or the terms should change, so the scene has a direction.
5. **End on a changed balance, and end when the trade settles.** State, after the scene, who
   got what and what it cost. A scene where nobody got anything and nothing was paid is a
   scene that did not happen, however good its lines. And once the trade is settled, the
   scene is over: every line after the settlement is the writer lingering, and a
   conversation that outstays its trade turns back into a lecture.

Even talk about ideas — power, loyalty, what is owed — follows the same rule. A scene in which
two characters discuss a theme is a lecture in two voices unless each is trying to get
something from the other by arguing it. Backstory obeys it too: a character's history told
as leverage over the listener, while the leverage is being applied, is a trade; the same
history told as a memory is a pause in the story.

## The player's options are offers

Where the player chooses lines, write each option as a move with a price rather than a
question with an answer. The player can press, flatter, concede, threaten, lie, pay or walk
away, and the character answering has a reason to give less than was asked. An option that
costs the player something — candour, a favour owed, standing with someone else, a resource
— makes the answer worth more than the same answer released by a question. A question option
is legitimate where the trade is the knowledge itself, but even then the answerer should
charge for it: an evasion first, a condition, a demand to know why the player wants to know.

Trades also give player choices their consequence without new content: the same reply
landing after a concession and after a threat leaves the relationship in different places,
and a later scene can read which.

## Decision rules

- **When a line can be answered with "go on", the scene is a lecture**; rewrite the asker
  to want something and to pay for each answer.
- **When a character gives information unprompted and unpaid, give them a motive** — they
  want something by giving it — or move the information to a scene where someone does.
- **When a scene has one active speaker, make the quiet one withhold**, because a silent
  party who wants nothing is furniture.
- **When writing a menu of options, check that no two are the same move.** Four questions
  are one move offered four times.
- **When a scene ends with nobody owing anybody anything new, cut or merge it.**

## When not to use this

Not every line is a negotiation in the narrow sense: grief shared, a joke between friends, a
moment of rest after a peak are exchanges where the currency is closeness and the trade is
soft, and forcing an angle onto them makes every character a hustler. Use the frame as a
diagnostic — what does each want here — rather than as a requirement that everyone bargain.
Pure system communication, such as a tutorial voice, is not a trade and should not pretend to
be one.

## Evidence status

That every scene is driven by characters who want something from each other is foundational
screenwriting and playwriting doctrine; the want, the stake and the why-now questions come
from a primary memo by a playwright to a television writing staff, and the explicit
get-or-give framing, with the rule that a conversation ends when its arc concludes, from an
animated series writer's interview, rated high by the research that collected it. Treating
each line as a move that shifts power or knowledge reaches this document through summaries
of a dialogue textbook, which is secondary. The application to player dialogue options as offers with prices is games-writing
practice and practitioner synthesis. None of it has been tested with players in a played
game.
