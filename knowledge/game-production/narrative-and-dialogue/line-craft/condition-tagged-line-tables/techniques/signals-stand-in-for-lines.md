# Signals stand in for lines

The named concern: carry the part of a character's reaction that no affordable table can
voice, through a channel that costs nothing per occurrence — a meter, a typeface, a colour, an
expression, a short sound. A line table can answer events; it cannot narrate a feeling that
changes every few seconds, and it should not try. The signal does that job, and does it in a
way the player can learn.

## Lines carry changes, signals carry levels

The division of labour is by the shape of the state. A **change** — the rivalry tipped from
contempt to respect, the ally just lost faith — is discrete, happens rarely, and deserves a
line, because the line is the moment the player learns it happened. A **level** — how angry,
how close to breaking, how much respect, how near the next turn — moves continuously, would
need a line for every step, and is better shown than said. A meter that fills as a rival's
grudge grows, a name card whose colour cools as an ally drifts, a typeface that cracks as a
speaker loses composure: each displays a level, so lines can be saved for the crossings.

The two work together in a specific way. The signal makes the hidden state the table queries
visible, so when a line changes the player can see *why* — the meter crossed the mark and the
rival's voice changed with it. Without the signal, reactive lines seem arbitrary, because the
player cannot see the facts they are matching.

## One signal, one meaning, one source

A signal is a vocabulary of one word, and it is only worth anything if the word has exactly
one meaning. A colour that marks anger in one place, low health in another and a rival's
identity in a third teaches the player to ignore colour. So each signal is declared with its
meaning, its range and its legend before it is used, and no second meaning is ever added to
it; a new meaning gets a new signal.

The signal reads the same fact the line reads, from the same owner
([one-authority-per-quantity](../../../../_laws.md#one-authority-per-quantity)). A meter computed
by the interface from its own approximation and a line selected from the true fact will
eventually disagree, and a character who speaks warmly under a meter showing contempt has told
the player that one of them is lying. Players believe the signal, because it is always there.

## Typography and colour as voice

A typeface is the cheapest form of characterization a text game has. A per-speaker face, or a
change of weight, case or texture under a declared condition — rage, injury, a corrupted
transmission — makes a generic line read differently without a second line being written. The
same holds for colour on a speaker's name or text. Two cautions attach. Legibility outranks
mood: a stressed face that cannot be read at the viewing distance is a line lost, not a line
coloured. And colour never carries meaning alone — a state shown only in hue is invisible to a
player who cannot separate the hues, so every colour meaning is doubled by shape, icon,
position or text.

## Behaviour is a signal too

In a game where characters act — drive, fight, follow — the way they act is the richest signal
channel there is and costs no writing at all. A rival whose grudge has grown drives at the
player rather than at the finish; an ally who has lost faith hangs back. The table does not own
that behaviour, which is decided by the agent layer, but it owns the agreement: the behaviour and
the line read the same fact, and the line can name what the player just saw the character do. A
change shown first in behaviour and confirmed later by a line lands harder than either alone.

## Signals as the overflow channel

When the arithmetic for a row says its pool cannot be filled — the event fires too often for
the lines anyone can afford — the signal absorbs the excess. The first occurrence is spoken;
the next several inside the cooldown are answered by a flash on the meter, a portrait reaction
or a short non-verbal sound. The player still sees the character notice, and the line keeps its
value by not being worn out. This is the most direct way a signal stands in for a line, and it
is the right answer more often than commissioning a deeper pool.

## Decision rules

- **When a state changes continuously, show it; when it crosses a threshold, say it.**
- **When a signal is introduced, declare its single meaning and its legend**, and refuse every
  later request to overload it.
- **When a signal and a line describe the same state, bind both to the same fact owner.**
- **When a speaker's state can be shown by type or colour, prefer that to a variant line**, and
  check the result at the real viewing distance.
- **When a colour carries meaning, double it with a non-colour cue.**
- **When a row's pool is too thin for its fire rate, route the overflow to a signal** before
  writing more lines.

## Evidence status

That a signal can show a character's feeling where no line can be afforded is a primary-source
claim from a studio writer explaining a companion-approval display, which the dossier rates
medium because only headlines and summaries could be read. Typeface as voice, with a face per
social class read before the words, is a high-confidence primary account from a director of a
text-heavy game. Behaviour as signal rests on the dossier's design synthesis rather than on a
cited source. The level-versus-change split, the one-meaning rule, the shared fact owner and
the colour-doubling rule are practitioner judgement. None of it has been tested in a played game
for which this subject was written.

## When not to use this

A signal cannot carry a cause. A meter can say the rival's grudge grew; only a line can say it
grew because the player put them in the wall. Where the point of the moment is attribution, the
signal is an accompaniment, not a substitute. And a signal added to a screen that already
carries many is not free: on a crowded display each additional meter competes for the
attention the others need, and the honest cost of a signal is the attention it takes from
everything else.
