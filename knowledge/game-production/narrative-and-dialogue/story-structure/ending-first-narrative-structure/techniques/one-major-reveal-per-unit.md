# One major reveal per unit

The named concern: give each unit of a campaign exactly one major reveal, build the unit
around it, and leave the unit room to absorb it. A unit is the span a player consumes in one
sitting and judges as a whole; a major reveal is one that recontextualises a relationship, a
motive or the stakes. The rule is a budget, and like every budget it shapes the work rather
than only capping it.

## Major and minor

A **minor reveal** supplies a fact: where the rival is racing next, what the debt amounts
to, who owns the garage. It moves the plot and asks nothing of the audience's
understanding. A **major reveal** changes the meaning of what came before: the mentor sold
the protagonist out, the debt was never the protagonist's, the rival is racing to pay for a
sibling's surgery. The test is retroactive: a major reveal makes the audience re-read
earlier scenes; a minor one does not.

The budget applies to majors only. A unit may carry several minors, and usually should,
because minors are what keep information moving between the majors. Among candidate majors,
prefer the human ones — a relationship or a motive recontextualised — over the conceptual
ones that disclose lore or the hidden machinery of the world. Audiences remember the first
kind and forgive the absence of the second, and a campaign whose majors drift from people
toward concepts is the one reviewers call overstuffed.

## The reveal and the unit's one idea

In a played unit the reveal has a partner: the unit's one new idea of play — a rule variant,
a new hazard, a changed objective — introduced, developed, twisted and then retired, so that
each unit feels like a new scene rather than the previous one with different numbers. The
strongest units fuse the two: the twist of the play idea *is* the reveal, or the reveal
changes how the rest of the unit must be played. A unit whose reveal arrives in a card while
its play is unchanged is two separate experiences in one sitting, and the play usually wins
the player's attention.

## Why one

A major reveal needs two kinds of room. Before it, a **build**: the unit's events tighten
toward the moment, plants surface, and the audience is primed without being told. After it,
an **aftermath**: the characters react, the player re-reads what they knew, and the reveal
changes a decision. Strip either and the reveal shrinks — no build and it arrives as an
announcement, no aftermath and it is overwritten before it is felt.

A second major in the same unit lands inside the first one's aftermath. The audience is
still re-reading the first when the second asks them to re-read again, and they do neither
fully. Both reveals are spent and neither is felt.

A unit with no major is filler, unless it was designed as a **breather** — a unit whose job
is to let the campaign's last major settle and to plant for the next. A breather is
declared in the plan, and it carries plants; an undeclared unit without a reveal is
connective tissue that has grown into a unit.

## Reveal inflation

The campaign-scale failure is escalation: each reveal designed to top the last, until every
character has a secret and every secret has a deeper one. The audience learns that nothing
they are told is final and stops investing in any of it — and the finale's reveal, the one
the whole campaign was planted for, is discounted with the rest. Inflation is what an
open-ended plan produces when it has no final card to answer to: without a destination,
the only available direction for the next reveal is up.

The remedy is the budget held across the campaign, not only per unit. Major reveals are
listed in the plan in order, each one recontextualises something specific, and none is
allowed to undo a previous major unless that undoing was itself planted. A reveal that
retracts a reveal is a promise broken twice.

## Procedure

1. **List the majors from the final card backwards.** The card's prerequisites name most of
   them. Assign each to a unit, latest first.
2. **Count units against majors.** More majors than units means either units must be added
   or majors demoted to minors or merged. Fewer majors than units means designed breathers
   or a campaign that is longer than its story.
3. **Place each reveal inside its unit** late enough to have a build and early enough to
   have an aftermath that changes a decision before the unit ends. The last third of the
   unit is the usual home; the very last moment works only as a cliffhanger, and a campaign
   of cliffhangers has no aftermaths at all.
4. **Check the order.** A reveal that depends on another is placed after it, with at least
   one unit between when the second recontextualises the first.

## Decision rules

- **When a unit has two majors, move one or demote one.** Moving is usually right; the
  second major is almost always the first major of a unit that does not yet exist.
- **When a unit has no major and was not designed as a breather, give it one or cut it.**
- **When a reveal must undo an earlier one, plant the undoing** as densely as the original;
  otherwise demote the earlier reveal to a misdirection the plan names from the start.
- **When the unit is a race, a fight or another played event, let the event carry the
  reveal** rather than a scene around it. A reveal that changes how the player plays the
  rest of the event is the strongest placement the medium allows.

The rule is [a budget shapes the output, it does not only cap it](../../../../_laws.md#a-budget-shapes-the-output)
at campaign scale: a plan given unlimited reveals spends them, and the shape of each unit —
its build and its aftermath — is what the budget of one buys.

## When not to use this

- **For an anthology or episodic structure** where units are self-contained and the
  campaign carries no reveal chain; each episode still benefits from one turn, but the
  campaign ledger has nothing to budget.
- **For a mystery whose genre contract is a cascade of revelations at the end.** The
  traditional gathering-of-suspects finale is a deliberate exception, and its several
  reveals are planted as one.
- **As a count to maximise.** One per unit is a ceiling and a floor together; a plan that
  shortens units to fit more reveals has optimised the count and lost the room the budget
  existed to buy.

## Evidence status

The budget itself — one major reveal per unit, with the cut of unpayable threads as its
twin — is stated in a prestige-series dossier and supported there by a secondary production
summary of one series that gave each episode one turning reveal (Medium), and by the
showrunner's own concession that a final season was rushed and overloaded (High for the
quotes, Medium for the reported early-cut length). The dossier offers no test for what makes
a reveal major, so the retroactive re-read test here is practitioner craft. The one-idea-per-
unit pairing rests on a primary level-design interview (High). The human-over-conceptual
preference is the series co-creator's own contrast between two seasons (High as a quote; the
link to reception is inference). None of it has been tested in a played game yet.
