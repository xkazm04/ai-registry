# Lock the final card first

The named concern: before any unit is outlined, write the last thing the player sees — one
concrete image, the final card — and lock it as the authority the rest of the plan derives
from. Everything else in this subject is a derivation, and a derivation from an unfixed
premise is a draft of a draft.

## What a final card is

A final card is an **image**, not a summary. It names who is present and who is not, where
they are, what they hold, what has been lost, and what the last visible action is. It is
short — two or three sentences — and it is concrete enough to be drawn.

The test that separates a card from a destination is generative: hand the card to a second
writer and ask what must have happened before it. A card that yields the same short list
from two writers is locked material; a card that yields nothing is a mood. "They win, at a
cost" yields nothing. "The champion's trophy sits on the hood of a burned-out car outside the
house she grew up in, and she walks away from both" yields a lot: a championship, a car that
burned, a house that mattered, and a decision to leave all three — each of which is a plant
the campaign now owes.

A card states the last moment of the campaign's **central relationship** — the pair whose
story the campaign actually is — not the plot's last event. The last event is frequently a
climax the player has just played through; the card is what comes after it, the quiet frame
in which the climax's meaning is held still long enough to be felt. A campaign that ends on
its climax ends on an exclamation and gives the emotion nowhere to settle.

Three further properties separate a card that carries an ending from one that merely closes
it. It shows a **price**, and the price is the direct cost of the victory — the thing the
protagonist gave up to win — never an unrelated loss added for weight; a clean win where
nothing was paid reads as cheating, and an arbitrary death reads as cruelty. It grants
**one image of what survived**, because a price without a remainder reads as nihilism
rather than depth. And it usually **returns** to a place, an object or a pairing from the
opening, changed by what was gathered on the way, which is what lets the opening be written
afterwards as the card's rhyme.

## Procedure

1. **Write three candidate cards before choosing one.** The first idea for an ending is
   usually the genre's default; writing three forces at least one that is specific to these
   characters. Each candidate gets its prerequisite list.
2. **Choose by prerequisites, not by mood.** Prefer the card whose prerequisite list touches
   the most characters and the earliest units, because that card will make the most of the
   campaign load-bearing. A card whose prerequisites all sit in the last unit is a card the
   rest of the campaign does not serve.
3. **Lock it in writing, in the plan, with a date.** The locked card lives in the same
   artifact as the beat sheet, at its head, because everything below reads from it. A card
   kept in a lead writer's memory is not locked; it is a preference.
4. **Derive the prerequisite list into payoffs.** Each prerequisite becomes a payoff the
   campaign must deliver before the card, and each payoff will need plants. This is the
   hand-off to the beat sheet.
5. **Define the change procedure at the moment of locking.** Changing the card is allowed.
   It is a re-plan: the prerequisite list is re-derived, plants that served only the old card
   are flagged for cutting, and the changed card carries a new date. The procedure exists so
   that the card changes deliberately or not at all.

## Decision rules

- **When the team cannot agree on a card, do not start outlining.** Disagreement about the
  ending discovered in unit six costs every scene written since; discovered on day one it
  costs a meeting. The plan is the place to have the argument.
- **When a card needs a reveal to make sense, the reveal is a payoff and is planted like
  one.** A final card that only works if the player learns something in the last minute is
  a card resting on an unplanted payoff.
- **When there are several endings, lock a card for each,** and treat the plants they share
  as critical-path plants and the plants unique to one ending as belonging to the branch
  that leads there. An ending without its own card is an ending the plan does not pay for.
- **When the card follows a decisive act, the player's character makes the decision.** A card
  whose climax was decided by an ally, in a cutscene, has taken the ending away from the
  person who played toward it, and that is the kind of card that gets rewritten after
  players say so. Usually the decision is performed with the game's core verb.
  - **The decision can be refusing the verb.** It can be withholding the verb, or refusing
    it, when the ending argues against what the verb has been doing. One shipped campaign
    trained killing for its whole length and ended on its protagonist declining to kill.
  - **What players objected to was who decided.** In the revised ending the rule's evidence
    comes from, the act taken from the protagonist was a decision to show mercy, not a use of
    the combat verb.
  - **The ownership is the rule, and the verb is its usual form.**
- **When writing reveals that the card is wrong, change the card through the procedure.**
  Discovery during writing is real, and the lock is not a vow; even a shipped ending has been
  revised when its decisive act was found to belong to the wrong character. What the lock
  forbids is the silent change. A lock described as absolute — the scene that may never
  change — is a lock that will be broken quietly the first time it must be.
- **When the central relationship has moved since the card's date, re-open the card.** A
  lock honoured after the material has grown away from it fails as surely as a drift. One
  long-running series shot its closing scene eight years before its finale. It then wrote
  the central relationship somewhere else, used the scene as shot, and much of its audience
  rejected the ending as a betrayal of the story they had watched. The trigger for re-opening
  is not time. It is a card whose prerequisites the written units no longer support.
- **When a card names an object, the object is a plant.** Props in final images are the
  most efficient plants a campaign has, and the most often forgotten.

The rule rests on
[one authority per quantity](../../../../_laws.md#one-authority-per-quantity): the card is the
single authority on where the campaign ends, and a second, unwritten ending held by a
writer or a director is a second model that disagrees with the first exactly where scenes
get written for it.

## When not to use this

- **For an open-ended or systemic story**, where the ending is generated by play rather than
  authored — a sandbox, a procedural career, an endless mode. Lock the shape of an ending
  class there (what any ending must show) rather than a single card.
- **For a unit-scale story inside a locked campaign.** A single chapter is planned from its
  reveal, not from its own card; the campaign's card already governs.
- **As a substitute for knowing the characters.** A card written before the people in it are
  understood will be locked onto the wrong people. Character work precedes the lock, by days
  rather than by months.

## Evidence status

The core practice — the final scene written first and held as a fixed reference for years —
rests on primary statements by working showrunners of two prestige animated series, read
directly (High in the source dossier).

**The early lock is not a predictor of success.** In a later interview, one of those showrunners
says versions of the end scenes were written and that the last scene is "nearly the same",
not unchanged (re-read verbatim 2026-10-09). Two independent research
lanes both found celebrated finales written forward and locked finales that were panned. One
acclaimed drama's creator said they knew "surprisingly little" about how its plants would
pay off, and he also advised against working that way. So this is survivorship evidence for
a discipline, not a measured effect.

**The decisive-act rule rests on two cases.**
- **A revised ending (Medium).** The studio revised the ending after release, and that is
  reported in an interview. The "stolen agency" reading is the journalist's account of some
  players. The revised act is a decision of mercy, re-read verbatim 2026-10-09.
- **A primary interview on a shipped campaign (High).** Its ending was changed from the
  protagonist killing to the protagonist refusing. Both research lanes named
  verb-refusal endings independently.

The re-open condition rests on one widely reported finale. Both lanes reached it
independently. Its quote is from a summary, because the trade report would not fetch.

The price-and-surviving-image properties rest on primary
showrunner statements (High); the "return to the opening" property is the dossier's own
synthesis across several works (Medium), and the change procedure and the
three-candidates step are practitioner craft with no cited source. None of it has been
tested in a played game yet: the campaign this was reconciled against is unplayed.
