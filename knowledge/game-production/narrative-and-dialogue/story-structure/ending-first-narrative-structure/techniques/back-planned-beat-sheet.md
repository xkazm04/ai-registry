# Back-planned beat sheet

The named concern: the campaign plan is a ledger written from the last unit to the first,
in which each unit's row names its major reveal, the payoffs it delivers and the plants it
carries for later payoffs — and the ledger is checkable. It is the artifact that turns the
rest of this subject from intention into a document several writers, or a generator, can be
held to.

## Why backwards

A plan written forward asks of each unit "what happens next?", and the answer is whatever
is most interesting at that moment. A plan written backwards asks "what must already be
true for this to land?", and the answer is a list of obligations on earlier units. The
second question is the one that produces plants. Written forward, a plan discovers its needs
for plants only when the payoff scene is drafted, by which time the units that should have
carried them are written, and the only remaining options are a retcon or a speech.

## The row

Each row is one unit and carries six fields:

- **Unit** — its number and its played or watched form (a chapter, a mission, a race event).
- **Major reveal** — one, stated as what it recontextualises; or "breather", declared.
- **Payoffs delivered** — each payoff closed in this unit, with a reference to its plants,
  and its kind: surprise (plants under cover) or dread (plants in plain sight).
- **Plants carried** — each plant in this unit, with the later payoff it serves and its
  channel (line, image, act, mechanic).
- **Theme answer** — the answer to the campaign's question this unit argues.
- **Fixed or free** — which of this unit's beats are locked by the plan.

The head of the sheet carries the locked final card, its date, and the theme question.

## When the finale is a test

In a game the last unit is often an event the player must survive or win, and the strongest
campaigns make that event grade everything before it: which allies arrive, what the vehicle
or kit carries, which verbs the player has been taught. Then the finale's **needs** are
payoffs like any reveal, and they are back-planned the same way. Write down what the finale
needs from each ally and each capability, and give each need two earlier beats — an
**acquisition** beat in which the player gets it and a **trust** beat in which it is proven
to work — plus a beat in which the verb is taught somewhere the player cannot fail, before
it is needed where they can. A finale built around its needs, rather than needs added to
justify a finale, is the difference between a last event that sums the campaign and one
that is merely its hardest.

## Fixed beats and free beats

A beat is **fixed** when something later depends on it: the final card, every major
reveal, and every plant some payoff needs. Everything else is **free** — the connective
tissue of how characters get from one fixed beat to the next — and writers may change free
beats without consulting the plan. This distinction is what keeps the plan alive. A sheet
that fixes everything breaks on the first rewrite and is abandoned whole; a sheet that
fixes only what is load-bearing survives every rewrite that leaves the load-bearing beats in
place, and flags the ones that do not.

Interactive campaigns push the fixed set smaller still. Where the order of events depends on
play, or where short lines are chosen at run time by a system that picks the best-matching
entry, only a handful of beats can be pinned to a slot — the hook, the turns, the major
losses, the finale — and everything else floats. The pinned beats are authored and placed
by hand, never left to a run-time picker, because a picker that is right on average is
wrong exactly at the climactic moment it was never designed to judge.

## Procedure

1. **Write the last row first**: the final unit, its reveal, its payoffs, the card.
2. **Walk backwards one unit at a time.** For each row, take the open obligations from the
   rows below — plants that later payoffs need — and assign them here or further back.
   Assign each payoff at least two plants in at least two earlier units, one of them well
   before the payoff, so that the support reads as history and survives a missed scene.
3. **Assign each unit its major reveal** from the list derived from the card, or declare it
   a breather.
4. **At the first row, check the opening** rhymes with the card and that no obligations
   remain unassigned. An obligation that reaches the top of the sheet unassigned is a
   payoff with no plant; it is planted somewhere, or the payoff is cut.
5. **Run the checks** below, then hand the sheet to the writers with the fixed beats
   marked.

## The checks

These are mechanical and should be run by someone other than the sheet's author, or by a
script, every time the sheet changes:

- every payoff has two or more plants, in two or more earlier units;
- every plant names a payoff that exists in a later row;
- every unit has exactly one major reveal or a declared breather;
- every finale need has an acquisition beat and a trust beat in earlier rows;
- every plant serving a critical-path payoff sits on the critical path, and every plant has
  more than one channel where its line could be skipped;
- the opening and the card share an image, an object or a pairing.

The second check is
[declaring an input is not consuming it](../../../../_laws.md#declaring-an-input-is-not-consuming-it):
a plant counts only when a payoff consumes it, and the census is of payoffs reading plants,
not of plants written. And passing all five is
[structural proof is necessary and never sufficient](../../../../_laws.md#structural-proof-is-never-sufficient):
the sheet proves the campaign is paid for on paper. Whether the plants register, whether the
reveals surprise and whether the card moves anyone are questions for a cold read of the
written campaign and, above that, for players.

## Decision rules

- **When a writer proposes a new scene, ask which row it serves.** A scene that carries no
  plant, no payoff and no reveal is free tissue — fine, but it is not allowed to introduce
  weight the sheet does not track.
- **When the card changes, regenerate the sheet from the bottom.** Editing rows in place
  after a card change leaves plants for the old ending in the sheet with nothing to read
  them.
- **When the campaign branches, keep one sheet per ending over a shared trunk.** Plants
  in the trunk serve every ending; plants in a branch serve only the endings reachable from
  it, and the check for critical-path plants is run per ending.
- **When the sheet is given to a generator, give it the rows, not a summary.** A generator
  told the story's arc writes a scene; a generator told this unit's plants, reveal and
  theme answer writes the scene the plan needs.

## When not to use this

- **For a short, single-unit story.** One reveal and three plants do not need a ledger.
- **For a systemic story** whose units are generated in play. Plan the ending class and its
  invariant plants instead.
- **As a replacement for drafting.** The sheet tells writers what each unit owes; it does
  not tell them how to make it good, and a sheet written in such detail that it does is a
  draft in a table.

## Evidence status

Building a campaign backwards from its finale's test rests on a primary statement by a
writer of a shipped role-playing game whose whole structure was built around its final
mission (High in both dossiers that cite it). The few-fixed-beats rule rests on a primary
interview with a roguelike's creative director (High) and a secondary account of the
fractal interest curve (Medium); the warning against letting a run-time picker place a
climactic beat comes from a primary essay on salience-based narrative (High). The
acquisition-and-trust pairing is a dossier's synthesis of that same finale-first source
(High for the source, the pairing is inference). The six-field row, the check list and the
fixed-or-free marking are practitioner craft; the mechanical checks have not been built as a
script. None of it has been tested in a played game yet.
