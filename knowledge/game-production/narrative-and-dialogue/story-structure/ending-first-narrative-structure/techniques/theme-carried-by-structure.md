# Theme carried by structure

The named concern: the campaign's theme is held by its shape — the rhyme of first and last
image, a situation that recurs with a different decision, parallel threads that argue
different answers — and never stated by a character as the moral. Theme stated is a
lecture the audience can decline; theme carried is an experience they have already had by
the time they could object.

## Theme as a question

A theme written as a moral ("family is what you choose") gives the writers nothing to
build with, because a moral is a conclusion and structure is an argument. Written as a
question ("what do you owe the people who made you?"), it becomes a device: the opening
poses it, every thread tests an answer, and the final card is the campaign's answer. The
question is also a filter. A unit whose events bear on the question in no way is either a
designed breather or a candidate for cutting.

The answer at the final card should be one the protagonist reaches, not one they are told,
and ideally one that the opening image would have contradicted. A theme whose answer the
audience already held at the start has nothing to carry.

## The structural carriers

**The rhyming pair.** The opening image and the final card show the same situation —
the same place, the same object, the same two people — changed. The audience does not need
the change explained; the rhyme is the explanation. This is the cheapest carrier and the
most reliable, and it is why the final card is locked first: the opening is written to
rhyme with it, not the other way round.

**The flipping motif.** An image, object or phrase recurs, and each return means something
different — usually the opposite of the last, or its completion. The rule that keeps it
honest is that a motif which means the same thing on its second appearance is a wink, not
an arc; the repeated catchphrase for the cheer is the naive version, and it changes nothing.
Three appearances are enough to carry a whole relationship: the motif destroyed, the motif
spared, the motif watched in peace after a loss.

**The prologue gap.** The opening units are deliberately a different story in tone — lighter,
smaller, survivable — and the rest of the campaign takes apart what the prologue built. The
distance between the two halves, often marked by a jump in time or a single changed number,
is the theme: what was done then becomes who people are now. The audience feels the gap
without anyone naming it.

**The recurring choice.** The protagonist faces the same kind of decision at the start, in
the middle and at the end, and decides differently as the campaign's events argue with
them. Interactive play makes this carrier unusually strong, because the player makes the
choice: the recurrence is felt in the hands. When the player is free to choose the same way
every time, the campaign must make the cost of that consistency visible — the choice is
theirs, the argument is the plan's.

**Arguing threads.** Secondary threads are given to characters who answer the theme's
question differently — one who pays every debt, one who runs from all of them — and their
outcomes are the campaign's evidence. A subplot that does not answer the question is either
texture or a thread to cut.

**Form as argument.** Occasionally the structure itself enacts the theme: a story about
inheritance in which each unit hands an object, a role or a debt to the next character; a
story about exploitation whose sequence of places follows the flow of the thing extracted,
so that the player travels upstream along the money to its owner; a story about repetition
built on circuits. This carrier is powerful and easy to make mechanical; use it where the
form is invisible until noticed, and where it also earns its keep for a second reason — a
sequence that is the supply chain is also a geography the player can learn.

The naive failure on this side is the opposite of the theme speech: a structural device that
carries nothing. A time jump, an intercut or a flashback used because the form is in fashion
is decoration, and decoration in the structure costs more than decoration in the dialogue,
because the audience cannot skim past it.

## Procedure

1. **State the theme as a single question** at the head of the plan, beside the final card.
2. **Write the final card's answer** in one sentence, and check the card shows it without
   words.
3. **Write the opening image to rhyme with the card** — same place, object or pairing,
   opposite condition.
4. **Tag every unit and every secondary thread** with the answer it argues. A column of
   blanks is a column of cuts or breathers.
5. **Search the dialogue for the theme's own words.** A character who says the theme aloud
   is a flag; it is allowed at most once, late, and only when the line is in character and
   the structure has already made the point.

## Decision rules

- **When a character explains what the story means, cut the line or move the meaning into
  an act.** The structure either already carried it, in which case the line is redundant,
  or did not, in which case the line cannot save it.
- **When a subplot answers a different question, cut it or re-aim it.** Two themes in one
  campaign split the rhyme and leave both answers weak.
- **When the opening and closing images do not rhyme, rewrite the opening.** The card is
  locked; the opening is the free variable.
- **When a motif recurs, check its meaning changed.** If it did not, cut the repeat or move
  it to a moment where the relationship has moved.
- **When a structural device carries no part of the answer, remove it.** A flashback or a
  time jump earns its place by arguing; otherwise it is spending the audience's patience.
- **When the theme needs the player to have seen optional content, it is not carried.**
  Thematic carriers live on the critical path like any plant a payoff depends on.

## When not to use this

- **For pure spectacle or arcade framing**, where the story is a wrapper and nobody asked it
  to mean anything. A theme imposed on a wrapper reads as pretension.
- **As a reason to make every element symbolic.** Theme carried by structure uses a few
  load-bearing rhymes; a campaign in which every object is a symbol is one in which none
  is.

## Evidence status

The structure-over-statement rule and the prologue gap rest on a series co-creator's own
account of splitting a season into a prologue and a back half (High in the source dossier),
and on an episode that carries inherited identity through intercutting, checkable on
screen (High). The flipping-motif rule rests on a sequence checkable on screen (High) and a
showrunner's warning against fan service (High). The supply-chain ordering is a dossier
proposal for one campaign and has no external source; it is offered as an illustration of
form as argument, not as observed practice. The theme-as-question method and the dialogue
search are practitioner craft. None of it has been tested in a played game yet.
