# Plant the betrayal in every earlier scene

The named concern: a reveal that rewrites a character must be supported by every earlier
scene that character appears in, each scene carrying a plant that reads innocently forward
and unmistakably backward. Betrayal is the paradigm because it is the reveal that asks the
most — it claims the audience misread a whole person — but the rule governs any reveal of
hidden allegiance, hidden debt, hidden grief or hidden identity.

## Why density, not a clue

A single clue is a coincidence. At the moment of reveal, the audience searches memory for
support; one remembered line reads as the writer reaching back, and two forgotten ones read
as nothing at all. A behaviour present in every shared scene reads as **character**: the
audience does not have to remember any one plant, because the reveal snaps into place a
pattern they have been watching for hours.

Density is also what protects the reveal against interactive play. A player who skipped one
cutscene still met the betrayer in five other scenes. A single-clue plan has a single point
of failure, and the player who missed it experiences the reveal as a retcon.

## The double reading

Every plant has two meanings. Its **cover meaning** is what the scene is about on first
experience — the line serves the scene's own purpose and would be written even if no reveal
followed. Its **second meaning** is what the reveal unlocks. The craft lives in the cover:
a plant that does nothing for its own scene draws attention to itself and is spotted, and
a plant whose cover is so complete it carries no second meaning is not a plant.

The reliable sources of double-reading plants are few and worth naming. The betrayer is
**absent at the right moment** for a reason that is given and is true. They **know a fact**
they had no visible way to know, and the scene moves on before anyone asks. They **argue for
a course of action** on grounds that are good and that also serve the other side. They
**react to a name, a place or an object** with a beat too long, or **over-correct** — too
quick a reassurance, too warm a denial. They **keep a small thing private** — a call taken
outside, a locked glovebox — that reads as personality. They **praise the wrong thing**: the
asset rather than the person, the car rather than the driver. They **tell the truth in a way
built to mislead**. None of these is a clue in isolation; each is a character trait, and the
set is the betrayal.

## Two shapes of betrayal, and the inverse plant

A **twist** betrayal is hidden: the act happened off-screen, long ago, and the plants carry
it under cover until the reveal. A **corrosion** betrayal is visible: loyalty erodes in
witnessed steps, each act a little harder for the loyal character to excuse, usually with
someone whispering the other side. The twist produces surprise; corrosion produces tragedy.
They are planted differently — the twist's plants hide, corrosion's plants are the steps
themselves, shown plainly — and a plan names which one it is building.

The same double reading can also run backwards. A **fair suspicion** plants absences,
evasions and vanishing objects that read as betrayal, and then resolves to loyalty: the
character was being pressured and refused. It tests the audience's trust instead of breaking
it, and it is the right shape when the character must remain someone the player relies on.
Its cost is lingering distrust, so it resolves well before the finale.

Choose the shape with the replay in mind. A friend revealed as a traitor poisons every warm
scene they were in, retroactively and for good. When the campaign needs its friendships to
survive the reveal, let an institution — the league, the employer, the paperwork — betray,
and let the friend be the one tested by it; the audience keeps loving the people while
hating the machine.

## Procedure

1. **Write the reveal scene first** and list every fact it asserts about the betrayer's
   past: when they turned, why, who they served, what they did on the protagonist's behalf
   that was actually on the other side's.
2. **List every earlier scene the betrayer appears in.** That list is the planting surface.
   A scene the betrayer is in without a plant is a hole in the pattern; mark it.
3. **Assign each scene one plant**, drawn from the reveal's fact list and expressed through
   one of the double-reading sources. Vary the source across scenes; five absences in a row
   is a pattern the audience will name before the reveal does.
4. **Escalate gently.** Early plants are nearly invisible; later ones are closer to the
   surface, so that a sharp player begins to suspect a unit or so before the reveal. A
   suspicion that arrives just before the reveal is not a failure — it is the reveal landing
   in two strokes, and it is far better than a reveal no one could have reached.
5. **Give the motive its own plants.** The audience must be able to believe not only that
   the betrayer did it but why. A betrayal with an unplanted motive reads as plot
   convenience even when the act was perfectly planted; this is where a fair grievance
   earns its place.
6. **Re-read backwards.** After the reveal is written, read every planted scene in reverse
   order and check that each one, with the reveal known, reads as inevitable. A plant that
   still reads as innocent after the reveal is a plant that failed.

## Decision rules

- **When the betrayer appears in a scene, the scene carries a plant.** No exceptions without
  a written reason, because every exception is a scene a player will remember as evidence
  of innocence.
- **When a plant only works if the player hears one specific line, carry it in a second
  channel as well** — a visible act, an object, a mechanic — because lines are what players
  skip.
- **When the plant is in optional content, it supports the reveal; it cannot carry it.** The
  critical-path plants alone must be enough.
- **When playtest players call the betrayal more than a unit early, reduce exposure of the
  late plants, not the count.** Removing plants buys surprise with inevitability, and the
  exchange rate is bad.
- **When the audience may already know the reveal** — an adaptation of known material, a
  replaying player, a spoiled secret — **get surprise from order rather than secrecy.**
  Rearranging when the plants and the reveal arrive restores the sense of surprise for an
  audience that knows the destination.
- **When the payoff is meant to be dreaded rather than discovered, plant it loud.** A
  collection the audience sees coming and cannot stop is a promise kept, and the playtest
  goal flips from "nobody guessed" to "everybody guessed and dreaded it".
- **Frame the betrayer's arc with large events at its opening and its close,** so the
  planted middle sits inside a frame the audience remembers.
- **When the betrayal is generated or drafted by many hands, keep the plant list in the
  plan,** not in each writer's head, because density is a property of the whole campaign
  that no single scene's author can see.

A plan that shows a plant in every shared scene has proven the pattern exists on paper. It
has not proven the audience perceives it at the intended strength — the plants may be too
quiet to register or too loud to cover — which is
[structural proof is necessary and never sufficient](../../../../_laws.md#structural-proof-is-never-sufficient):
the plant ledger is the necessary rung, and a cold read and a play are the rungs above it.

## When not to use this

- **For a reveal designed to be unguessable on purpose**, such as a horror shock or a
  random-event twist. Those are not reveals about a character's history and need no
  history to support them; but they should then not claim to be.
- **For a minor reveal.** Planting every scene for a fact that changes nothing about a
  relationship spends cover on a payoff that does not need it.
- **When the character is not in enough scenes to carry a pattern.** A betrayer who appears
  twice cannot be densely planted; either give them more scenes or choose a different
  betrayer.

## Evidence status

The density rule — every earlier scene between the pair hinting at what is coming — and the
rearrangement rule for an audience that knows the source rest on a primary statement by the
creator of a prestige animated adaptation, read directly (High in the source dossier). The
institution-betrays, friend-is-tested rule rests on a mix of primary creator statements and
secondary script analysis (High in the dossier, though its generalisation is the dossier's
own). The corrosion shape rests on a secondary character essay (Medium-High); the dread
payoff rests on a design-risk note in a game-narrative dossier with no external source
(the dossier's inference, Low); the fair-suspicion shape is a dossier proposal, not an
observed practice. The procedure, the plant sources and the re-read step are practitioner
craft. None of it has been tested in a played game yet.
