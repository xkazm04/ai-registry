# Cut the thread that cannot pay off

The named concern: every setup is a promise, and a promise the locked plan cannot keep is
cut at plan time — before it costs scenes, recordings, translations and the audience's
attention. The audit runs in both directions: a payoff without a plant is unearned, and a
plant without a payoff is a debt.

## Why audiences keep the ledger

An audience treats anything given weight as a promise. A character introduced with a
close-up, a locked box, a named rival, an unexplained scar, a line delivered with a pause —
each opens an account, and the audience carries it until it is closed. Writers who forget a
thread have not closed it; they have defaulted on it, and the audience notices at the end,
when the campaign stops and the account is still open. An ending that leaves several
weighted threads unresolved is experienced as incomplete even when its main line resolves
perfectly, because the audience's attention was split across all of them.

Threads also cost the present. Each open thread takes screen time, consumes reveal budget,
and competes for the audience's attention with the threads that will pay. A campaign
carrying five threads to pay off two has spent three-fifths of its setup on nothing.

## The two traps: later, and everything

The first tempting case is the thread held "for later" — a second season, a sequel, an
expansion. It feels free: the thread costs nothing now and might be valuable then. It is not
free. It costs the current arc's attention, and when later arrives it arrives with a new
plan, new constraints and often a shorter runway, and the held threads are now obligations
the new plan did not choose.

The second is the instructive one, because it survives a locked ending. A final instalment
keeps every thread it has inherited and every one its writers love, and plans to land them
all in the finale. Its ending can have been fixed for years and still the material arrives
at production larger than the runtime: the season compresses what it can, trims what it
cannot — often the action and the aftermaths, which are the cheapest to cut and the most
expensive to lose — and is received as rushed, with too much going on. The showrunners'
own description of that failure is a bag packed with more than it holds. The lesson is
that **the lock and the cut are separate disciplines**: a fixed ending tells you which
threads can pay off, and nothing makes you act on it except a cut made at plan time.

When threads compete for the cut, cut the conceptual before the human. Threads about the
world's hidden machinery, its mythology or its politics in the abstract are forgiven when
absent; threads about what two people owe each other are remembered when dropped.

A thread may legitimately cross into a later instalment under two conditions, both
required: it is **declared** in the plan as deliberately open, and the current arc's
**emotional ending does not depend on it**. A declared open thread is a hook; an undeclared
one is a default.

## Procedure

1. **Inventory every setup** in the plan: every introduced character, object, mystery, rival,
   debt and question that the material gives weight to.
2. **Map each to a payoff unit** in the beat sheet. A setup with a payoff is kept. A setup
   without one is either assigned a payoff now, demoted to texture (removed of its weight —
   no close-up, no pause, no naming), declared open for a later instalment under the two
   conditions above, or cut.
3. **Map each payoff back to its setups.** A payoff with no setup is a deus ex machina in
   waiting; it is planted or replaced.
4. **Repeat the audit at every re-plan,** and in particular whenever the final card changes:
   a card change orphans exactly the plants that served the old card, and they are the ones
   nobody remembers to cut.

## Decision rules

- **When a thread has no payoff in the plan, decide its fate now.** "We'll figure it out"
  is the answer that produces the default. An unassigned thread is recorded as
  *unassigned*, never as open, because silence about a thread reads as approval.
- **When a thread is loved but cannot pay off, demote it to texture before cutting it.**
  Removing its weight often keeps the world-building and drops the promise.
- **When holding a thread for a sequel, write down the sequel's payoff now** — a sentence is
  enough. A thread whose payoff cannot be stated even in a sentence is not being held; it is
  being avoided.
- **When cutting, cut the setup scene's weight, not only the setup scene.** A thread
  survives its scene being deleted if another scene still names it.
- **When the payoff is in optional content, the setup's weight must match.** A setup given
  critical-path weight and paid off in a side mission is a default for most players.
- **When the plan's runtime is fixed, count before writing.** Majors, payoffs and the
  aftermaths they need are summed against the units available; an overrun found in the plan
  costs a cut, the same overrun found in the edit costs the aftermaths.
- **When the ending carries a price, no hook may undo it.** A sequel hook that restores what
  the finale cost is a thread that pays off by retracting the payoff.
- **When a pitch for a new thread arrives mid-production, the default is no.** Thread
  pitches are cheap and their cost lands later; a high rejection rate is a sign the plan is
  being defended, not that the room lacks ideas.

The two-directional audit is
[declaring an input is not consuming it](../../../../_laws.md#declaring-an-input-is-not-consuming-it)
applied to story: a census of readers, not of writers — a setup counts as paid only when a
payoff consumes it. And an unexamined thread is
[unmeasured, which is not a pass](../../../../_laws.md#unmeasured-is-not-a-pass): the plan
labels it unassigned until someone decides.

## When not to use this

- **For texture.** A world needs detail that is not a promise — a street name, a stranger's
  argument in the background. Texture carries no weight and needs no payoff; the audit's
  first job is to tell texture from setup, and the tell is weight.
- **For a deliberately unresolved ending** whose ambiguity is the point. An ending that
  leaves its central question open is a design decision and is written as the final card;
  it is not a collection of forgotten threads.
- **For player-authored threads** in a systemic game, where the player opens accounts the
  writers never saw. The rule binds what the writers give weight to.

## Evidence status

The cautionary case — a final season of a prestige animated series whose ending was fixed
from the start and which its own showrunners describe as overloaded and short of time —
rests on primary showrunner quotes (High), with the reported length of the finale's early
cut from a secondary summary (Medium) and the link between overloading and the season's
mixed reception an inference the dossier states without measurement. The dossier's claim
that overstuffing is the most commonly reported failure of a final season has no source
behind it and is not relied on here. The human-over-conceptual preference is the
co-creator's own contrast (High as a quote). The high-rejection-rate rule leans on a quest
director's statement read only through a journalist's summary (Medium). The setup-as-promise
framing and the two-directional audit are long-standing screenwriting craft (the
setup-and-payoff tradition, usually written in reverse from the payoff). None of it has been
tested in a played game yet.
