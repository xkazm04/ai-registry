# Ending-first narrative structure

The emotion of a final scene is not stored in the final scene. It is deposited earlier, a
line and a gesture at a time, and the finale withdraws it. A campaign written forward —
each scene the most interesting next thing — arrives at its end holding whatever it
happened to deposit, and the finale has to spend that balance. It is almost never enough,
and the shortfall is covered by the three things an unearned ending always reaches for: a
speech that announces what the audience should feel, a coincidence that resolves what the
characters could not, and a reveal nobody was given any grounds to believe.

This subject owns the opposite order. Decide the last image first and lock it; derive the
campaign backwards from it; plant every later reveal in the scenes before it; give each
unit of the campaign one major reveal; cut every thread the locked plan cannot pay off; and
let the theme be carried by the shape of the whole rather than said by anyone in it. The
artifact this produces is not prose. It is a back-planned beat sheet: a ledger of payoffs
and of the plants that pay for them, written from the last unit to the first.

## The ending is a specification, not a destination

The naive reading of "know your ending" is a destination: a rough sense of where things go,
held in the lead writer's head. A destination can be revised quietly every time the writing
gets interesting, and each quiet revision orphans the plants that were made for the old
one. Nobody decides to orphan them, which is why nobody cuts them.

Ending-first means something stricter: the ending is a **specification** that an earlier
scene can be checked against. The test is whether two writers handed only the final card
would independently list the same things that must have happened before it. "The hero
wins" fails that test; it constrains nothing. "She crosses the line alone in his car, the
passenger seat empty except for his jacket" passes it: the car must be his, he must be
gone, the jacket must have been established, and the two of them must once have shared that
cab. A concrete final image generates its own prerequisites, and the prerequisites are the
plan. The card that generates the most is the last moment of the campaign's central
relationship, not its last plot event: it shows the price the victory cost, paid directly
for that victory rather than by an unrelated tragedy, and one small image of what survived.
In a game it adds a constraint a film does not have — the decisive act it follows belongs
to the player's character and is performed with the verb the whole game has trained, because
a finale whose key move is made by someone else reads as agency taken away.

Locking is what makes backwards planning cheap. The final card is the single authority the
plan derives from, which is
[one authority per quantity](../../../_laws.md#one-authority-per-quantity) applied to story:
an unofficial second ending in a writer's head is a second model, and the disagreement
between the two stays invisible until a scene is written for the wrong one. The card may
change — a lock is not a vow — but changing it is a formal re-plan that re-derives every
plant, never a drift. See [lock-the-final-card-first](./techniques/lock-the-final-card-first.md).

## Surprise forward, inevitability backward

A major reveal is read twice. Forward, on first experience, it must surprise. Backward, on
recall or replay, it must feel inevitable — the reaction that marks a reveal as earned is
not "I didn't see that coming" but "of course". Both properties come from the same plants:
each plant carries an innocent meaning in its own scene and a second meaning that only the
reveal unlocks. A plant with only the second meaning telegraphs the reveal; a plant with
only the first is not a plant at all. The two naive failures sit at either end — the twist
from nowhere, which is a retcon wearing a reveal's clothes, and the twist everyone called
two units early, which was planted without cover.

Not every payoff wants surprise, and confusing the two kinds is a common planning error. A
**dread payoff** — the creditor finally collecting, the loss the campaign has been walking
toward — is meant to be predicted: the audience sees it coming, cannot stop it, and feels
it as a promise kept rather than a twist. Its plants are deliberately louder, and its
playtest question inverts: did players predict it, and did they dread it? Mark each payoff
in the plan as surprise or dread, because the same plant is a failure under one label and
exactly right under the other.

Betrayal is the hard case and therefore the paradigm. A betrayal is a claim about a
character's whole history — that they were, all along, someone the audience did not see —
and a claim about a whole history is only believed if the whole history supports it. One
clue in the second chapter reads at the reveal as a coincidence; a behaviour present in
every scene the betrayer shares with the protagonist reads as character. So the rule is
density, not cleverness: every earlier scene with the betrayer in it carries a line or an
act that holds up under both readings. The same density has a price worth choosing
deliberately: a friend revealed as a traitor poisons every warm scene they were in,
retroactively and for good, which is why a campaign that needs its friendships intact on
replay lets an institution do the betraying and lets the friend be tested by it. See
[plant-the-betrayal-in-every-earlier-scene](./techniques/plant-the-betrayal-in-every-earlier-scene.md).

## Interactive play makes plants harder to place

A viewer sees every scene in order. A player does not. They skip cutscenes, miss optional
conversations, play a route the writer did not walk, and come back after a week. Four rules
follow, and they are what separates this subject in a game from the same craft on a page.

A plant that a critical-path payoff depends on must itself sit on every route to that
payoff; a plant in optional content can only pay off optional content. A plant worth having
is carried in more than one channel — a line, an image, a mechanic — so that a skipped
cutscene does not delete it. The strongest plant the medium has is a **mechanic**: something
the player did with their own hands, unremarked at the time, that the finale turns on, and
which no amount of skipping removes. And a plant that pays off sessions later needs a second
touch in between, because a player's memory of a three-day-old line is not an author's.

The medium also lets the finale be a **test** rather than only a scene, and that changes what
backwards planning produces. When the last event grades the campaign — which allies turn up,
what the vehicle carries, which verbs the player has mastered — the finale's needs are
payoffs in their own right, and each one generates earlier beats: a beat in which the
capability is acquired and a beat in which it is proven trustworthy. A finale that is merely
the hardest event, to which nothing earlier contributes, has been planned forwards whatever
order the documents were written in.

## One unit, one major reveal

A unit is the span a player consumes in one sitting and judges as a whole — a chapter, an
episode, a mission, a race event. A major reveal is one that recontextualises a
relationship, a motive or the stakes, not one that merely supplies a fact. Each unit carries
one, because a reveal needs room on both sides: the build that makes it land and the
aftermath in which the characters, and the player, absorb it. A second major reveal in the
same unit lands in the first one's aftermath and diminishes both. A unit with none is filler
unless it was designed as a breather. And a campaign whose reveals keep topping each other
trains the audience to discount every revelation — including the finale's, which is the one
that cannot afford it. See [one-major-reveal-per-unit](./techniques/one-major-reveal-per-unit.md).

## A thread is a promise, and audiences keep the ledger

Every setup is a promise, and the audience keeps the account even when the writers do not.
A thread the locked plan cannot pay off is cut at plan time, while it is still cheap — before
its scenes are written, voiced, translated and built around. The naive instinct keeps it
open for later — a sequel, a second season, an expansion — or keeps everything and plans to
land it all in the finale. The second instinct is the more instructive failure, because a
locked ending does not protect against it. A final instalment can hold its closing scene
fixed for years and still arrive at production with more threads than runtime, compress
what it can, trim what it cannot, and be received as rushed and overstuffed. Locking the
card and cutting the threads are two disciplines, not one, and the first gives no credit
toward the second. When threads compete for the cut, the conceptual ones — lore,
mythology, the system's hidden machinery — go before the human ones, because relationships
are what an audience remembers and lore is what it forgives.

The audit runs in both directions, and it is
[declaring an input is not consuming it](../../../_laws.md#declaring-an-input-is-not-consuming-it)
in narrative form: a payoff with no plant is unearned, and a plant with no payoff is a
declared input nothing ever reads — it costs scenes, and it charges the audience attention
for a return that never comes. A thread deliberately left open for a later instalment is
legitimate only when it is declared as such and the current arc's emotional ending does not
depend on it. See [cut-the-thread-that-cannot-pay-off](./techniques/cut-the-thread-that-cannot-pay-off.md).

## Theme is the shape, not the speech

Theme stated in dialogue is a lecture; theme carried by structure is felt, and structure is
the one thing an audience cannot skim past. The structural carriers are few and reliable: an
opening image that poses a question and a final card that answers it; a motif that recurs
with its meaning flipped; a situation that recurs with a different decision each time; a
prologue unit deliberately unlike the rest, so that the gap between them is the argument;
parallel threads that argue different answers to the same question; a plan whose very form
enacts the argument. The working rule is to write the theme as a question rather than a moral, make the
final card the answer, and treat a unit that bears on the question in no way as a candidate
for cutting unless it is the designed breather. See
[theme-carried-by-structure](./techniques/theme-carried-by-structure.md).

## The plan is a ledger, and the ledger is written backwards

The back-planned beat sheet starts at the last unit and walks to the first. Each row names
the unit's major reveal, the payoffs it delivers, the plants it carries and which later
payoff each one serves, and where the unit stands on the theme's question. Some beats are
**fixed** — the final card, each unit's reveal, and every plant those depend on — and
everything else is connective tissue the writers may change freely without touching the
plan. Locking only the fixed beats is what keeps the plan from becoming brittle; locking
everything is how a plan gets abandoned in the second week of writing. Player variance
pushes the same way: the more of a campaign is sequenced by play or picked by a system at
run time, the fewer beats can be pinned, and the pinned ones are never left to the picker.

The sheet admits mechanical checks that a prose read cannot do reliably: every payoff has
plants in at least two earlier units, one of them well before it; every plant has a payoff;
every unit has exactly one major reveal; every critical-path payoff's plants sit on the
critical path. Those checks prove the structure. They do not prove the ending moves anyone,
which is [structural proof is necessary and never sufficient](../../../_laws.md#structural-proof-is-never-sufficient)
pointed at story: a sheet that passes every check is an ending that has been paid for on
paper, and only a read, and finally a play, says whether the payment cleared. See
[back-planned-beat-sheet](./techniques/back-planned-beat-sheet.md).

## Failure modes of the naive reading

- **Writing the finale first and then writing past it.** A finale drafted early and never
  used as a check is a destination with a word count. Writing it is not locking it.
- **Planting once.** One clue, early, that nobody remembers at the reveal.
- **Planting where players are not.** A betrayal planted in an optional conversation pays
  off for the fraction who had it and is a retcon for everyone else.
- **Hoarding threads for later.** Every open thread costs the current arc attention and
  scenes, and later rarely arrives with the plan the thread needed.
- **Reveal stacking.** Two majors in one unit, or a campaign of escalating twists, each one
  discounting the last.
- **The theme speech.** A character who explains what the story meant is a confession that
  the structure did not.
- **Locking the tissue.** A plan that fixes every scene breaks on the first rewrite and is
  then abandoned whole, plants and all.

## What this cannot decide

Whether the locked ending is the right one is taste, and nothing here supplies it. Whether
the lines that carry the plants are well written is dialogue craft. Whether players notice
the plants — and whether they notice them too early — is a playtest question no plan
answers. And whether a campaign that is structurally paid for actually moves anyone is the
judgment the whole ledger exists to make likely, and cannot make certain.

## Boundaries

**Against branching-narrative-graph-validation.** That subject proves a conversation graph
is playable: every node reachable, every declared ending attainable, every variable written
before it is read, every choice a choice in state. This subject decides what the campaign
must plant and pay off, and where. The two meet at one point — the rule that a plant must
sit on every route to its payoff is answered by exactly the reachability walk the neighbour
owns. The rule for picking: if the question is whether a player *can get to* a node or an
ending, it is the neighbour's; if it is whether the ending is *earned* when they get there,
it is this one's. A graph can validate perfectly and deliver a finale nobody was prepared
for, and a perfectly back-planned campaign can still softlock in its third scene.

**Against combat-pacing-and-dramatic-arc.** That subject owns tension over encounter time —
a curve built from intensity and threat, mined for climax, comeback and dead zone, over
seconds or minutes of play. This subject owns arc over a campaign, and its unit of account
is story information — a plant, a reveal, a payoff — not intensity. The rule: when the
measurement is a curve over the time of one encounter, it is the neighbour's; when the
quantity is what the audience knows and the span is the whole campaign, it is this one's.
The two do touch where a race or a fight *is* a unit's reveal: the neighbour says whether the
event peaks; this subject says what the peak has to reveal and what earlier events had to
plant for it.
