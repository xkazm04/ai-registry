# Take it after a win

The named concern: when the story takes away something the player built, take it at the peak
of a victory, through the terms rather than through play, by a named agent with a motive the
player already knows, so that the player blames the agent and not the game.

## Why the built thing is the right thing to take

Ownership raises value: what a person holds is worth more to them than the same thing they
could acquire. Building raises it again — what a person made themselves is valued above
the same object handed to them — and the premium depends on completion: a half-built or failed
thing carries little of it. A game manufactures exactly this object over hours: the car tuned
across ten events, painted, named, carrying a win count. By the time the story needs a loss, the
most valuable thing in the player's head is something they finished building and still hold. A
thing the story gave them and they never touched is the wrong object; its loss costs nothing.

That is why the take works, and also why it is the point in the game most likely to turn a
player against it. The same value that makes the loss felt makes a badly attributed loss
unforgivable.

## Take the will, not the skill

Players assign a failure to themselves, to the game, or to circumstance, and players who take a
failure as their own rate a game higher than those who blame the game. A scripted loss is not
the player's failure, so it needs a fourth address — an agent inside the fiction — and two
choices decide whether the player accepts it.

**The channel.** If the player loses the thing in an event they could have won, they blame the
game: their skill was tested and the result was overruled. If they lose it to a contract,
a lien or a claim they could never have beaten, they blame the holder. The take defeats the
player's will, not their skill, and it happens in a scene, never in a race the player was
allowed to win and then loses anyway.

**The moment.** Taken after a defeat, the removal arrives through the same channel as the
defeat and is filed under the same heading: the game is against me. Taken immediately after a
win, it cannot be read as a consequence of play, because play just went well. Something else
took it, and the scene shows who. The contrast between the high and the take is the drama.

## Procedure

1. **Choose the object** for how completely the player has made it theirs: named, customised,
   used in the events that mattered. Taking money reads as a tax; taking the car reads as theft.
2. **Place the take late enough to sting and early enough to rebuild** — after ownership has
   accumulated for most of the arc, with room left for a lean stretch and a recovery before the
   final act.
3. **Stage it right after a decisive win**, in the same flow, before the reward is spent — a
   win the event structure guarantees, not one the field happened to allow.
4. **Show what is being taken**: its name, its paint, its parts, its record, on screen as it goes.
   The player's investment is the content of the scene.
5. **Show the taker**, in one line, with the motive the player was already shown, and let the
   people who cannot stop it be present and powerless. No monologue: one line, one stamp on the
   ledger, the slot emptying, the sound dropping.
6. **Convert the loss into a goal on the same screen**: the ledger's next threat line names what
   was taken, who holds it, and what getting it back requires.
7. **Keep the economy whole.** The take removes an object of the story; the player leaves able to
   start the next event, and the object is held, not destroyed, so recovering it is possible.

## Decision rules

- **Anchor the take to an event that only a win clears.** If the event before the take advances
  on any qualifying result, "after a win" is a statistic of the field, not a design, and an
  ordinary balance pass will move it without anyone aiming at the story. A boss, a must-win story
  race or a gate retried until won makes the timing hold by rule. When no such event sits where
  the story needs the take, either make one or measure the share on the reference path and state
  it; a take that lands straight after a last place is the piling-on the technique exists to
  prevent, whatever the plan said.
- **When the player could plausibly have prevented the loss by playing better, either let them
  prevent it or do not stage it there.** A loss that looks preventable and was not is the clearest
  possible message that agency is fake.
- **When the plant on the ledger implies a condition the player can meet** — "if the debt is
  called, the car goes" — taking it after the player met the condition reads as cheating unless
  the creditor's breach is shown as a breach. Align the plant with the channel.
- **Take one thing, once.** Repeated seizures teach the player not to invest, which destroys the
  value the technique depends on.
- **When the player paid real money for the object, do not take it.** The fiction cannot absorb
  that grievance.
- **When the taken object can return in the antagonist's hands** — the player's own car, carrying
  every upgrade they bought, driven against them — consider it for the final act. The investment
  turned against its owner is the debt made physical.

## When not to use this

A loss at the very opening is a different instrument: an inciting loss of something the player
has not yet invested in works as a goal rather than a grievance, and needs neither the
after-a-win timing nor the ownership premium. In a game whose ownership is light — a rotating
roster, a loadout chosen per run — there is no built thing to take. And never use it twice in one
arc: the second take is predictable and reads as a pattern of the game, which is the
attribution the technique exists to avoid.

## Evidence status

The value premium of ownership and of self-built, completed objects comes from primary
experimental work in behavioural economics and consumer psychology, read for this subject. The
ownership gap survives replication, but its size depends on how value is elicited and its
loss-aversion mechanism is now disputed, so it is a direction, not a multiplier. The self-built
premium's loss on destruction was measured when people undid their own work; nobody else took it.
The attribution of failure to self, game or circumstance, and the higher rating given by players
who take failure as their own, comes from a game-studies paper on difficulty: an association in
one online sample of 85 players who reported their own reason for failing, not a measured cause. The will-not-skill
rule is the source dossier's own synthesis of sourced parts (medium); the shipped games it cites
show a taken object as a campaign's spine and a defeat turned into a revenge goal, not the
after-a-win timing itself. The staging details and the placement late in the arc are dossier
proposals for one unplayed design. The win-gated anchor was measured on that design's code: over
seeded reference careers its qualifier-anchored take followed a win between a quarter and four
in five of the time across four balance builds, while its win-gated events were wins every time;
a training-data lane reached the same rule independently. Those are reference drivers, not
players. None of this has been tested in a played game for this subject yet.
