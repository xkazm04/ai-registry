# Survive the lean stretch

The named concern: the stretch of play after a major loss is deliberately leaner than what came
before, and the player must survive it. It is short, it is winnable on the intended path, it
shows the way back, it teaches something, and surviving it is what makes the final act feel
earned.

## Why this beat is the dangerous one

After a scripted loss the player holds two things at once: the strongest grievance the story has
given them and the weakest position the game has put them in. Either alone is manageable.
Together they produce the quit — not at the loss itself, which is a scene, but at the first event
after it that the player cannot get through, which they read as the game confirming the loss
was unfair.

Both naive designs fail. **Restoring power at once** — a better replacement waiting in the next
scene — makes the loss free, and the player learns that losses in this story are cosmetic.
**Leaving the field as it was**, tuned for the power the player no longer has, makes the loss a
wall. The third naive design is subtler: **skipping the stretch**, so the player meets the final
act in the reduced state without ever having played it. The loss is then felt for the first time
in the event that matters most, and the player learns the replacement under the highest stakes.

## Shape of the stretch

**Short.** One to a few events, not an act. Long enough that the reduced state is felt in the
hands; short enough that it ends before grievance curdles into abandonment.

**Leaner, not hopeless.** The player's power relative to the field drops, and the events are
chosen so that a player on the intended path can still finish well enough to progress. The dip is
in the ratio, not in the guarantee: the economy's floor holds throughout.

**Story on finishing, not on winning.** In the stretch, the story advances when the player
completes an event, and winning gates only money and rank. A difficulty wall in front of a story
beat is the trough's characteristic failure; keying the beat to completion removes it without
making the events free. A loss in the stretch is still content — a line from the mechanic, a
gloat from a rival — never a bare retry prompt.

**With a visible way back.** The ledger's threat line, from the first event after the loss, names
what recovery requires and shows progress toward it. A trough without a visible exit is the
shape players quit in.

**With something the lean state teaches.** The stretch is strongest when the reduced state is
different, not merely worse: a loaner with another strength, a borrowed ability, a route the
strong car never needed. Where the final act will use a new tool, the stretch is where it is first
used, in an event that cannot be lost — a shakedown with anxious commentary — so the verb is
learned before it is needed in anger.

## Decision rules

- **Declare the dip to whoever owns the progression curve.** A deliberate step down in power is a
  piecewise join, and an undeclared join will be found by a curve audit as a wall. Declared, it
  becomes a stated design with a stated depth and length the audit checks against, and its deficit
  is reported apart from the ordinary target ratio rather than averaged into it.
- **Prove survivability on the reference path before calling the stretch tuned.** That a dip
  exists in the data proves nothing about whether a player gets through it; a simulated and then a
  played run of the stretch is the evidence, and a curve with the right numbers is a structural
  pass — [structural proof is necessary and never sufficient](../../../../_laws.md#structural-proof-is-never-sufficient)
  applied to a story beat.
- **When the stretch includes help, make the help visible and partial.** An ally who lends a car
  belongs here; an ally who wins the events for the player does not.
- **Do not add new antagonist pressure during the stretch.** The leash has just been pulled; a
  second rewrite in the trough reads as piling on.
- **End the stretch with a result the player earns in the reduced state**, before power returns, so
  the recovery is something the player did rather than something the plot delivered.

## When not to use this

When the loss did not reduce the player's power — a story loss with no mechanical shadow — a lean
stretch invented to make it felt is a punishment with no cause. When difficulty is adaptive by
design, express the dip in that system's terms, or the adaptation will quietly undo it.

## Evidence status

That losing must be content rather than a wall rests on primary statements by narrative leads of
two shipped games, and the warning that difficulty walls block story on a primary interview (high
in the source dossier). Teaching a key tool in a no-stakes activity before it is needed rests on a
low-confidence fan-wiki summary of developer commentary. The short junk-car stretch and the
shakedown are dossier proposals for one unplayed design, and the rule to declare the dip to the
curve follows from this bundle's treatment of piecewise curves. No lean stretch has been run
against a reference driver or played by anyone for this subject yet.
