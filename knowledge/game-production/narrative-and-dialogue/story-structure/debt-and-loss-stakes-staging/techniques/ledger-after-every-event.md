# Ledger after every event

The named concern: give the stakes a fixed, short, in-world place the player reads after
every event, so that what they stand to lose is in front of them at the one moment their
attention is guaranteed. The ledger is the narrative channel of a progression game. A
cutscene is watched once and skipped thereafter; the post-event screen is read every time,
because it answers the question the player actually has — what just happened to me. An
abstract debt mentioned in a scene is forgotten by the next session; a line item that moves
every night is felt.

## What a ledger line is

A ledger is three to five lines in the voice of someone in the world who keeps the account:
a fixer, a bookkeeper, the creditor's clerk, the player's own handwriting. Each line is a
change, a balance or a threat, and each is about something the player can name. "Purse 4,200.
Their cut 1,260. Still owed 18,000. The car is collateral." is a ledger. A paragraph of
narration about how the night went is not, and a table of eleven statistics is a receipt.

The three kinds of line do different work. A **change line** says what this event moved. A
**balance line** says where the player now stands on the thing the story turns on. A **threat
line** says what is next at risk and who holds it. A ledger with no threat line has reported
the past and staked nothing; a ledger that is all threat has stopped being an account and
become a cutscene in a smaller font.

## The player's choices must move it

A ledger the player cannot affect is a weather report. The stake is felt only when the
player's own decisions visibly move a line: which event they entered, whether they took the
riskier purse, which part they bought, whether they took an ally's cash or the ally's part.
The change line is where that shows, and it shows best when the direction of the change has a
consistent visual signal the player learns to dread or hope for. Where the stake is in bands
rather than in exact figures — comfortable, tight, overdue, called — the band is state the rest
of the game reads, so that characters who never appear on the ledger still react to where it
stands.

## The keeper is a character

When the ledger is kept by the antagonist's office, it is the antagonist's voice between
confrontations, and the cheapest way to keep a villain present who is otherwise met only at
the end: a signature under the account, a remark in the margin, one corrected figure. The
register matters more than the content. A creditor's ledger speaks of what is owed, what is
fair and what was agreed, never of force, because a debt's power is that it makes the debtor
feel they are the one in the wrong.

## The ledger and the receipt share one source

Where the game also shows a receipt — the economy's statement of gross, deductions and net —
the ledger is a reading of the same numbers, never a second computation of them. When the
story says the creditor took thirty percent and the receipt shows twenty-eight, the player
trusts the receipt and concludes the story is decoration. Every figure on the ledger is drawn
from the quantity the economy owns, which is
[one authority per quantity](../../../../_laws.md#one-authority-per-quantity) applied to a debt.
The reverse also binds: a narrative quantity the economy does not hold — a favour owed, a
grudge, a claim in kind — is declared and stored once, as state, and the ledger reads it; it is
never typed into the line as prose. A creditor who quotes figures in his own unit of account is
a strong characterisation and a dangerous one: the conversion is shown, and the quoted unit is
never spendable, or the game has grown a second currency through its story.

Each number carries what it counts.
[A number carries its unit and its basis](../../../../_laws.md#a-number-carries-its-unit-and-basis)
applies to fiction as firmly as to an engine: a player who cannot tell whether a debt went up
because of interest, a fee or a rewrite cannot read the story the numbers are telling. So the
lines that move the balance are separated — what was paid, what was credited, what was
diverted, what interest added — rather than netted into one figure that hides the creditor's
hand.

## Procedure

1. **Choose the keeper.** One voice owns the ledger for the whole game, and the voice is a
   character choice: a creditor's clerk makes every line a reminder of who holds the leash; a
   friend's handwriting makes every line a worry. Do not let it drift scene to scene.
2. **Fix the shape**: at most five lines, in a stable order — change, balance, threat. The
   player learns where to look, which is what lets a single altered line be noticed.
3. **Bind every figure** to the owning quantity, with its unit, and keep the components that
   move the balance as separate lines.
4. **Write the threat line per event**, from state, so it can escalate. A threat line that is
   the same string for twenty events is invisible by the third.
5. **Keep the history**: lifetime totals and the last account remain reachable, so the player
   can check the arithmetic — and catch the creditor cheating, if the story needs them to.
6. **Reserve the ledger for the stake.** Unrelated rewards and unlocks go elsewhere, because
   every line that is not about the stake dilutes the one that is.

## Decision rules

- **When an event changes nothing on the stake, still show the ledger**, with the balance and
  threat lines only. Its absence after one event teaches the player it is optional.
- **When the story needs a line the economy contradicts, change the story.** The economy's
  number is the one the player can check.
- **When the ledger is about to carry a twist, leave its shape alone** and change one line. The
  player notices the one line because the shape never moves.
- **When the economy guarantees a floor, let the ledger show it holding.** A protected minimum
  take-home that appears on the account is the game telling the player, in the creditor's own
  book, that the leash has a limit.
- **When a line will not read in a few seconds on the target display, cut it**, because the
  ledger is read at a distance, between events, by someone who wants to play the next one.

## When not to use this

In a game with no persistent position between events — a single-session game, an arcade
ladder with no carry-over — there is nothing for a ledger to keep, and a post-event screen
pretending to keep an account is noise. In a game whose stake is a relationship rather than a
quantity, a number-shaped ledger flattens it into a meter; carry the relationship in behaviour
and keep the ledger for what can be counted.

## Evidence status

The ritual end-of-day account that the player's work moves is documented from a primary source
on a shipped game whose stakes are a household budget (rated high in the source dossier). The
creditor's language of obligation rests on a primary anthropological text on debt; its
application to games is inference. The ledger as the antagonist's voice, the debt bands and the
separated balance lines are design proposals from the dossier and from one unplayed design,
not observed results. The binding of story figures to economy figures is a law of this bundle.
None of this has been tested in a played game for this subject yet; every claim about what a
player will notice is design intent until someone has played it.
