# The last act is the player's

The named concern: the decisive act that breaks the leash, recovers what was taken or defeats the
antagonist is performed by the player, with the game's core verb, as a contest they could lose.
Everything else in this subject takes power away from the player on purpose; the ending is where
it comes back, and it only comes back if the player's own hands bring it.

## The five ways an ending is borrowed

**Watched.** The final confrontation is a cutscene. The player has spent the whole game in the
verb and is shown the payoff in another medium; the message is that the leash was never theirs
to cut.

**Given.** An ally lands the decisive blow, pays the debt, or makes the crucial decision. Players
read this as stolen agency, and they say so: an ending in which a supporting character makes the
protagonist's defining choice is the kind a studio revises after release. Allies prepare the final
act; the player performs it.

**Fumbled.** The antagonist defeats themself — overreaches, crashes, is betrayed by a henchman.
In a film, where the audience never had agency, that can satisfy; in a game it tells the player
their effort was incidental.

**Rigged.** The final event cannot be lost. A climax won by turning up is a scene with a
controller.

**Timed out.** The final event is decided by a counter — a lap count, a clock, the most damage
dealt when time expires. A mechanical fallback is sometimes necessary, but if it can award the
win, the decisive act belongs to the timer. The fallback ends the attempt as a draw or a retry and
never names a winner.

## Procedure

1. **State the decisive act in one sentence** with the player as the subject of the verb: the
   player wrecks the creditor's car in the arena, driving the rig their allies built.
2. **Use the core verb**, at its hardest honest level, not a mechanic introduced for the occasion.
   A tool new to the finale was taught in the lean stretch.
3. **Give the allies visible, announced contributions** — they rebuilt the car, they cut the
   lights, they brought the papers — that the player's performance cashes, not replaces.
4. **Make it the last conversation.** The final contest is the culmination of a relationship the
   player has felt throughout; a stronger stranger at the top of a ladder carries no weight.
5. **Close the ledger.** The last entry, in the keeper's voice, settles the debt line the first
   entry opened. A claim voided by victory is recorded as voided, separately from money paid, so
   the account says what happened rather than pretending the debt was settled in cash.
6. **Let the aftermath be short and the player's.**

## Decision rules

- **When a narrative constraint requires someone else to resolve the plot, move that resolution
  before the final act** and leave the final act to the player. The constraint usually concerns
  how, not who.
- **When the final event is hard enough to stop players, make retry cheap and identical** — same
  vehicle, same condition, no consumable charge — and keep it losable. A cheap retry protects the
  player; an unlosable climax protects nobody.
- **When the ending includes a choice, make it with the controls**, after or as part of the
  decisive act; a choice offered from a menu after a cutscene victory is not an act.
- **When the ending should cost something**, the cost is the direct price of the victory, not an
  unrelated tragedy; what the ending charges is the ending's own business, but it must not be
  charged by taking the decisive act away.

## When not to use this

In a story whose point is that the player cannot beat the system — a tragedy by design — the rule
inverts: the player's last act is the choice that makes the tragedy theirs, and the borrowed-ending
failures still apply. A last stand the player cannot win still works when it is played, not
watched, and the payoff later returns to the player's hands. In a game without a decisive confrontation, there is no last act to own, and
inventing one to satisfy this technique is a different game.

## Evidence status

The stolen-agency criticism of an ending decided by a supporting character, and the studio's
revision so the protagonist makes the call, rest on an interview with the game's narrative lead.
In it, the criticism and the account of the revision are the journalist's words; the lead's own
quoted words say only that the studio answered player feedback quickly, so the agency reading is
journalism reporting players, not the studio's stated reason (re-read verbatim 2026-10-09). The ending that hands the player the last shot and waits rests
on critical essays about a shipped game (rated high in the dossier, though the sources are
secondary). The rule against a timer or counter awarding the win is an upward lesson from one
unplayed design's fight rules, and that design's code now refuses the timer, the ally and the
unlosable finale at the rule level. The unwinnable played last stand is a shipped exception both
research lanes named independently. None of this has been tested in a played game for this subject yet.
