# The leash can be rewritten

The named concern: a debt in a story works when it is a relationship of power rather than a
number to pay down, and what makes it one is that the creditor can change the terms. A debt
that can only shrink is a loan, and a loan is a progress bar with a face: once the player has
done the arithmetic, the threat is gone. A debt whose terms the holder can rewrite is a leash.
It directs where the player goes, takes a share of what they win, and tightens when the holder
wants something.

## What a leash does that a loan does not

A loan gives the player a goal. A leash gives the antagonist a **reason to act on the player
between confrontations**: the creditor chose this event, takes this cut, wants this car. It is
how an antagonist who is not on the track is still in every race, and it answers the question
every progression story has to answer — why does the player keep doing what the creditor wants
— without a new compulsion per act.

The creditor's levers are the ones a company town holds: the unit of account, the shop, the
repair prices, the venue, the terms. The creditor speaks only in the language of obligation —
owed, fair, agreed, settled — and never in the language of force, because the leash bites
hardest when the debtor feels the fault is theirs. A creditor who threatens is an obstacle; a
creditor who reminds you what you signed is a leash.

The rewrite is the leash's teeth, and it is the most dangerous instrument in the subject,
because a player who sees terms change for no reason concludes that the game is moving the
goalposts. Anger at the designer and anger at the creditor feel identical from the inside and
produce opposite results: one makes the player want to beat the creditor, the other makes them
want to stop playing.

## When a rewrite lands on the creditor

A rewrite lands where the story needs it when three conditions hold.

**It has a visible basis.** Either the original deal, on the ledger from the first event,
contains the clause that permits it — the right to call the debt, to change the share, to take
collateral — or the rewrite is an open breach that the fiction names as a breach, by a creditor
the player has already caught cheating. Both work. The fine-print rewrite makes the player
think they should have read the terms; the exposed-fraud rewrite makes them know the creditor
was never going to honour them. What fails is the third kind, with neither a clause nor an
established dishonesty behind it, which the player can only attribute to the designer.

**It is motivated**, and the strongest motive is the player's own success. A creditor who
tightens the leash because the player was getting free converts the player's competence into
the antagonist's motive; the leash pulls exactly when the player can see the end of it.

**It is announced on the ledger at the moment it takes effect**, in the keeper's voice, with the
new term and the old term both visible. A rewrite discovered later, by a number that does not
add up, reads as a bug.

## Show it bite someone else first

Before the leash bites the player, let it bite a character the player knows: a lien posted on
a car someone else cares about, a crew stripped of its tools. A witnessed punishment needs no
explanation, and it turns the player's later loss into a promise kept rather than a twist. The
companion rule is that the player is free to leave — and leaving is visibly worse than staying.
A rule that simply says the player cannot leave reads as the game's structure; a door the
player chooses not to walk through is menace.

## The floor the leash never crosses

The leash tightens on what the player is owed, where they are sent and what they keep. It never
tightens through the economy's guarantees: the player can always start the next event, a bad
result still leaves them able to continue, and the interest does not grow on retries. A
creditor who can rewrite the terms into a dead end is not a villain; it is a softlock with
dialogue. The terms are stored as one declared quantity the economy settles against, so the
share the story names and the share the settlement takes are the same number —
[one authority per quantity](../../../../_laws.md#one-authority-per-quantity).

## Decision rules

- **When the player is close to paying the debt off, that is the moment for the decisive
  rewrite.** A goal in view, moved under a visible basis, spends the instrument at full value;
  a rewrite at a random point spends it for less.
- **Rewrite rarely**: once or twice in a story, at act turns. A creditor who rewrites every few
  events teaches the player that the numbers are meaningless, and a player who believes that
  stops reading the ledger.
- **When a rewrite needs a clause the deal did not contain, add the clause now and rewrite
  later**, or establish the creditor's dishonesty now and breach later. Never rewrite first and
  explain afterwards.
- **Keep the debt payable in arithmetic under the current terms.** A debt the player can see is
  unpayable is a fake stake and stops being treated as one. The threat lives in the terms, not
  in an impossible number.
- **Give the player one lever on the leash** — evidence against the creditor, an ally who can
  expose the books, a term they can buy back — so the relationship has two sides and the final
  act has something to cut.

## When not to use this

When the antagonist has no plausible hold over the player, a debt invented to supply one reads
as contrivance; a hostage, a contract or a reputation does the same work. In a story with one
act there is no room for a rewrite to be planted and paid off. And where the player can repay
at will with an easily farmed currency, the leash is cosmetic: it needs a cost the player
cannot simply grind away.
