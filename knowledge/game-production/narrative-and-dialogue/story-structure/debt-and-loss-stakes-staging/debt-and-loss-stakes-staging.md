# Debt and loss: staging stakes

A stake is something the player stands to lose, believes they stand to lose, and can see
being put at risk. All three halves are needed and each one fails on its own. A stake the
player cannot read is not a stake; it is a variable. A stake the player reads but never
sees threatened is decoration on a menu. And a stake that is taken without a reason the
fiction owns is not drama at all: the player files it as a complaint against the designer,
and from that moment the story is being played against rather than through.

This subject owns the craft of making stakes felt in a game with a progression the player
builds — a garage, a crew, a reputation, a home. It owns four instruments and the order in
which they are used: a short **ledger** the player reads after every event, a **debt** that
works as a leash because the creditor can rewrite its terms, a **scripted loss** of
something the player built, and an **ending** whose decisive act belongs to the player. The
instruments are narrative; the numbers they speak about are not, and the line between the
two is drawn below.

## Stakes live where the player looks every time

A story's stakes are usually written into the scenes: a threat delivered in a cutscene, a
reminder in a briefing. Scenes are skipped, and the ones that are watched are watched once.
The player's attention in a progression game is reliably in exactly one place — the screen
that tells them what just happened to their position. That screen is therefore where the
stakes have to live.

The ledger is that screen given a narrative voice: a handful of lines after every race,
fight, job or day, saying what changed, what is now owed, and what is at risk next. It is
short because it is read every time, and it is read every time because it is short. A
ledger is not a receipt. The receipt is the economy's conservation statement and it answers
"was I paid correctly"; the ledger answers "what does that payment mean to someone in this
world". The two may share a screen and they must never disagree, because the moment the
story's account of a debt and the economy's account of a balance differ, the player believes
the number and stops believing the story — which is why every figure the ledger shows is read
from the quantity the economy owns,
[one authority per quantity](../../../_laws.md#one-authority-per-quantity) applied to a
plot. The ledger must also move with the player's choices; an account the player cannot
affect is a weather report. See
[ledger-after-every-event](./techniques/ledger-after-every-event.md).

## A debt is a leash, not a loan

A loan is a goal: a number to pay down, after which the player is free. That is a fine
economic sink and a weak story, because the creditor is a progress bar. A leash is a
relationship of power. The creditor sends the player where the creditor wants them, takes a
share of what they win, and — the property that makes it a leash — can change the terms.

The creditor speaks only the language of obligation — owed, fair, agreed — because a
debt's peculiar power is to make the debtor feel the fault is theirs; a creditor who
threatens is an obstacle, and one who reminds you what you signed is a leash.

The rewrite is the instrument, and it is dangerous for a precise reason: an arbitrary
rewrite reads as the designer moving the goalposts, and the player's anger goes to the
wrong address. The craft is to give every rewrite a visible basis — a clause the player was
shown, or an open breach by a creditor the player has already caught cheating — a motive
the creditor owns, and an announcement on the ledger the moment it takes effect. Then the
anger lands on the creditor, which is where the story needed it. Escalation laid out as a
schedule when the deal is struck is not a rewrite; it is a term, read as a challenge, and the
fairness test binds only terms changed mid-contract. A leash also has a floor it
may never cross: it tightens on what the player is owed, sent to and allowed, never on the
economy's guarantee that the next event can be started. And a leash made of money goes slack
when the money is paid: a debt that can clear before the climax needs its payoff to be the
rewrite, or a term that outlives the money named on the ledger at that event, or the story has
no stake from the zero to the take. See
[the-leash-can-be-rewritten](./techniques/the-leash-can-be-rewritten.md).

## The loss that lands is the one taken after a win

The loss that makes a story is the removal of something the player built: the car they
tuned for ten events, the ally they recruited, the garage they furnished. Its weight comes
from two well-measured effects. People value what they own above what they could acquire,
and they value what they built themselves above what they were handed — provided they
finished building it. A completed, owned, self-built thing is the most valuable object a
game can create in a player's head, which is exactly why taking it works and exactly why it
must be taken with care.

Channel and timing are the whole technique. The take defeats the player's will, not their
skill: lost in a race the player could have won, the thing is blamed on the game; lost to a
contract the player could never have beaten, it is blamed on the holder. And taken after a
loss, the removal reads as the game piling on, the one attribution that poisons everything
after it. Taken immediately after a win, at peak ownership, by a named agent with a motive
the player has already been shown, the removal reads as theft, and theft has a thief. The win
has to be guaranteed by the event structure — a gate only a win clears — because a take placed
after an event any finish clears follows a win only as often as the field allows, and a balance
pass moves that share without anyone looking at the story.
Players who blame themselves for a failure rate a game higher than players who blame the
game; the scripted loss adds another address, the antagonist, and the craft is to make it
the only plausible one. See [take-it-after-a-win](./techniques/take-it-after-a-win.md).

## The loss was always on the page

A loss the player could not have seen coming is a twist; a loss they could have seen and
did not take seriously is a tragedy, and tragedy is the stronger register for a game because
the player recognises it rather than resenting it. The ledger is the planting ground: the
collateral clause in the debt's terms, visible from the first screen; the creditor's interest
in the car, noted when the car first wins. Around it, every appearance of the taker carries a
detail that reads as generosity now and as foreclosure afterwards, and somewhere earlier the
leash takes from a character the player knows, so that the player has seen it work. When the
take happens the player looks back and finds it there — surprising the first time, inevitable
afterwards. One trap deserves a name: a plant that implies a condition the player can meet
("fall behind and the car goes") promises a rule, and a take after the player met it reads as
cheating. See
[foreshadow-the-loss-on-the-ledger](./techniques/foreshadow-the-loss-on-the-ledger.md).

## After the loss, a stretch the player survives

The beat after a major loss is the most dangerous in the game, because it combines the
player's highest grievance with their lowest power. The naive designs fail in opposite
directions: restoring power at once makes the loss meaningless, and leaving the player
underpowered against a field tuned for the old power makes the loss a wall — and a player
quits at a wall at the moment they are angriest. A third failure is quieter: skipping the
stretch, so the player meets the reduced state for the first time in the final act. The
craft is a short, deliberately leaner stretch, tuned so that a player on the intended path
survives it, with story that advances on finishing rather than winning, a visible way back
and, ideally, something the reduced state teaches that the strong state never did. Surviving
it is what earns the final act. See [survive-the-lean-stretch](./techniques/survive-the-lean-stretch.md).

## The decisive act is the player's

Every instrument above transfers power away from the player, on purpose. The ending must
transfer it back, and it must do so through the player's own hands: the decisive act is
played, not watched; contested, not guaranteed; performed with the game's core verb, not a
new one; and not given to an ally, to luck, to a timer, or to the antagonist's own blunder.
Players notice a borrowed ending and call it stolen agency. The ledger's last entry closes
the debt line, in the same voice that opened it. A story that spent its
whole length making the player feel the leash and then lets a cutscene cut it has told the
player that the leash was never theirs to cut. See
[the-last-act-is-the-players](./techniques/the-last-act-is-the-players.md).

## The failure modes of the naive reading

The commonest failure is **stakes in prose only**: a debt mentioned in the opening scene and
never again, so the player discovers what it meant only when it is called. The second is
**the arbitrary rewrite**, where a creditor changes terms with no clause and no motive and the
player correctly concludes that nothing they do matters. The third is **loss as punishment**:
the scripted seizure placed after a defeat, or delivered through a mechanic the player
believes they could have prevented by playing better — a race they were allowed to win and
lost anyway. The fourth is **the endless trough**: a post-loss stretch with no visible way
up, tuned against the pre-loss field. The fifth is **the borrowed ending**: the climax
resolved by someone or something other than the player. Every one of them moves the blame
from the antagonist to the designer, which is the single transfer this subject exists to
prevent.

## Boundaries

**Against racing-career-economy.** That subject owns the rules of a career's money: the
insured share of the repair bill, the participation floor, the settlement ticket and its
receipt, the finite shop, the fixed rival schedule. This subject owns what the player is made
to feel about those numbers and the authored events that sit on top of them — the debt, its
terms, the seizure. The rule for picking: when the question is "can this result strand the
player or pay twice", it is the economy; when it is "who is the player paying, why, and what
will they take next", it is this subject. The economy's guarantees outrank the story: a
leash may tighten on the player's share and freedom, never through the floor, and a scripted
seizure is an authored event with a restoration path, not a sink.

**Against ending-first-narrative-structure.** That sibling owns writing the ending first and
building the arc backwards from it, including where plants and payoffs fall across the whole
story. This subject owns one thread inside that arc — the stake, its loss and its recovery —
and the specific instruments that carry it. The rule: if the question is "what is the ending
and what must be set up for it", it is the sibling; if it is "what does the player stand to
lose on the way and how is the loss staged", it is this subject. The two meet at the final
act, where the sibling decides what the ending is and this subject insists the player is the
one who performs it.

**Against the progression-curve work in game-economy-tuning.** That subject tests whether a
curve is the family it claims to be and treats a step at a piecewise join as a wall. A scripted
loss deliberately puts a step in the player's power curve; this subject owns why it is there
and how long the lean segment lasts, and the curve work owns proving the step is the size the
design states and that the segment after it is survivable on the reference path. A loss that
was never declared to the curve will be found by it as a defect — correctly.

## The path, in order

1. **Name the stake**: the one built thing whose loss the story turns on, chosen for how much
   the player will have made it their own by the time it is taken.
2. **Open the ledger on the first event** and write the debt, its terms and its collateral into
   it, sharing every number with the economy.
3. **Plant the loss on the ledger** at least an act before it happens, and escalate the plant
   once.
4. **Script the rewrite and the take** at the peak of a win that only a win could reach, by a
   named agent, under a clause the player was shown — and if the debt can clear before then,
   make the payoff the rewrite or carry the leash on a term that outlives it.
5. **Tune the lean stretch** as a short, survivable, declared dip with a visible way back.
6. **Hand the final act to the player** and close the debt on the ledger in the voice that
   opened it.
7. **Say what is untested.** Until people have played it, every claim about what a player will
   feel is a design intent, and it is reported as one.
