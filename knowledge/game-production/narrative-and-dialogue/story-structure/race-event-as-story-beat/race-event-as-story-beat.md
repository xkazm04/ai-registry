# Race event as story beat

A race-based campaign has a unit of play, and the unit is the event: a grid, a few laps or a
fixed distance, a finishing order, a purse. Players come for that unit, replay it, and learn
to skip everything around it. Story placed between events, as scenes the player watches,
competes with the thing the player came for and loses; somewhere around the third event the
skip input is pressed before the first line has landed, and from then on the campaign has a
story that exists only in its script. Story placed *inside* the event is felt, because it
changes what the player has to do in the next three minutes.

This subject owns that move: making the event itself the carrier of a story beat. Each event
carries one idea and then retires it. Each beat arrives in the same four parts, a card before
the grid, a rule variant during the race, short lines while driving and a persisted change
after the flag. The rule variant is the beat's content and the words are its label. Only a
few beats are pinned in place; the rest may be met in any order. And the words around an
event are held to a budget of about twenty seconds on each side, because every second past
that is spent against the player's patience rather than on the story.

## One event carries one idea, and then the idea is spent

An event's idea is a dramatic proposition the player can act on: *the rival will not let you
pass*, *you are carrying something you cannot afford to lose*, *the car is not yours yet*,
*someone on your own side wants you to lose*. One per event, for the same reason a teaching
schedule introduces one competence at a time: two ideas in one event are two experiments in
one trial, and neither the player nor the author can say afterwards which one the event was
about.

Inside the event the idea gets a small arc of its own. The discipline is the one platform
designers use for a single-mechanic level: introduce the idea where it costs nothing, develop
it, twist it so the player has to think about it in a way they did not expect, conclude it,
and put it away. In a race that maps onto the clock the player already reads: the grid and
the first lap introduce, the middle laps develop, the last lap twists, the flag concludes. An
event whose idea is fully stated on the card and then merely persists for three minutes has a
premise and no arc.

Then the idea retires. Once delivered, it does not come back as the same variant with a new
card; it may return only transformed, as a callback that reverses or escalates it, so the
rival who blocked you in the second event drafts you in the ninth. The one standing exception
is the finale, which may call back several retired ideas at once, because a finale that
examines what the campaign taught is the campaign concluding rather than repeating. The naive
reading treats a good idea as a good mode and reuses it, and repetition converts a beat into
inventory: by its third appearance the player reads the escort event as an event type, the
card as boilerplate, and the story as decoration on a playlist. A racing game's modes exist
precisely to be repeated; a story beat exists precisely not to be.

## A beat has four parts, and each part fails differently when it is missing

The **pre-event card** names who, what is at stake and the one rule that is different today,
in one image and a line or two. The **rule variant** is the mechanical expression of the
idea, live for the whole event and visible on the heads-up display. The **mid-event barks**
are short lines triggered by state and by race phase rather than by the clock, the rival
speaking when the block happens, the passenger when the cargo takes a hit, each carrying one
fact in a few words, and they confirm that the idea is in play rather than introducing
anything new. The **post-event state** is what the campaign remembers: money, carried damage,
a rival's standing, an ally gained or lost, the next card's opening line.

Each omission has a signature. A card with no variant is a trailer for a race that plays like
every other race. A variant with no card is a gimmick, a modifier with no reason. Barks that
explain are narration over driving, read by nobody because the player's eyes are on the road.
An outcome that is shown but not persisted is a beat that evaporates at the next menu; the
persisted state is the only proof the beat happened, and the next card reads it, as does a
later one, because a consequence echoed several events on in another character's mouth
proves the world kept a record in a way the result screen cannot. The outcome also branches
on how the event went, because a race can be won, lost or survived, and a loss that only
offers a retry has removed the story from exactly the players who most needed it. A retry's
card reads the previous attempt for the same reason: the third attempt that opens with the
same words as the first tells the player the attempts are outside the story.

## The story changes what the rules do, or it is not in the event

The test is deletion. Remove the card and the barks; if the event still plays exactly like an
unmodified race, the beat was a scene stapled to a race, and the player who skipped the card
experienced no story at all. A beat whose stakes are declared in text and read by no rule is
an input nobody consumes
([declaring-an-input-is-not-consuming-it](../../../_laws.md#declaring-an-input-is-not-consuming-it)):
the card says the debt is crushing, and nothing in the event costs more because of it.

So the variant is the literal enactment of the idea. Debt becomes repairs the player cannot
afford, so damage carries into the next event. Betrayal becomes an ally's car that rams
instead of blocks. A wounded driver becomes steering that pulls. The words then do the cheap
part of the job, naming what the hands are already feeling.

Two constraints keep a variant fair. It changes a rule or what an input achieves, never which
input does what, because remapping controls punishes muscle memory and teaches nothing about
the story. And every effect the story has on the rules is disclosed, a handicap and a help
alike, announced on the card and shown on the display for the whole event: a handicap the
player did not see coming reads as a defect rather than a plot, and an ally's help the player
cannot see is an invisible boost that quietly devalues every result it touches.

The converse holds as well. A beat that cannot be expressed as a rule does not belong on the
track; it belongs in the hub between events, where a scene does not compete with a race. A
card bolted to an unmodified race is the worst of both placements.

## A few beats are pinned, the rest are free

A campaign has a spine of a handful of fixed beats, typically the opening, the turn, the low
point and the finale, that every player meets in order whatever their results. Everything else
is flexible: playable in any order inside its tier, failable without blocking progress,
replayable, and written so the story still holds whichever subset the player has met.

The reason is that racing players fail, retry and wander. A strictly linear story pins
progress behind whichever event is hardest and makes every replay a rerun of the same lines; a
campaign with no fixed beats has no arc, only a list of modifiers. Flexible beats therefore
read campaign state and never each other's order, and their cards are tagged by condition and
picked by the most specific match, with a broad default always present, rather than placed by
sequence. Fixed beats gate the tiers and carry the arc, and they are never left to that
selector, because a most-specific-match pick lands worse than an authored placement exactly
at the moments that matter most. They are allowed what flexible beats are not: more time,
more words and a guaranteed audience. And the story advances on *finishing* a beat rather
than on winning it; winning gates money and rank, because a story behind a difficulty wall is
a story most players never see.

## Twenty seconds before, twenty after

The budget per event is about twenty seconds of story before the grid and twenty after the
flag, with the barks inside the race counted as separate short lines rather than charged to
either side. Against an event of two to four minutes, plus the menus and the shop around it,
that holds story to somewhere between a tenth and a fifth of the session, a ratio chosen so the
player is still there for the race rather than one anyone has measured as the reading
threshold. It is a budget that shapes the writing, not only a cap on it
([a-budget-shapes-the-output](../../../_laws.md#a-budget-shapes-the-output)): an author handed
two minutes writes two minutes, and the event becomes an interruption in its own campaign.

The figure carries its basis or it is not a budget
([a-number-carries-its-unit-and-basis](../../../_laws.md#a-number-carries-its-unit-and-basis)):
seconds at a stated reading or speaking rate, counted from the card's appearance to the grid's
first input, on the display the game ships on. Fixed beats may overrun it, which is part of
what being fixed buys and part of why there are few of them. And every story surface is
skippable, but a skip skips only words: the rule variant still applies and is still shown, and
the post-event state is still written.

## The starter set of rule variants

A campaign needs an authoring vocabulary before it needs a script: a small library of rule
variants, each a mechanical change with the story meanings it can carry. A workable starter
covers pursuit of one named target, being hunted by a force that targets only the player, a
stake that drains visibly during the race, escort of something fragile, damage carried
between events, a handicapped start, a restricted arsenal, time or fuel pressure,
elimination, a system that fails mid-race, a shared fate with an ally who must also finish, a
reversed objective in which winning is the wrong result, and a trial that cannot be lost,
which gives a character room to talk and teaches a verb before the story needs it in anger.
Each variant carries a given idea once. Composing two variants is permitted only when both
express the same idea; two variants with two ideas is the two-experiment event again.

## What the player hits decides what the story says

A combat racer's central verb is violence against other drivers, and the beat decides how
that violence reads. The rule variant chooses what is hit, a hunting enforcer, a rival with a
grudge, a convoy, the league's own machines, and what is hit decides the act's meaning more
than any line about it: the same ram is self-defence against a collector, cruelty against a
stranger, and satire when the target is the spectacle itself. So the framing is a decision
made once for the campaign, who the violence's weight or joke lands on, and every variant
keeps it there. The naive failure is a campaign whose cards plead for sympathy while its rules
reward wrecking whoever is nearest; the hands win that argument, and the story is the one that
looks dishonest. Humour obeys the same discipline: it lives in a character's way of coping
under real stakes, never in the world winking that the stakes are not real.

## Failure modes of the naive reading

**"The story lives in the cutscenes."** Then it lives where the skip input is. The event is
the only surface every player is guaranteed to attend to.

**"A good event type should come back."** A mode comes back; a beat retires. Reusing a variant
for its idea a second time converts the story into a playlist.

**"The card explains the stakes, so the stakes exist."** Stakes that no rule reads are prose.
Delete the card and play the event; what remains is the beat.

**"Losing means retry."** For a flexible beat, losing is an outcome with its own state and its
own line. A campaign whose story continues only for winners tells its story to the players
who needed it least.

**"More story is more investment."** Past the budget, every added second is spent teaching the
player where the skip input is.

## Where this subject stops

**Against combat-pacing-and-dramatic-arc.** That subject owns the shape of tension inside one
encounter over time: the curve, its named beats, the duration envelope and the readout. This
subject owns what the event *means* and what changes because it happened. The picker: if the
question is whether the three minutes rise, peak and resolve, it is pacing; if it is what the
event says and what the campaign remembers afterwards, it is here. The two touch at the barks,
which pacing can trigger (a comeback detected is a moment worth a line), and this subject owns
what the line says.

**Against learning-curve-and-teaching-design.** Both subjects put one idea in one unit, and
they then do opposite things with it. A skill atom is introduced, practised and tested again,
because a competence the game never asks for again is one the player correctly treats as
optional. A story idea is delivered and retired, because a dramatic proposition repeated
becomes a mode. The picker: if the player must still be able to do the thing three events
later, it is a teaching atom and the schedule owns its recurrence; if the player must
remember what happened, it is a beat and it must not come back unchanged. An event may host a
teaching introduction and a story beat at once, but the beat must not be the only site where
the atom is ever practised.

**Against racing-track-authoring-and-lint.** That subject owns the circuit as geometry and the
checks that make it raceable. A rule variant may want a track property, a night run or a
flooded section, and the track still answers to its own lint; this subject chooses which track
hosts a beat and never bends geometry to tell a story.

**Against ending-first-narrative-structure.** The sibling owns the campaign's arc, decided from
its ending backwards: what the story must have set up by which point, and therefore which
beats have to be pinned. This subject owns how any beat, pinned or free, is carried inside one
event. The picker: "what must the campaign have said by tier three" is the sibling's; "how
does this event say it in twenty seconds and a rule" is this one's.
