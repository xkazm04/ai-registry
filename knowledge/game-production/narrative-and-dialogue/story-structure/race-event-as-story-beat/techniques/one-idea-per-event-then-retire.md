# One idea per event, then retire it

The named concern: every event in a race campaign carries exactly one dramatic idea, the idea
is stated once as a proposition the player can act on, and after the event it is spent. It may
return only transformed. This is the rule that keeps a campaign of thirty near-identical
three-minute races reading as a story rather than as a playlist.

## What counts as an idea

An idea is a proposition about the player's situation that the event makes true for its
duration: *this rival will not let you pass*, *the thing in your boot must arrive intact*, *the
car is borrowed and every dent is owed*, *your team-mate has been paid to lose you the race*.
It is phrased from the player's side and it implies a demand. A theme is not an idea ("trust",
"greed"), because a theme cannot be driven; a setting is not an idea ("the desert stage"),
because a setting changes the scenery and not the situation. When a proposed idea cannot be
finished with "so in this race you have to...", it is not yet an idea.

## Why one, and not two

Two ideas in one event are two experiments in one trial. The player finishes the race unable
to say whether it was the race where the rival blocked or the race where the cargo mattered,
and the author cannot tell from telemetry or from interviews which one landed. The second idea
also halves the card, the barks and the variant: two propositions in twenty seconds of text is
two half-read propositions. The cost of the rule is that a campaign needs more events than a
writer's outline has scenes, which is the right pressure: it forces the outline to be broken
into drivable pieces instead of compressing several scenes into one race.

## The idea's arc inside the event

One idea is not one moment. Inside the event the idea is introduced, developed, twisted and
concluded, the four-step shape a single-mechanic level in platform design gives its one
gimmick, and a race already has the clock to hang it on. The grid and the first lap introduce
the proposition where it costs little: the rival is visible, the cargo meter is full. The
middle laps develop it: the rival blocks, the meter falls. The last lap twists it, in a way
the player did not expect but the idea already contained: the rival's team-mate joins the
block, or the cargo starts to matter more than the position. The flag concludes it, and the
outcome records which way it went. An event whose proposition is fully stated on the card and
then merely persists for three minutes has a premise and no arc, and it plays as a modifier.
The twist is the step most often missing, and its absence is visible on a design sheet: if
nothing about the idea changes between the first lap and the last, write the twist or accept
that the event is a modifier.

## Why retire

The idea is retired because repetition changes its category. Met once, an escort race is a
story about protecting someone. Met three times with three cards, it is an event type, and the
player starts reading the card as the label on a mode. A retired idea may come back as a
**callback**, and a callback is legitimate only when it changes the proposition: the rival who
blocked now drafts the player; the cargo that had to arrive intact now has to be lost on
purpose; the borrowed car is now owned and the debt has moved to someone else. The test for a
callback is that its card could not be written without the earlier event having happened.

The finale is the one place several retired ideas may return together. A last event that asks
the player to use what the campaign made them do, the rival's block, the cargo discipline, the
cheap car, is the campaign concluding its own arc, and it reads as an examination rather than
a repeat precisely because each idea was retired before it. An idea that was never retired
cannot be called back; it was simply still running.

Retirement is tracked, not remembered. The campaign keeps one register of ideas with the event
that delivered each and the callbacks that transformed it, and the authoring of a new event
reads that register before it proposes an idea
([one-authority-per-quantity](../../../../_laws.md#one-authority-per-quantity)). Without it, a
campaign written by several hands repeats its best idea in three tiers, each author believing
it was theirs.

## Procedure

1. Break the outline into propositions, one per event, each finishable with "so in this race
   you have to...".
2. Enter each in the idea register with its event, its rule variant, and its status: live,
   retired, or transformed by a named later event.
3. For every proposed new event, check the register: a match on a retired idea is either a
   callback with a changed proposition or a rejection.
4. Review the campaign as a list of propositions, not of tracks or modes; two adjacent events
   whose propositions are paraphrases of each other are one idea delivered twice.

## Decision rules

- **When an event's card needs "and" to state its stakes, split the event or cut one idea**,
  because the second idea will be the one nobody remembers and it costs half the first one's
  budget.
- **When a popular event type is requested again, reuse the variant with a new idea, not the
  idea with a new card.** The variant is vocabulary and may recur; the proposition may not.
- **When a callback's card could be written without the original event, it is a repeat**, and
  it is rejected as one.
- **When a player can meet events in any order, a callback must name its prerequisite in
  campaign state**, and it is only offered once the original has been delivered.
- **When an outline applies introduce-develop-twist-conclude to one variant across several
  events, read each later appearance as a callback** and hold it to the
  callback test; the four steps belong inside one event, and across events only a changed
  proposition earns a return.

## When not to use this

Free-play and repeatable events outside the campaign are modes and should repeat; the rule
governs only events that carry story. A deliberately recurring motif, such as a rival who
appears in every tier, is a character rather than an idea, and each appearance still carries a
different proposition. And an idea that is really a competence the player must keep is not a
story idea at all: it belongs to the teaching schedule, which requires the opposite treatment.

## Evidence status

The four-step shape of one idea inside one level, and the retiring of a level's gimmick
afterwards, rest on a primary interview with a platform game's director, rated high
confidence by the research this subject distils; the retirement half is the research's
paraphrase of that interview rather than a quotation. That a racing game has carried story
through chains of rule-variant events with voice-over around them comes from a search summary
of a page that did not load, so it is medium confidence and illustrative only. Carrying the
level-scale rule over to race events, the lap-by-lap mapping, the callback test and the finale
exception are design inference. None of it has been tested in a played race campaign.
