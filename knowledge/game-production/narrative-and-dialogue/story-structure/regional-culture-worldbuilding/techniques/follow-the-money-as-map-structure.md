# Follow the money as map structure

The named concern: order the campaign's regions along the movement of value, so that the
player travels the supply chain from where material is taken to where the surplus is
consumed. The map stops being a menu of places and becomes an argument about who feeds whom.

## Building the chain

Start at the end of the chain: the centre that produces nothing and consumes everything — the
capital, the league's home track, the arena, the tower. Then ask what it consumes and where
that comes from, one link at a time: the market that sells it, the convoy region that moves it,
the works that refine it, the edge where it is dug, fished, salvaged or grown. Each region's
export is the next region's import, and each link takes a cut. Five or six links is a
campaign; more than that and the chain becomes a lecture. Keep the goods few as well: two or
three things everyone needs, each held by a known party, explain a world's politics,
geography and motives at once, where a dozen commodities explain nothing.

Stage the joins. Between any two links there is a crossing — a gate, a bridge, a weigh-in, a
pass — and who is allowed across it, at what toll and in which direction, is the class
structure made into geography. Crossings are where confrontations belong, because they are
where the chain is enforced.

The player usually travels it from the edge inward, because the edge is where the
powerless start and the centre is where the antagonist sits. Travelling inward, the player
watches each link take its cut, and arrives at the centre already knowing what it is built on.
The difficulty ramp and the ramp of proximity to power then coincide: the regions get harder
because they are closer to the people who profit, which gives the difficulty a meaning the
player feels without a line of explanation. Where the terrain can be made to follow — a climb
into thin air before the descent into the basin where the centre sits — the physical arc and
the economic one coincide, and the route has a crescendo of its own.

Once the order carries the economy, the difficulty and the terrain together, it is a decision
to defend. A late request to swap two regions — to reach a reveal sooner, to vary the biomes —
usually breaks the chain and the terrain arc to gain one beat; the default answer is not to
reorder, and to move the beat instead.

## What the structure does for free

Each region's economy is defined relative to its neighbours, so the cultures cannot drift into
interchangeability — the convoy region's code is about delivery and the refinery region's is
about yield because that is the work their place in the chain gives them. The route carries
the theme: the player's path is the argument, so no character has to state it. And a reversal
is available that no menu map has: when the player's coalition cuts a link, the centre starves
in a way the player understands mechanically, because they travelled the chain.

## The economy must agree with the story

The chain is a claim the story makes, and the player tests it against the economy they
handle: what things cost in each region, what the purse pays, what repairs cost, what is scarce
and what is plentiful. When the edge is described as starving and the player's purse there is
generous, the player believes the purse. The chain and the tuned economy are two statements
of one quantity, so the tuned economy owns the numbers and the story reads them —
[one authority per quantity](../../../../_laws.md#one-authority-per-quantity) — and a
contradiction between them is a defect to resolve before any scene is written.

## Decision rules

- **When regions are ordered by biome variety, check whether that order is also a supply
  chain**; if it is not, reorder or give the order another reason the player can feel.
- **When a region's import and export are unconnected to its neighbours, connect them** or
  explain the break as a fact of the world (a blockade, a cut road).
- **When the centre produces something, ask whether it is really the centre**; the centre of a
  follow-the-money map consumes.
- **When the chain needs more than six links, merge**; the player must hold the route in mind.
- **When the story says a region is poor, make it cost more and pay less there**, and check
  the numbers against the economy's own tables.
- **When the campaign cuts a link late, make the consequence mechanical** — a price rises, a
  part vanishes, a purse shrinks — at the centre, so the player feels the chain work.

## When not to use this

A campaign whose structure is a journey with no economic logic — an escape, a pilgrimage, a
search — should not force a supply chain onto the route; another ordering principle (distance,
memory, seasons) can carry the argument. And where region order is decided by the ending-first
plan — a unit that must come before another to plant a reveal — the plan wins and the chain
bends around it, with the bend explained in the world. And where the player choosing the order
of regions is itself a pillar — a level-select structure, an open map — a fixed route costs
that pillar. Keep the chain as the world's logic instead: each region's import names the
region it comes from, so the chain reads from wherever the player starts, and the economy
the player handles still has to agree with it.

## Evidence status

Defining a world by two or three goods everyone needs and who holds each rests on a film's
on-screen world (high). Class shown as geography and as what flows between spaces rests on a
critic's essay and the researcher's own readings, checkable on screen (medium). Ordering a
campaign's regions as a supply chain, the coinciding terrain arc and the advice not to reorder
are the research's own design inference (low to medium), as is the rule that the tuned economy
must agree with the chain. That player-chosen region order can be a design's strength rests
on public facts about a long-running level-select series and a director's statement about an
open world (high on the facts); it bounds the defend-the-order rule to fixed routes. None of
it has been tested in a played game.
