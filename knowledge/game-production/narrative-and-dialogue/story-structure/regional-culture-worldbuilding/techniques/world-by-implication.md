# World by implication

The named concern: convey a world's history, economy and politics through what it left
behind, with very little said outright. The history is written in full and kept in the bible;
what reaches the player is its residue. A world the player infers is believed; a world the
player is told is skipped.

## Residue, not account

Every history leaves marks that the people living in it no longer notice: the price on a
sign, a rule painted on a wall, a toll everyone pays without comment, a ruin nobody mentions, a
word everyone uses and nobody defines, a place name that is a compressed event, a gesture
people make passing a certain spot, a thing nobody will sell. The craft is to choose residues,
not to write accounts. For each fact the player should come to know, pick two or three
residues of it that sit in different channels — an object, a line, a price, a rule — and let
the player assemble the fact.

The strongest residue is an object whose state changes. A piece of gear set aside, a tag
hanging unclaimed, a door painted to match a missing arm: the object reports a history now,
and when its state changes later — picked up, claimed, painted over — it reports an arc without
a line. Prefer residue that is **repaired, not only ruined**: a patch, a retrofit, a part made to
fit what it was never meant for implies people who stayed and kept things running, while
uniform wreckage implies only a past and makes every region look the same.

Characters inside a world do not explain it to each other. The test for a line of dialogue is
whether two locals would say it to each other; if it only makes sense addressed to the
audience, it is exposition in costume. A newcomer character who asks is an old device for
smuggling explanation in; it works once, for the player's own entry, and its cost rises every
time it is used again.

## Touchpoints guard against opacity

The opposite failure is the riddle world: everything implied, nothing anchored, the player
unable to tell which details matter. The guard is the **touchpoint**: every implied fact the
campaign depends on must touch something the player uses — a price they pay, a rule that
changes what they may do, a word that appears on a screen they must read, a person whose
behaviour follows from it. A fact the player uses is learned without being told, because use
forces attention. A fact with no touchpoint is lore: it costs writing and art, and the player
cannot tell it from noise. That is the narrative form of
[declaring an input is not consuming it](../../../../_laws.md#declaring-an-input-is-not-consuming-it),
and the cure is the same — find the reader or cut the fact.

## The exposition budget

Give each region a small, explicit budget of lines that state a fact outright — a handful —
and spend them only on facts the player needs in order to act: what a resource is for, what a
rule forbids, what the stakes of the next event are. Everything else is carried by residue.
Track the budget in the bible; when a writer needs a line beyond it, the question is which
residue would carry the fact instead.

Spend the budget on how people live now, never on how the world ended. Survivors care about
consequences; a catastrophe left unnamed costs nothing and frees every line for the rules of
living. And keep the system behind a person: when the subject is structural — debt, class, a
league that owns people — the protagonist's personal errand stays in front and the system
passes behind every scene, in its prices, its cages, its posters. A system looked at directly
becomes a lecture; glimpsed behind someone's want, it becomes a world.

## Decision rules

- **When a fact is explained in dialogue, ask whether two locals would say it to each other**;
  if not, move it to an object, a price or a rule.
- **When a fact matters to play, give it a touchpoint** the player handles; when it matters
  to nothing the player handles, cut it or keep it in the bible only.
- **When a fact is conveyed by a single residue, add a second in a different channel**;
  one residue is a detail, two consistent ones are a history.
- **When the plot depends on a fact, do not leave it to inference alone**: give it residues
  in several channels and one plain statement at the moment the player needs it. Players
  read the same scene differently, and residue reliably carries only the history the player
  may take or leave.
- **When a line of the economy is read but not acted on — a deduction on a receipt — show
  the number plainly and let the label speak in the world's voice** until the story's reveal
  renames it. A label that names the theft from the first receipt spends the reveal, and it
  contradicts any card that plants the euphemism beside it.
- **When a proper noun is invented, make it carry an event or a function** — a place named for
  what happened there, a title named for what its holder does.
- **When the opening needs context, start in the middle of the world's routine** — a queue, a
  weigh-in, a toll — and let the routine explain itself.
- **When the region's exposition budget is spent, convert, don't add.**
- **When a line explains how the world ended, cut it** unless the player needs the fact to act.
- **When a scene stares at the system, put a person's errand in front of it.**
- **When a deep backstory exists, keep it**: write the private history of every character,
  vehicle and place in full, because consistent residue needs a source to be consistent with —
  and show none of it directly.

## When not to use this

Systems the player must operate — the rules of a mode, the meaning of a gauge, the cost of a
repair — are taught plainly, not implied: implication is for the world, not for the controls,
and a player confused about the controls blames the game, not the fiction. And a game played
in short sessions on a shared screen gets fewer chances to assemble residue, so it should
anchor its few essential facts more plainly and imply everything else.

## Evidence status

Writing deep private backstories and showing only their marks rests on a film director's own
interviews (high). Leaving the catastrophe unexplained rests on a novel read directly (high).
Keeping the system in the background rests on a philosopher's recorded commentary on a film
(high). A used world shown as repaired rests on a film director's and a game director's own
statements (high). Explaining through objects rests on on-screen readings partly the
researcher's own (medium). The touchpoint rule and the exposition budget are practitioner
judgement (low to medium). That players bring their own frame to an environmental scene and
reach different conclusions, and that a message which affects play should be unambiguous,
rest on two level designers' published talk notes (high, primary); they bound inference to
optional history. The split receipt line comes from one shipped campaign read on 2026-10-10
(one case). No developer-sourced figure on how many players read optional lore was found.
None of it has been tested in a played game.
