# Political economy of spectacle

The named concern: when a campaign is built around staged violence — a blood sport, a racing
league, an arena, a televised hunt — treat the spectacle as an institution with a balance
sheet. It exists because somebody profits, it is paid for by somebody else, and it is kept
alive by an audience that wants something from it.

## The spectacle is the means

The naive reading takes the spectacle as the product: the league exists to hold races, the
arena to hold fights. Historically and dramatically it is the other way round. Funders spend on
games to buy something else — popularity that pays off in votes or loyalty, legitimacy, fear,
labour discipline, a channel for anger that would otherwise go somewhere worse — and powers
that understand this end up monopolising the games, because the games are power. Decide what
the spectacle buys before deciding what it looks like.

The spectacle may also do real work. A league that is the only thing keeping fuel, parts and
medicine moving is partly right to exist, and that partial rightness is what stops the world
from being a cartoon of exploitation — and what obliges the ending to say what moves the goods
once the league falls.

## Three questions

**Who profits.** The owners and promoters; the bookmakers; the sponsors whose names are on the
cars; the local powers who take a cut of contracts and keep their licence to keep their people
working; the central power that uses the spectacle — to recruit fighters, to give the poor a
lottery instead of a revolt, to settle disputes it could not settle openly, to display its
strength. Name each beneficiary and what exactly they take: gate money, betting margin, a cut
of the purse, political quiet. Name what each one pays as well: even the owner pays, in the
need to keep the show fresh and the stars dependent.

**Who pays.** The competitors, first, with their bodies; their families and crews; the regions
whose material and young people feed the league; the excluded, who get nothing from it but
whose salvage and labour it turns into equipment; the spectators themselves, in money and in
habit; and the dead, who should be counted, because a spectacle that hides its dead is
telling the player what it wants them to believe.

**What the audience wants.** The answer is never only blood. It is hope — the story of the
nobody from the edge who made it; loyalty — a regional champion to bet their pride on;
justice — the hated favourite brought down; and ritual — the week's one shared event. An
audience with wants is a faction, and in the end it is the system's real constituency. Its
appetite shifts as the campaign goes on, it can be won and lost, and its demands push the
owners to change the rules, which gives the world a mechanism of change that does not need a
villain's decision. An audience that is draining away — because the regions that fill the
stands are going broke — is itself a plot: it pushes the owners toward a louder example, and
it gives the ending an image to earn in who fills the stands at the last event.

## The protagonist is the product

The most productive fact in this territory is uncomfortable: the protagonist's success is what
the league sells. Every win from the edge proves the lottery can be won and keeps the poor
paying in. So the player's rise strengthens the system they mean to bring down, and a
campaign that ignores this has a protagonist who is unknowingly an advertisement. A campaign
that faces it has its best late-game question: what does the player do with a popularity that
belongs to the owners — use it, refuse it, or turn the crowd?

Stage the bind concretely. The player's face on a poster they did not consent to; their name
in the bookmaker's odds; a purse that grows as their story sells; an owner who tells them,
courteously, that the crowd loves them and that the crowd is his.

The bind has a flip side, and it is where the finale lives. A winner popular enough stops being
product and becomes a threat: the crowd's loyalty now has somewhere to go that the owners do not
control. The system's answer is to change the rules until the contest is meant to remove that
winner, and to do it in public, because a public removal is the only kind that restores the
lesson the spectacle exists to teach. That makes the last event matter beyond the two
competitors in it.

## Who the violence is aimed at

Staged violence reads as satire, as tragedy, as cruelty or as nothing, depending on whom its
joke is on: the audience, the promoter, the rules, the competitors. Decide the target and keep
it there. Violence with no target reads as gore; violence whose framing — the broadcast, the
commentary, the odds — keeps the promoter in the frame stays an argument even when it is
loud. What the competitors may hit, and what happens to it on screen, is part of that
decision.

## Making the balance sheet visible

The balance sheet is shown, not stated: who sits in the stands and who sits in the box; who
cleans the track after the race; how much of the purse reaches the driver and where the rest
goes, on the results screen itself; what the audience chants in each region, and how it
changes. A results screen that splits the purse visibly is the cheapest piece of political
economy a racing game can show.

## Decision rules

- **When the crowd only cheers, give it a want that can change** and let the change alter a
  rule, a purse or a matchup.
- **When the league has no beneficiary besides the antagonist, add one** — a bookmaker, a
  sponsor, an owner — so the spectacle is a system, not one man's toy.
- **When the dead are not counted anywhere the player looks, count them** — a wall of names, a
  retired number, a widow at the gate.
- **When the player's popularity has no owner, give it one**, and give the player a moment to
  see who it is.
- **When the spectacle and the central power are the same thing, still separate the audience
  from both**; the crowd is the one party whose loyalty can move.
- **When the political economy is explained in a speech, cut the speech and put the split on
  the results screen.**
- **When the finale is a private duel, ask whether the system needs it public**; a champion
  who threatens the league is removed in front of the crowd or not at all.
- **When the violence's target is unclear, name it** — audience, promoter, rules — and frame
  every broadcast, card and commentary line toward it.

## When not to use this

A sport or contest that is genuinely just play — friendly racing, a festival — does not need a
political economy, and inventing one turns a light game grim for no return. And the
antagonist's personal control of a necessity is owned by the antagonist's design; this
technique owns the spectacle as a system of which he may be only the largest beneficiary.

## Evidence status

Spectacle as a means of buying power rests on historical scholarship on ancient games and an
author's interview on building a fictional televised contest from them (high for the fiction,
contested for the history). One school of historians reads the ancient sponsors' giving as
neither redistribution nor depoliticisation but display of grandeur, an end in itself; the
purchase reading is a choice a fiction may make, and standing for its own sake belongs on the
list of what funders get. The popular
champion as a threat the system removes in public rests on a film read directly (high). The
target of satirical violence rests on public facts about several works (high on the facts); the
generalisation is the research's own (medium). The table of who profits and who pays, the
draining audience as plot and the results-screen split are the research's own design inference
(low to medium). None of it has been tested in a played game.
