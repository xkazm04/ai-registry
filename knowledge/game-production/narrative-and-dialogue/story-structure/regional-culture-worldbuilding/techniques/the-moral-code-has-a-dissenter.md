# The moral code has a dissenter

The named concern: every regional code is disputed, on screen, by a named person who lives
under it. A code everyone in the region holds is wallpaper — a sentence on the bible page that
no scene ever tests. A code someone argues with is politics, and a region with politics is a
place the player's arrival changes.

## What the dissenter does

The dissenter proves the code is real, because nobody argues with a rule that does not bind
them; the argument is the evidence that the code has teeth. The dissenter gives the region an
inside — the player hears the code from two people who both live under it and disagree, so the
culture has depth rather than a single face. And the dissenter hands the player a side to take
at almost no production cost: the choice is between two people the player has already met,
and its consequence can be as small as who speaks to the player in the next region.

## Writing the dispute

The dissenter's case must be **partly right**. A dissenter who is merely wrong — a thief
complaining about the rule against theft — is a lesson the region teaches, not a dispute it
contains. The productive dispute is between two goods the economy has set against each other:
the salvage rule that keeps the divers alive and also keeps the guild rich; the blessing that
comforts the convoys and also taxes them; the honour of racing clean that also gets the clean
racer killed. Each side should be able to state the other's case fairly; if the defender of the
code cannot say what the dissenter gets right, the dispute is a straw man.

The sharpest dissent in a world with a central power accuses the code of serving that power:
the hut that shelters everyone is why the road stays the controller's, the rule that debts are
everyone's is why the young stay owing the old, the log that owns the past is owned by whoever
licenses the loggers. A dissent of that shape ties the region's private moral to the campaign's
public politics in one line, and it makes the player's arrival a question the region was
already asking.

The dissenter should **pay something for dissent**, visibly, because a dissent that costs
nothing is an opinion. They are refused work, watched by the institution, mocked by their own
people, or simply poorer. The cost is the region's code in action and it is often the cheapest
way to show the code at all.

The dissenter is **a named character in play** — a mechanic, a rival, a crew member, a voice on
a radio — not a rumour or a codex entry. Place them where the player will meet them at least
twice: once to hear the dissent and once to see what it cost or what it won.

## Decision rules

- **When a region's code has no one who disputes it, give it a dissenter before writing more
  lore**; one disputing voice adds more culture than a page of history.
- **When the dissenter is simply wrong, find what the code costs and let them be right about
  that.** The dispute is between two goods or it is not a dispute.
- **When the dissenter's argument costs them nothing, give it a price** in the region's own
  currency — work, standing, safety, family.
- **When the player's choice between code and dissent has no consequence, give it a small one
  that appears later** — a line, a price, a face at the finale — rather than none; a choice
  with no echo trains the player to stop choosing.
- **When the dissenter and the antagonist agree, keep it**: the uncomfortable alignment is
  often the region's most interesting fact, and it forbids the player an easy side.
- **When the campaign has many regions, vary who dissents** — the young, the old, an outsider,
  a former enforcer — so dissent is not one archetype repeated; a bible in which every region's
  dissenters are "the young" has written one dissent several times.
- **When the dissent is attributed to a group ("some say", "the young hands"), name one member
  and give them a scene**; a group dissent is a rumour until a person carries it.

## When not to use this

A region designed as a breather — a safe harbour, a place of rest — may hold its code
unanimously on purpose, as a contrast that makes the disputed regions read as disputed. Use it
once, and know it is a choice. And a code that is the antagonist's own law, enforced on
everyone, is not this technique's object: dissent from the central power belongs to the
region's politics and to the antagonist's design; this technique is about the region's own
moral and the person inside it who refuses it.

## Evidence status

That a faction reads as real when its own members disagree and challenge each other rests on a
game director's interview about his factions (high, primary). That several voices and an
institution make a town rests on a showrunner's account of his town (high). That the dissent
must be named, partly right and priced, and the shape of dissent that accuses the code of
serving the central power, are practitioner judgement and the research's own inference from
those sources (low to medium). None of it has been tested in a played game.
