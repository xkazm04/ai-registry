# Regional culture worldbuilding

A campaign region designed by the art team alone is a palette: a ground colour, a sky, a
hazard, a music cue and a name. Palettes are necessary and they are not a world. The test is
simple and most campaigns fail it — take away the backdrop and listen to the people. If a
mechanic from the first region and a boss from the fourth could swap lines without anyone
noticing, the campaign has one culture painted six colours, and the player feels it as
sameness long before they can name it.

This subject owns the other thing: giving each region of a campaign a culture, meaning a
way of surviving and an account of who owes what to whom. It owns the questions every region
answers — what it makes and what it lacks, the one institution that organises life there,
the words its people coined, the code they hold and the person who disputes it, the local
power and what that power wants, the grip the central power has on it and what siding with
the player will cost it. It owns how that world is conveyed — by implication, with almost no
exposition — and two structures that sit over the whole map: the supply chain that orders
the regions, and the political economy of the spectacle, when the campaign is built around a
blood sport, a league or an arena that somebody profits from.

## A culture is derived, not decorated

The naive order of work is to start from mood and add details: a desert region, so dusty
slang, a gruff boss, a sandstorm. Every item is plausible and none of them is connected, so
none of them generates anything. A writer asked what a character from that region would do in
a situation the bible did not foresee has nothing to reason from.

The working order runs the other way, and it starts below the economy, at the material
conditions the art team already chose: the climate, the ground, what is lying around. Those
conditions decide the **economy** — what the region extracts, makes, moves or sells, and what
it cannot produce and must import — and the economy is the one fact the rest can be derived
from. The **institution** is how that economy is enforced and who keeps its books: the guild
that licenses the divers, the chapel that blesses the convoys, the weigh-house where the
salvage is priced. It owns a place, a face and one recurring object — a board, a log, a wall
of names — that the player sees often enough to read its state. The **slang** grows out of
the work and the institution, because people name what they handle every day and what they
fear. The **code** is what the economy punishes and rewards turned into a moral: where water
is scarce, wasting it is the worst sin; where everyone works on credit, a broken promise is.
Derived in that order, every field explains the others, and a writer meeting an unforeseen
situation can ask what the economy would make these people do. See
[economy-institution-slang-code-per-region](./techniques/economy-institution-slang-code-per-region.md).

Derivation is a consistency engine, not the only door in. Worlds begun from a handful of
image words, from a map, or from an invented language whose speakers came later read as
inevitable too, because something else carried their consistency. The order of invention is
free; the order of checking is not. However a region was conceived, run the chain over it
before the bible is locked: if its slang and its code cannot be traced to what the region
makes and lacks, they are costume whatever came first. And the economy is the root, not the
whole — belief, history and the lie of the land have to show through as well, or every region
reads as a function of its trade.

The same order is the cheapest check that the regions differ. Two regions with different
economies cannot share a code without the bible saying why; two regions with the same economy
should be merged, or one of them is a palette. The order also predicts how the language
changes across the map: at the working edge, slang is blunt and names the thing; at the
centre, where power lives, it turns to euphemism — a seizure called a retirement, people called
equity — because the people who profit need words that hide what they do.

## A code nobody disputes is wallpaper

A region whose people all hold the same moral code is a backdrop with a motto. The code
becomes culture at the point someone in the region argues with it — the young driver who
thinks the old salvage rule is a racket, the widow who refuses the chapel's blessing, the
mechanic who repairs for whoever pays. The dissenter does three jobs at once. They prove the
code is real, because people only argue with rules that bind them. They give the region
politics, which is what makes the player's arrival matter. And they hand the player a side
to take, which is the cheapest choice a region can offer. The dissent must be voiced by a
named character in play, not reported as a rumour, and it should be partly right: a dissenter
who is simply wrong is a lesson, not a dispute. See
[the-moral-code-has-a-dissenter](./techniques/the-moral-code-has-a-dissenter.md).

## Every region has a power, and winning it has a price

Each region has a **local power** — a boss, a council, a family, a foreman — with a want of
their own that is not the protagonist's want and not the central power's. And each region
sits under the **central power's hold**: a tithe, a license, a monopoly on a necessity, a
debt, a hostage, a garrison. Those two facts together set the region's politics: what the
local power would do if the hold were loosened, and what the hold currently costs them.

The question that turns a map into a campaign is what **joining the player costs the
region**. In a campaign where the player wins regions, recruits their champions or earns
their loyalty, the naive version treats each region as a trophy: beat the boss, collect the
region, move on. Nothing was spent, so the late-campaign coalition is a list of names. When
joining has a price — the central power raises the tithe, cuts the supply, calls in the debt,
punishes the dissenter, or the local power loses standing with its own people — each region
becomes a decision the region makes, and the coalition at the end is something the player
owes. The price is paid on screen, in the region's own currency, at a moment the player can
see, and it is a loss measured against what the region would have had without joining. A
downgrade the region's ordinary progression would have produced anyway — every rival moving
up a car on the same schedule — is not a price, however it is voiced. The sharpest price is the thing the region's own code exists to protect — the neutral
pass that must take a side, the honest log that must be produced and gets its keeper erased —
because then joining costs the region a piece of who it is, not only a sum. And a local power
joins on terms, when its own want is protected, not because it was beaten. See
[what-the-local-power-wants-and-what-joining-costs](./techniques/what-the-local-power-wants-and-what-joining-costs.md).

## The world is shown by its residue

Worldbuilding fails in two opposite directions. The common one is exposition: the opening
crawl, the codex, the old man who explains the war. Exposition front-loads facts the player
has no reason yet to care about, and the player skips it, so the world is both told and
unknown. The less discussed failure is opacity: a writer who has learned not to explain and
now implies a world so obliquely that nothing lands and the player cannot tell what matters.

World by implication sits between them. The history is written in full in the bible and
almost none of it is said; what reaches the player is its **residue** — the price of water on
a sign, a rule painted on a wall, a word everyone uses and nobody defines, a ruin nobody
mentions, a toll everyone pays without complaint, a name that is a compressed event. A player
who meets three consistent residues of the same history infers a history, and an inferred
history is held more firmly than a told one, because the player did the work. It is not
always the same history: players bring their own frames to the same scene and reach
different conclusions, and much of what a residue-built world is admired for reaches its
audience through other players' explanations rather than their own inference. So residue
carries the history the player may take or leave. A fact the plot depends on gets residues
in several channels and, at the moment the player needs it, one plain statement. The guard
against opacity is that every implied fact has at least one touchpoint the player actually
uses — a price they pay, a rule that changes what they may do, a word that appears on a
screen they must read. A fact with no touchpoint is lore, and lore is cut first. See
[world-by-implication](./techniques/world-by-implication.md).

A line of the economy the player reads but cannot act on — a deduction on a receipt — is
both a system and a residue, and it splits. The number is shown plainly, because the
player's money must reconcile. The label speaks in the world's voice — the centre's
euphemism, a fee that sounds reasonable — until the story's reveal renames it. A receipt that
names the theft from the first race has spent the reveal on a label.

The budget is the rule that makes implication hold: a region gets very few lines of outright
explanation, each spent on a fact the player needs in order to act, and everything else must
be carried by objects, prices, rules, names and behaviour. What the budget is spent on matters
as much as its size. Spend it on how people live now, never on how the world ended — survivors
care about consequences, and a catastrophe left unexplained costs nothing. Keep the system in
the background and a person's want in the foreground, because a system stared at directly
becomes a lecture and a system glimpsed behind someone's errand becomes a world. And show a
world that is repaired, not only ruined: a patch, a retrofit, a part made to fit what it was
never meant for implies people who stayed and kept things running, where uniform rubble
implies only a past.

## The supply chain is the map

A campaign that orders its regions by biome variety, or by difficulty alone, presents the
map as a menu: places to visit, in an order that means nothing. A campaign that orders its
regions along the movement of value presents the map as an argument. Raw material is taken at
the edge, refined in the next region, moved by the one after, sold in the market, and the
surplus ends up at the centre that produces nothing and consumes everything. A player who
travels the map in that order watches each link take its cut and arrives at the centre already
knowing who it is fed by. The chain is legible only when it is short: two or three goods
everyone needs, each with a known holder, and the crossings between regions — the gate, the
bridge, the pass — staged as places where it matters who is allowed through. See
[follow-the-money-as-map-structure](./techniques/follow-the-money-as-map-structure.md).

The structure does three jobs for free. Each region's economy is defined by its neighbours —
its import is the previous region's export — so the cultures cannot drift into
interchangeability. The difficulty ramp and the ramp of proximity to power coincide, so the
regions get harder because they are closer to the people who profit, which is a reason a
player feels without being told; where the geography can climb with it — up a pass, down into
the basin where the centre sits — the physical arc and the economic one become one crescendo.
And the theme is carried by the route rather than said by a character. Once the order carries
all three, swapping two regions for a narrative convenience breaks more than it gains, and the
order should be defended as a decision rather than treated as a sequence. That holds for a
campaign whose route is fixed. Where choosing the order is itself a pillar of the design, a
fixed chain costs that pillar; the chain then survives as the world's logic, readable from
any region because each region's import names its neighbour, rather than as the route. The rule that keeps it honest: the campaign's economy as the player handles it —
prices, purses, repair costs — must read from the same supply chain the story claims, because
a player checks the story's claim against what their money does.

## The spectacle has a balance sheet

When the campaign is a blood sport, a league, an arena or any staged violence, the spectacle
is not scenery. It is an institution, and it exists because somebody profits from it — and
what they profit is rarely the gate money. The spectacle is the means; what its funders buy
with it is something else: legitimacy, popularity that pays off in loyalty, fear, labour
discipline, a channel for anger that would otherwise go somewhere worse, or standing displayed
for its own sake. A world built around
it answers three questions: **who profits** — the owners, the bookmakers, the sponsors, the
local powers who take a cut and keep their licence, the central power that uses the spectacle
to recruit, to distract or to settle what it could not settle openly; **who pays** — the
competitors, their families, the regions whose labour and material fund it, the excluded who
get nothing from it, and the dead; and **what the audience wants** — blood, but also hope, the
story of the nobody who made it, a loyalty to bet on, and a place to see their own region win
something.

The third question is the one teams skip, and it is the one that makes the world move. An
audience with wants is a faction — in the end it is the system's real constituency: its
appetite shifts, it can be won and lost, and its demands drive the league's rules. The most
uncomfortable and most productive fact in this territory is that the protagonist's success is
the league's product: every win sells the hope the system runs on, so the player's rise
strengthens the thing they mean to bring down. Its flip side is what makes a finale: a winner
popular enough stops being product and becomes a threat, because the crowd's loyalty now has
somewhere to go that the owners do not control, and the system's answer is to try to remove
that winner in public. The campaign has to say what the player does with a popularity that
was never theirs. See
[political-economy-of-spectacle](./techniques/political-economy-of-spectacle.md).

## Failure modes of the naive reading

**The palette region.** Each region distinct in art and identical in people. Fixed by
deriving the culture from the economy, not by writing more lore.

**Flavour slang.** Coined words picked because they sound good, unconnected to work or fear.
They read as costume and they are inconsistent by the third region.

**The unanimous code.** Every local agrees with the local moral. The code is a motto and the
region has no politics.

**The trophy region.** Winning the region costs the region nothing. The final coalition is a
roll call.

**The lore dump** and its mirror, **the riddle world.** Told and skipped; or implied and lost.

**The menu map.** Regions ordered for variety; the route carries no argument and the economy
the player handles contradicts the one the story claims.

**The faceless crowd.** An audience that only cheers. A spectacle with no balance sheet is a
backdrop, and the player's success in it means nothing beyond the score.

## What this subject cannot decide

Whether a culture is interesting is taste. Whether its lines are well written is dialogue
craft. Whether players actually infer the history from its residue is a playtest question,
and a bible in which every region answers every question has paid for a culture on paper
only — [structural proof is necessary and never sufficient](../../../_laws.md#structural-proof-is-never-sufficient).
A filled table proves the questions were answered; only a reader in play, and then a player,
proves the answers reach anyone.

The evidence behind the techniques is stated in each. The strongest claims — culture derived
from material conditions, factions that contain internal dissent, a world shown through its
residue, and spectacle as something its funders buy power with — rest on working creators' own
statements and on historical sources; the per-region price of joining and the euphemism of the
centre are practitioner judgement and design inference; none of it has been tested in a played
game. Three of them were bounded by counter-evidence on 2026-10-10. Derivation is one route to
a consistent culture, not the only one: worlds begun from language or from mood words are on
record from their own makers. Inference from residue varies from player to player, by a
level designer's own account. And the reading of the ancient arena as bought power is
contested; one school of historians reads its sponsors' giving as display of grandeur, an end
in itself. A fiction may still write the spectacle as a purchase, but that is a choice the
fiction makes, not a fact history hands it.

## Boundaries

**Against procedural-level-planning.** That subject owns the zone graph: whether every region
is reachable, whether its difficulty band follows from the bands that lead into it, and
whether its partition and tables are single-sourced; the region and theme descriptions it
accepts are prose inputs to a generator. This subject owns what those regions *mean* — who
lives there, what they make, what they want and what it costs them to side with the player.
The rule for picking: if the question is whether a region can be reached, in what band, or
how its space is laid out, it is the neighbour's; if it is who the player meets there and why
they behave as they do, it is this one's. The two meet at region order: this subject proposes
the order the supply chain implies, and the neighbour's ordering check decides whether that
order is a step or a wall.

**Against ending-first-narrative-structure.** That sibling owns the campaign's shape from the
final card backwards — what each unit must plant and reveal, and the theme carried by
structure. This subject owns the world the units take place in. They meet where the region
order carries the theme: the sibling decides what the route must argue and what each unit
reveals; this subject supplies the economy, the cultures and the prices of joining that make
the route an argument rather than a sequence. When the question is what happens and in which
unit, read the sibling; when it is what the place is and who pays for what, read this one.

**Against antagonist-fair-grievance-craft.** That sibling owns the central antagonist as a
character — his grievance, his means, the necessity he rations in public, the leash. This
subject owns the system of regions he sits on top of: the local powers below him, their own
wants, the specific shape his hold takes in each region and what joining the player costs
them. The rule: if the fix changes what he wants, holds or does, it is the sibling's; if it
changes what a region makes, believes or pays, it is this one's. His rationing is one link in
the supply chain this subject maps; the chain itself, and who else profits from it, is here.
How debt and loss are staged as systems belongs to the sibling that owns debt and loss
staging; here debt appears only as one form a central hold can take. And a local power who
refuses before turning, or who joins at personal cost, is a character arc owned by the
sibling that holds the rival-to-ally turn; this subject owns only what the turn costs the
region.
