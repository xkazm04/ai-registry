# Shipped artifact as primary evidence

For a candidate with no employment record, the made thing is the file. A thesis, a
working project, a portfolio, a published piece of analysis, a tool other people use —
this is the only item that was produced under real constraints, that can be examined
rather than believed, and that a reviewer can form an independent judgment about. The
technique makes that primacy explicit in three places where it is usually left implicit:
what you ask for, what you weight, and what you actually open.

## The procedure

**In intake.** The completeness prompt names the artifact as the strongest evidence, in
those words, and shows the candidate what it is worth. A checklist that lists "project
or thesis" beside "phone number" with equal visual standing is telling the applicant
those items are comparable. Weight the artifact item at roughly twice any other single
completeness item, label it plainly as the strongest thing they can add, and put it
first. This costs nothing and changes what arrives.

**In weighting.** An artifact with a real description enters at the tier its provenance
earns — evidence of doing, not of claiming — and it outranks every self-description of
the same skill. Where an artifact and a claim conflict, the artifact wins; where an
artifact is present and a claim is absent, the artifact still counts, because the
person demonstrated something they did not think to list.

**In review.** Someone opens it. The failure that makes this whole technique a lie is
weighting an artifact heavily and never examining it, which rewards *having a link* and
penalises the candidate who built the harder thing and described it modestly. If your
process cannot afford to open every artifact, weight it at the tier you can afford to
verify and say so, rather than pricing a signal you do not inspect.

## What a reviewer reads it for

- **Scope actually completed** versus scope announced. A finished small thing beats an
  ambitious unfinished one, every time, and it is the closest available proxy for
  delivery under constraint.
- **Decisions with reasons.** The value is rarely the artifact's polish; it is whether
  the person can say why they made the choices they made when asked. That question
  belongs in the interview, and the artifact is what makes it answerable.
- **Contribution boundary.** Group projects, course templates and assisted work are
  normal and not disqualifying. Ask what part was theirs; record the answer as what it
  is — the candidate's account — not as a verified fact.

## The path upward: observation mints evidence

An artifact is the best evidence the *file* can hold, but it is not the ceiling. The
strongest evidence available to a person with no history is capability observed
directly — a work sample submitted and examined, a skill demonstrated live and quoted.
Where your evidence hierarchy allows observation to reach the top rung, an early-career
candidate has a route to full credit that does not run through employment; where it does
not, the population is capped no matter how the rubric is tuned.

Two disciplines make minting observed evidence safe. **Mint only from strong signal**:
require a real competence bar, require the rating to rest on quoted or submitted
material rather than impression, and refuse to mint from an assessment whose own
confidence was low. And **mint asymmetrically** — a strong performance adds evidence; a
weak one adds nothing and subtracts nothing. An assessment that can lower a score turns
into a gate the candidate did not know they were walking through, and it will be a gate
disproportionately fired by nerves, bandwidth and language.

## Decision rules

- **When an artifact is present, it becomes the anchor for the interview.** The single
  highest-yield early-career round is a walkthrough of their own work, because it is the
  only topic where the candidate has more information than the interviewer and the
  interviewer can still probe depth.
- **When an artifact is absent, record absent — never infer weakness.** Unpaid building
  time is unequally available, and its absence is a fact about circumstances at least as
  often as about capability. Absence lowers the readiness score through a missing input,
  which is enough; it must not additionally trigger a penalty.
- **When you cannot open or reach an artifact, that is unverified, not disproved.** Say
  "could not review", never "no substantive work found"
  ([say-only-what-the-record-holds](../../../_laws.md#say-only-what-the-record-holds)).
- **When a model summarizes an artifact, the summary is a hypothesis.** Render it as
  one, with the probe it suggests, distinct from anything a human examined
  ([inference-must-look-like-inference](../../../_laws.md#inference-must-look-like-inference)).
  A generated reading of a repository is not a code review, and the difference must be
  visible to whoever acts on it.
- **When an artifact contradicts the file, prefer the artifact and flag the conflict for
  a human** — do not silently reconcile. Contradiction handling belongs to the routing
  and authenticity subjects; this technique only insists the artifact is not the side
  that gets discarded.

## When not to use it

- **Not where the work cannot be shared.** Confidential, clinical, classified or
  employer-owned work exists in most fields; a portfolio requirement in those domains is
  a filter on who had permissive employers. Offer an equivalent lane — a described piece
  of work probed live, a work sample you set — and price it the same.
- **Not as an application gate.** Requiring an artifact to apply excludes exactly the
  candidates whose time is spoken for by paid work or care.
- **Not as a proxy for authenticity screening.** Whether the artifact is really theirs
  is a separate discipline with its own techniques; over-reading provenance from a file
  listing produces confident accusations that a hiring process cannot survive making.
