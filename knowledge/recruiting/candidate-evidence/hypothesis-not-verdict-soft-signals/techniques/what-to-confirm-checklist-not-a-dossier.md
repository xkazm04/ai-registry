# A "what to confirm" checklist, not a dossier

The same set of signals can be assembled two ways. One is a page **about a
person** — their patterns, their apparent weaknesses, what the system makes of
them. The other is a list of **things to confirm** — bounded, ordered by what
would change the next decision, each item ending in a question. The underlying
data is identical. The artifacts are not remotely equivalent, because a reader
does what the artifact's form invites, and one of these forms invites judgment
while the other invites asking.

Everything else in this subject produces good signals. This technique is about
what they are assembled into, and it is where good signals most often go wrong.

## Dossier tells

Diagnose the artifact, not the intent:

- **Rows describe the person rather than the record.** "Tends to move on quickly"
  versus "average tenure 1.4 years across 4 roles — confirm the reasons".
- **The list is unbounded.** Everything the detectors found, in detector order.
  Length signals thoroughness and reads as weight of evidence.
- **There is no favourable column**, or there is one and it is shorter, lower, or
  collapsed by default.
- **Nothing on it is actionable.** No probes, or probes phrased as areas of
  concern rather than as questions someone could ask on Thursday.
- **It persists unchanged after the interview.** A checklist empties as questions
  get answered; a dossier accumulates, and by the third round it is a file.
- **It is exportable as a narrative.** If someone can copy it into a message and
  it reads as an assessment of a human being, it is one.

## Procedure

1. **Order by decision impact, not by severity or by detector.** The top item is
   the one whose answer most changes what happens next. Severity ordering
   manufactures a case; impact ordering manufactures an agenda.
2. **Cap the list hard — three to five open items.** The cap is not cosmetic: it
   forces the ranking in step 1 to be real, and it matches what a panel can
   actually ask inside a scheduled round.
3. **Every row is an item to confirm, and its verb is a verb of asking.** Confirm,
   ask, check, demonstrate. No row's verb is assess, judge, note or consider.
4. **Render risk and strength in one list, interleaved by impact**, not as two
   sections. Two sections get read as prosecution and defence; one list gets read
   as an agenda.
5. **Give every row a state**: open, confirmed, refuted, not asked. Rows leave the
   open list as the process answers them, and a refuted row stays visible as
   refuted so nobody re-raises it in the next round.
6. **Record who confirmed what.** A resolved item names the human who resolved it
   and when, because a checklist that clears itself is a machine deciding
   ([every-decision-names-its-actor](../../../_laws.md#every-decision-names-its-actor)).
7. **Keep the artifact internal and scoped to the round.** It is working material
   for a panel, not a record about a person that travels; and nothing on it may
   become a rejection reason, because unconfirmed hypotheses are not things the
   record holds
   ([say-only-what-the-record-holds](../../../_laws.md#say-only-what-the-record-holds)).
8. **Show what the checklist did not look at.** A list of five items reads as
   exhaustive unless the surface states its scope. Bound it in the same place the
   items are shown.

## Decision rules

- **When an item cannot be phrased as something to confirm, it does not belong on
  the checklist** — this rule alone removes most dossier content, because
  character observations resist the phrasing by nature.
- **When the checklist exceeds its cap, drop from the bottom rather than
  paginate.** An item nobody will ask is not being preserved by scrolling; it is
  being preserved as ambient suspicion.
- **When the panel closes the round without asking an item, it renders as "not
  asked", never as "unresolved concern".** The distinction is the difference
  between a gap in your process and a mark against a person.
- **When the checklist is the only artifact reaching a decision-maker, it must be
  visibly incapable of being a recommendation** — no aggregate count, no summary
  verdict, no colour that reads as a grade. Aggregation is where a checklist turns
  back into a score.
- **When an adverse outcome follows, the file must show a human deciding on
  evidence, not on the checklist.** The checklist is the agenda that produced the
  evidence; it is never the evidence
  ([no-adverse-outcome-is-solely-automated](../../../_laws.md#no-adverse-outcome-is-solely-automated)).

## When not to use it

- **Where the panel already has a structured scorecard covering the same ground.**
  A checklist that duplicates the competencies being scored splits attention and
  double-counts; the probes should be folded into the scorecard's questions and
  the separate surface dropped. That handoff belongs to the interview-scorecard
  sibling.
- **For hard eligibility facts.** A required credential is a gate, not a
  confirmation item, and burying it in a checklist of soft questions loses it.
- **Where no human will read it before the next decision.** An automated consumer
  of a "what to confirm" list will do something with it, and the only thing it can
  do is treat unconfirmed items as findings. If nobody is reading, do not generate.
