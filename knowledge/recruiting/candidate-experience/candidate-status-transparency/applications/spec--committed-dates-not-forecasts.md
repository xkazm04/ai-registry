# Dates in hiring notices, as the texts and the data have them

**Pin.** Retrieved 2026-09-28 with curl; every quotation below was matched
against the fetched text after drafting. The public-sector texts move with
administrations and the benchmark is annual, so the clock is six months.

## What the texts require, and what they leave out

- **The US federal hiring memo (OMB/OPM M-24-16, 14 August 2024), p.4**
  requires "timely communications to applicants about their application status
  with notifications for each applicable stage of the hiring process, including
  application acceptance, determination of qualifications, issuance of
  certificates to hiring manager, and selection or cancellation of announcement
  and using data analytics to verify applicant notifications were sent".
- **OPM's FAQ on the memo (August 2024), p.2**, declines to set a number: "The
  memo does not define timely since there is no one-size-fits-all solution.
  However, agencies should have timelines that work for them and ensure they
  are being met." That is this technique's rule 2 as policy: a timeline is
  owned by the organisation and its keep-rate is checked. Its leading practice:
  "When using multiple hurdles, notify applicants of the results at the
  completion of each hurdle."
- **The federal job site's own help (updated September 2026)** forecasts
  nothing ("The amount of time it takes to hire someone varies depending on the
  job you're applying to, how many people have applied and the hiring
  agency"), and states the one owned fact that explains most of the silence:
  "Agencies will not review your application until the job announcement
  closes."
- **None of these texts forbids a forecast, and none requires a date.** The
  golden path's ban on forecasting a human decision rests on the
  inference law, not on a text.

## What the evidence says about telling people when

- **SIOP's 2020 candidate-experience paper** puts timing inside fair feedback.
  Its Selection Procedural Justice Scale includes "I had a clear understanding
  of when I would get my test results", and its practice table says "track
  time-to-feedback for each selection hurdle".
- **Talent Board's 2023 benchmark report** (a practitioner benchmark, employers
  self-selected): "only 16% of candidates were told how long the application
  process would take". Its better-rated employers "establish follow-up dates
  with finalists and keep those dates 16% more often". Both numbers describe
  participating employers only.
- **A tax authority's research on avoidable calls (Ipsos MORI for HMRC,
  Report 352, 2015)** is the out-of-domain case for anchoring. People who had
  an acknowledgement still called, some "unsure of when the three week
  timescale began", and "recall of having received the message or the
  information in it was low". Its recommendation: "Include a date in the SMS
  rather than a timescale for processing." A professional body's 2023 note on
  the same authority's progress tool found that "many progress-chasing queries
  were not near the deadline", with the tool unused. That is the evidence
  behind the golden path's condition that deflection is a hypothesis to
  measure.
- **A UK fire and rescue service's recruitment page** publishes a dated,
  seven-step timetable for its whole campaign ("Wholetime Duty System 2025
  Recruitment Timeline"). This is what an owned schedule looks like when it is
  published: every applicant sees the same dates, set by the organisation.

## What this changes

The golden path's "Deliberately absent: predicted dates" becomes "forecasts of
a human decision", and the owned-dates paragraph is new. The timeline's "no
date" rule gains the owned-date exception. Nothing here supports a
per-candidate predicted decision date, and the technique still refuses one.
