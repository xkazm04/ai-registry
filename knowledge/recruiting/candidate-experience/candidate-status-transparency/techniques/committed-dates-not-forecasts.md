# Committed dates, not forecasts

The concern: the rule "never show a predicted decision date" is right, and it
is routinely over-applied into "never show a date". The result is a status
page that answers *where* and never *when*. The candidate then does what
anyone does with an open question about their livelihood: they chase. Being
told when you will hear is part of what applicants mean by timely, fair
feedback, and few are told it.

The line is not between showing a date and showing none. It is **who controls
the date**. A forecast is a date that depends on a decision the organisation
has not taken and does not schedule: "decision expected by the 14th". A
commitment is a date the organisation sets, can keep by its own action, and
measures itself against: "applications are reviewed after the posting closes
on the 3rd", "you will hear from us by the 20th, whatever the outcome". The
first is a guess in the grammar of a promise. The second *is* a promise, and
promises are what a status page is for.

## The procedure

1. **Inventory the dates the organisation already owns.** When the posting
   closes. When review of a batch starts. The dates of an assessment centre or
   a campaign's fixed stages. The deadline on anything the candidate was sent
   to act on. The team's own service level for replying at each stage, where
   it has one. These exist in most hiring setups. They are usually kept
   internal, which is the whole gap.
2. **Publish a date only if its keep-rate is measured.** An internal service
   level becomes a promise the moment a candidate reads it. Before it crosses
   the boundary, the team must be able to say how often it is met, from its
   own records, per
   [a-claim-carries-its-sample-and-its-basis](../../../_laws.md#a-claim-carries-its-sample-and-its-basis).
   A target that is met half the time is a forecast with a nicer name. Either
   publish a looser date that is kept, or keep the target internal.
3. **Anchor to the calendar.** Write "by Friday 20 March", not "within three
   weeks". A relative timescale leaves the candidate to guess when the clock
   started, and people who guess wrong chase before the deadline has passed.
   The page holds the anchor (the date of the event the clock runs from), so
   it can render the absolute date.
4. **Promise the reply, not the decision.** "You will hear from us by the
   20th" is keepable whatever happens, because "we have not decided yet, and
   here is the next date" is itself a reply. "You will have a decision by the
   20th" is only keepable if the panel cooperates, and that is exactly the
   forecast this technique does not allow.
5. **When a committed date will slip, say so before it passes, with the new
   date.** A commitment the organisation missed in silence has turned into the
   thing the golden path forbids. The status view shows the moved date and
   says it moved. It does not quietly overwrite the old one.
6. **Render recorded dates and committed dates differently.** "Interview held
   on the 9th" is a fact. "You will hear by the 20th" is a commitment. Per
   [inference-must-look-like-inference](../../../_laws.md#inference-must-look-like-inference)
   the page must not let a commitment look like a recorded event, or the
   reverse. A label such as "we will reply by" is enough.

## Decision rules

- **When the date depends on a person's decision the process does not
  schedule, it is a forecast. Leave it out.** Offer the next date the
  organisation *does* control instead, even if it is only a reply date.
- **When the only honest date is far away, show it anyway.** "Review starts
  after the posting closes on the 30th" is more useful than silence, even
  a month out. It tells the candidate that the quiet is expected.
- **When reviews are batched, the batch date is the most valuable date on the
  page.** Continuous-looking progress over a batch process is decorative. The
  timeline sibling says when to use a single sentence instead of a spine, and
  this is that sentence.
- **When a deadline binds the candidate, show it on the status view too.**
  An action's closing date is a commitment the organisation has already made
  and enforces. Hiding it on the one page the candidate returns to only makes
  it easy to miss.
- **Never show a "typical" duration drawn from other applicants' journeys as
  if it were this application's date.** A median of past cases is a forecast
  with a sample behind it. If it is shown at all, it is shown as what it is
  ("most applications at this stage hear back within ten working days"),
  anchored, with its basis, and never in the space where a committed date
  would go.

## When NOT to use it

- **No date is owned.** If nothing about the process is scheduled or measured,
  do not invent a service level to have something to display. Say what has
  happened, and fix the process first.
- **Individually negotiated late stages.** Offer negotiation and final panels
  often move on the candidate's own schedule as much as the team's. A
  one-to-one conversation carries the date, and the page says who owns the
  next step.
- **Internal targets used for team pressure.** A stretch service level set to
  push a team is, by construction, one it often misses. That makes it the
  wrong thing to show a candidate. Publish the date the team keeps, not the
  one it aspires to.
