# Session-shape triage and turn budget

A fixed intake script is wrong for almost every requestor. Run fifteen
questions at someone who delivered the whole role in three sentences and you
teach them intake is bureaucracy; run the same fifteen at someone who cannot
compose the role at all and you produce fifteen shrugs. The first job of the
session is therefore **diagnostic**: within the opening turns, classify the
shape of the conversation you are in, and set the turn budget and question
order from that rather than from a template.

## The delivery-shape heuristic

The useful first cut is not the requestor's seniority or function but **how
they deliver information**:

- **Power-unit delivery.** Dense, pre-compressed answers; one sentence
  carries an outcome, a constraint and a requirement. They have thought about
  the role; what they have not done is expose the reasoning inside the
  compression. *Budget: eight substantive turns or fewer.* Spend it on
  laddering and the disqualification test. Reinstatement is usually
  unnecessary and reflection is low-yield — their answers are already
  complete.
- **Story delivery.** Narrative, rich in incident, thin in specification: the
  content is present but unsorted and the requestor is discovering their own
  view as they talk. *Budget: fifteen to twenty-five turns.* Spend it on
  reflections that harvest and consolidate and on converting episodes into
  outcomes; interrupting with a structured question list destroys the
  mechanism producing your content. A hidden macro-structure keeps length
  from becoming a wander — goal, current reality, options, commitment — never
  announced, but used to decide what the next turn is for.
- **Empty delivery.** Neither compressed nor narrative: short, generic,
  option-seeking. They are not withholding; they genuinely cannot compose the
  role. *Budget: medium, with an inverted method.* Supply the frame
  aggressively — the ninety-day question, a concrete scene, a this-or-that
  contrast used earlier than usual and always framed as disposable — while
  sourcing every substantive claim from them. Do not
  mistake their agreement with your frame for their authorship of the
  content.

### Diagnosing it

Diagnose from the first one or two requestor turns, on observable signals
rather than impression:

- **Toward the short path:** an explicit reference to an existing seat — a
  backfill, a replacement, "another one", "same as last time", a pointer to a
  previous description; a first answer that is already concrete (a title, a
  team, two or more specific capabilities); and, decisively, a first ladder
  probe that bottoms out — the "why" returns the same content restated.
- **Toward the long path:** hedging density ("not sure", "kind of", "we
  think", "maybe"); a mismatch between the level they named and the scope
  they described; solution words with no problem behind them; and first-hire
  markers ("we've never had one", "new team", "maybe it's two roles").

Three rules keep the triage honest.

- **Undetected defaults to the longer path, kept tight.** Over-serving a
  transactional requestor costs a few minutes of patience; under-serving one
  who needed the long path costs the brief. A late concrete signal may still
  collapse the session to the short path.
- **Re-diagnose once.** A requestor can start empty and open into story
  delivery once a scene loads; an apparent power-unit delivery sometimes
  turns out to be a memorized template with nothing behind it — the tell,
  again, is that laddering the first requirement produces nothing.
- **A signal-based triage is language-fragile and must be exercised in every
  language the session runs in.** In an inflecting language a marker list
  built from dictionary forms silently matches nothing; every backfill in
  that language then falls to the long path — safe but wrong, and unnoticed
  because the failure still produces a working session. Where an automated
  interviewer may override the heuristic, the override is justified and
  recorded, never silent: a triage nobody can inspect cannot be corrected.

## Requestor patterns worth naming

Delivery shape sets the budget; the requestor's characteristic distortion
sets which techniques get the turns. The recurring ones:

- **The over-specifier** — arrives with twelve must-haves. Ladder the top
  ones, then move straight to the disqualification test and the ranking of
  the top three. Do not try to ladder all twelve; you will run out of session
  and goodwill.
- **The solution-jumper** — leads with a title, a level, a tool list or a
  named person. Quarantine at first mention and hold the return commitment.
- **The template-reuser** — hands over the previous incumbent's description.
  Quarantine the document, then dig it against the described work; documents
  concede contradictions cheaply.
- **The portrait-drawer** — specifies the person who left rather than the
  work. Redirect to what that person *did* that mattered, and watch for
  characteristics that must not become requirements.
- **The budget-evader** — deflects compensation. Ask once, accept the
  deflection, record it as explicitly deferred rather than silently absent.
- **The delegator** — is not the person with the need. Name it early: either
  reschedule with the person who has the episode, or run the session marked
  second-hand throughout.
- **The evaluation-anxious** — embarrassed not to know; answers shorten as
  questions get harder. Say the non-judgment explicitly and invert the
  funnel — open with what they can certainly answer (who they work with,
  what is on their plate), reach seniority and budget after the guard drops.
- **The role-existence doubter** — questions whether the role should exist at
  all. Route to the ninety-day outcomes and offer role-shape hypotheses as
  disposable options; never argue for the hire.
- **The derailer** — drifts into reorg politics. Pull the agenda back gently,
  but harvest the drift: the reason the seat exists is usually inside it.
- **The urgency-driver** — every answer is a deadline. Convert urgency into a
  constraint and a trade-off question rather than letting it truncate the
  session; a fast wrong brief is the most expensive artifact in hiring.

These are diagnostic aids, not boxes. A requestor is allowed to be two of
them, and the value is in the *technique routing*, not the label.

## The turn budget

- **Set the budget explicitly and early**, and tell the requestor the shape
  of the session: people pace themselves to a stated structure and ramble
  against an unstated one.
- **Protect the core slots before spending on depth.** The ninety-day
  outcome, the must line, the seniority question and compensation are what
  the whole downstream depends on; depth that costs the session its outcome
  question is a bad trade however rich the material.
- **Order is fixed even when length is not.** Scene, outcome, requirements,
  trade-offs, compensation, then the return to anything parked. A shorter
  session runs the same order with fewer turns per slot; it does not reorder.
- **When the budget runs out, close honestly.** Read back what was captured,
  name what was not reached, schedule the remainder. A session that ends by
  inventing the unreached parts is worse than one that ends short.
- **Record every unreached slot as unreached.** This is where the technique
  meets
  [absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence](../../../_laws.md#absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence):
  a short budget is a legitimate choice, but the brief it produces must
  distinguish "asked and there is no constraint" from "never asked". A
  compensation slot rendered blank because the session ran out becomes,
  downstream, an unbounded band; an unreached technical-depth slot becomes a
  role with no technical bar. The unreached state is a value, not an empty
  field.

## Automation note

An automated intake makes triage more important, not less: it has no ambient
read of the room and its default failure is to run the whole script at
everyone. Two rules keep it honest. The diagnosis must be made from
observable delivery and be allowed to change mid-session; and the budget must
be a real stopping condition with an honest close, not a soft target the
script overruns — an intake that outstays its usefulness converts a
cooperative hiring manager into one who delegates the next kickoff to a
coordinator.

## When not to use it

- **In a re-open or a merge session**, where the shape is already known from
  the first session and the job is to update specific fields rather than to
  characterize a requestor.
- **When the requestor has explicitly bounded the meeting** ("I have ten
  minutes"). Their constraint is the budget; triage only the ordering.
- **As a label attached to a person across sessions.** The shape is a
  property of *this conversation about this role*, and a requestor recorded
  as "the over-specifier" in a shared system is a judgment about a colleague
  that no intake process should be producing.
