# Context-reinstatement opening

The first question of an intake session decides the altitude of every answer
that follows. Open at the abstraction the requisition lives at — "what are
you looking for?", "tell me about the role" — and the requestor answers from
the same abstraction: a title, a level, a shape of person. Open by putting
them back inside a concrete remembered situation, and they answer from
episodic memory, where the specifics actually live.

This is the interviewing craft's oldest reliable finding, borrowed from
investigative interviewing: **mentally reinstating the context of an event
before asking about it yields on the order of a third more correct recall
without a matching rise in errors** — more detail, not more invention — and
it does so without the leading effect that a menu of options introduces. Recruiting has a direct application, because the requestor's real
knowledge of the role is not stored as a specification. It is stored as
memories of work going undone, of a launch that slipped, of the thing they
personally picked up last quarter because nobody else could.

## The procedure

0. **Pair the anchor with the non-judgment, in the same opening turn.** "No
   wrong answers here, and vague is fine — that's what this session is for.
   Now think back to last month..." The two moves are complementary: the
   non-judgment lowers the guard that keeps answers short and safe, and the
   anchor gives the lowered guard somewhere concrete to go. Delivered
   separately, the reassurance reads as filler.
1. **Choose a reinstatement anchor**, in this order of preference:
   - the most recent moment the gap actually hurt ("think about the last time
     you wished this person was already here — what was happening that
     week?");
   - the work currently sitting undone or wrongly assigned ("what is on your
     plate right now that would move to them on day one?");
   - the predecessor's real week, if this is a backfill and the predecessor is
     safe to discuss ("walk me through what a normal Tuesday looked like for
     the person who left");
   - the team as a working system, for a genuinely new role ("who is
     currently absorbing the work this person would take, and how?").
2. **Ask for the scene, not the summary.** Time, participants, what was
   happening, what was blocked. The goal is that they narrate rather than
   classify.
3. **Stay in the scene for at least two exchanges** before moving. The first
   answer is usually still summary; the specifics arrive on the second or
   third turn, once the memory is genuinely loaded.
4. **Harvest concrete nouns and verbs and reuse them verbatim** for the rest
   of the session. The scene is where the requestor's own vocabulary for the
   work is produced; everything later — laddering, reflections, the
   outcome question — is more productive when phrased in it.
5. **Bridge from the scene to the outcome question**, not to requirements:
   "so if they'd been here that week — by the end of their first ninety days,
   what would have to be true for you to feel that hire worked?" The scene
   makes the ninety-day question answerable for requestors who would
   otherwise stall on it.

## Decision rules

- **When the requestor opens with a title or an existing description, do not
  engage with it — reinstate first, then return to it.** Engaging first
  anchors the session in the artifact; reinstating first gives you a scene
  against which the artifact can be tested. (Park the artifact explicitly;
  see premature-solution quarantine.)
- **When an answer arrives in the present tense and the plural ("we need
  someone who can generally..."), the scene has not loaded.** Do not push
  harder with the same question — change the anchor. A requestor who cannot
  produce a scene for one anchor will often produce a vivid one for another.
- **When the role is a backfill and the departure was contentious, use the
  work-undone anchor rather than the predecessor anchor.** The predecessor
  anchor is the most vivid one available and also the one most likely to
  produce a specification of a *person* — sometimes an unlawful one. A brief
  built as a portrait of the leaver is the most common way an intake produces
  a discriminatory requirement without anyone intending it.
- **When this is a first-of-its-kind role, expect reinstatement to fail and
  say so.** There is no episode to reinstate. Substitute a *prospective*
  scene — "describe the week, six months from now, that this hire has made
  possible" — and mark the resulting content as constructed rather than
  recalled. A prospective scene is a plan; a retrospective one is evidence,
  and the two do not carry the same weight when a requirement is later
  challenged.
- **Reinstate again after any long detour.** Context decays across a
  quarantine, a budget discussion, or a scheduling interruption; a one-line
  re-anchor ("back to that week you described — ...") costs a turn and
  restores the altitude.

## What reinstatement cannot do

Reinstatement improves recall of what the requestor experienced. It has no
power over what they never observed: how the work is actually done by the
people they do not manage, what the market prices the role at, or what a
competency would look like in evidence. Those are gaps the session must reach
by other means, and where it does not reach them, the gap is recorded as
*unreached*, never as absence of a requirement. A brief that renders "we did
not discuss the technical depth needed" identically to "no technical depth is
needed" has violated
[absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence](../../../_laws.md#absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence)
at the point where the violation is cheapest to prevent and most expensive to
detect.

## When not to use it

- **In a re-open of an intake completed days earlier.** A full reinstatement
  re-runs a conversation you already have; open instead by reading back the
  current brief's outcomes and asking what has changed. Reinstating twice
  from cold trains requestors to experience intake as repetition.
- **In a triage-length session** with a requestor who has already produced a
  crisp, outcome-shaped ask. Reinstatement is a recall aid; someone who has
  clearly recalled does not need it, and spending three turns on it burns the
  budget that laddering their requirements would have used better.
- **With a requestor who is not the person with the memory** — a coordinator
  relaying a request, an executive one level removed. Reinstating with them
  produces a vivid scene that is not evidence about the role. The correct
  move is to name the gap and get the session in front of the person who has
  the episode, or to run the session explicitly as a proxy intake whose
  content is marked as second-hand.
