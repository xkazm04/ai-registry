# Framing-not-legal-advice disclaimer

## The concern

Between saying nothing about law and claiming conformance with it there is a
narrow, honest, genuinely useful position: **framing**. Naming the instruments
that apply, and stating which of your behaviours corresponds to which duty,
without asserting that a competent authority has agreed.

Both neighbours are worse. Saying nothing produces a compliance surface that
fails its only job — a talent team facing a procurement questionnaire cannot
use "we take privacy seriously", and a candidate cannot look up a duty you
declined to name. Claiming conformance produces a statement you cannot support:
certified conformance under most of these regimes is a formal act with an
assessment and often a registration behind it, and a sentence in a product is
not that act. The gap between "we align with" and "we are certified under" is
the gap between a defensible product claim and a misrepresentation.

The disclaimer is what makes the useful middle publishable. It is load-bearing,
not decorative.

## What the sentence must do

Three things, in one place:

1. **Name what these references are** — framing, orientation, a map of which
   instruments apply.
2. **Name what they are not** — not legal advice, and not a claim of certified
   conformance. Both denials are needed; teams routinely include the first and
   omit the second, and the second is the one that matters commercially.
3. **Name what the reader should do instead** — obtain their own advice for
   their own jurisdiction and circumstances. Without this the disclaimer reads
   as evasion rather than as a boundary.

## Procedure

1. **Place it at the source, not only at the surface.** The disclaimer belongs
   in the catalog itself, next to the data, so that every consumer inherits it
   and no new surface can render the instruments without it. A disclaimer that
   lives only in one page's footer is one refactor away from being absent.
2. **Keep it adjacent to the claim.** A boundary at the bottom of a long page
   does not bound what was read at the top. Where a specific surface makes a
   specific assertion, the boundary travels with the assertion.
3. **Do not style it as fine print.** Grey, small, collapsed text signals
   "ignore me", which is precisely the wrong signal for a sentence that
   determines how everything above it should be read. Legible, adjacent,
   ordinary weight
   ([law](../../../_laws.md#inference-must-look-like-inference)).
4. **Keep the vocabulary distinct.** Maintain a small, closed set of standing
   words — *aligns with*, *maps to*, *supports*, versus *certified*,
   *conformant*, *approved*, *audited by* — and use the second set only where a
   producible artifact backs it
   ([law](../../../_laws.md#say-only-what-the-record-holds)). Reviewing copy against
   a word list catches in seconds what a general reading misses.
5. **Distinguish framing from process claims.** "This process includes human
   review at every advancement gate" is a factual claim about your product and
   must be true; it is not a legal conclusion and does not need hedging. Hedging
   facts to sound careful is its own failure — it makes the true statements as
   weak as the legal ones.
6. **Version it with the catalog.** When the as-of date moves, the disclaimer's
   date moves with it, so a reader can tell whether the map they are reading was
   drawn before or after a change they know about.

## Decision rules

- When a claim can be supported by a producible artifact — an assessment
  report, an independent audit, a registration — say so and cite it. The
  disclaimer bounds the *unsupported* framing, and using it to hedge a claim
  you can actually prove wastes the strongest thing on the page.
- When a customer asks "are you compliant", answer with the article map, the
  role split and the gap register rather than with a yes. A yes is unprovable
  and a no is untrue; the register is both accurate and more useful than either.
- When a surface names a specific provision to a *candidate*, the framing
  boundary applies but the tone shifts: a candidate needs to know the duty that
  protects them and where to raise a concern, not a vendor's hedge. Name the
  right and the route; do not decorate a candidate's rights with commercial
  caution.
- When counsel reviews the surface, ask them specifically whether any sentence
  reads as advice to the reader. That, not the accuracy of the instrument
  names, is the exposure.

## When not to use this

Do not attach it to statements of fact about your own system. "Candidate data
is retained for a stated period", "a named person approves every rejection",
"these sub-processors handle candidate data" — these are commitments, and a
disclaimer on a commitment reads as an intent not to keep it.

Do not use it to license vagueness. A disclaimer does not make an inaccurate
instrument name acceptable; naming the wrong law and disclaiming advice is
still naming the wrong law, and the disclaimer will not be the sentence anyone
quotes back.
