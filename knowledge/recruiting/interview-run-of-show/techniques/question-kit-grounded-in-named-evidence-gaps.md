# Question kit grounded in named evidence gaps

A question earns its place in a plan by naming the **gap it closes** — a specific thing
this candidate's record does not settle — not by naming a topic it covers. This is the
difference between a question kit and a question list, and it is what makes the
interview add information rather than re-collect what is already known.

Compare: *"Tell me about a time you handled conflict"* against *"The record shows you
moved from leading a team of six to an individual contributor role at the next employer;
walk me through how that change came about."* The first is answerable identically by
every candidate alive and produces a story whose only variable is storytelling skill. The
second can only be answered by this person, and its answer changes what is known.

## The procedure

1. **Assemble the evidence set** — the record, the application material, whatever earlier
   rounds produced — and the competency set this round owns.
2. **List what is unsettled.** For each competency: what does the evidence already
   establish, and what does it leave open? The unsettled items are the gaps, and each one
   gets a short name. This step is where the kit's quality is decided; skip it and you
   get topics.
3. **Generate one question per gap**, phrased to be answerable only by someone who was
   actually there.
4. **Bucket the questions** by the kind of gap they close.
5. **Attach a scaffold** to each.
6. **Cut to the round's maximum question count**, keeping one question per competency
   before keeping a second for any.

## The bucket taxonomy, and why it needs a catch-all

Three working buckets:

- **Behavioural** — patterns of past conduct the record hints at but does not evidence.
- **Technical** — a claimed capability with no demonstration behind it. The gap here is
  usually not "can they do it" but "did they do the part they are claiming".
- **Defence** — the places where the record contains something a reviewer will worry
  about. See below; this bucket carries the most craft.

And a fourth, non-negotiable: **a catch-all.** Every taxonomy applied to generated
content eventually meets an item that fits nothing — a genuinely new kind of question, or
just a spelling variant of an existing label. The bucket label on a generated question is
untrusted free text, not a value from a closed set, and treating it as closed produces a
specific and nasty failure: the question is counted in the total but belongs to no group,
so it appears in no grouped view and is hidden by every filter. It is simultaneously
present and invisible, which is worse than being dropped, because the totals say nothing
is missing.

Route unclassifiable questions to a visible "other" group and let a human decide, and
hold the grouping to a checkable invariant: **the per-group counts sum to the total.**
That single assertion is what makes the catch-all real rather than aspirational. A
taxonomy without one is a silent filter, and the gap it filters out goes unprobed and is
recorded afterwards as unassessed for a reason nobody can reconstruct.

## The defence question

Where the record holds an apparent problem — an employment gap, a short tenure, a step
that looks like a step down, a missing credential, a project that ended badly — the kit
carries a question that **names the worry out loud** and invites the explanation.

This feels adversarial and is the opposite. The concern exists whether or not it is
voiced; unvoiced, it travels silently into the decision meeting where the candidate is
not present to answer it. Voiced, it becomes answerable. The practitioner's formulation
is that **defensiveness reads worse than the gap itself** — which is a claim about the
interviewer as much as the candidate. A question improvised in the moment on a delicate
subject comes out accusatory, the candidate hears an accusation, defends, and the
defence becomes the finding. Writing the question in advance is what keeps the tone an
invitation.

**When the record holds no worry, do not drop the bucket — invert the question.** A
clean record is not a reason to skip the hardest question in the kit; it is a reason to
ask the candidate to name their own weakest point against this role and say how they
would compensate. That question is often the most informative one in the pack, because
what it measures is calibration — whether a person knows the shape of what they do not
know — and calibration is one of the few things a conversation reads better than any
document does.

Three rules:

- **The worry is a question, never a finding.** The gap in the record is a gap, and
  where the record does not settle it, the uncertainty resolves toward the candidate,
  per [uncertainty-resolves-toward-the-candidate](../../_laws.md#uncertainty-resolves-toward-the-candidate).
  The kit may say "unexplained fourteen-month gap"; it may not say "likely dismissed".
- **State the observation, then ask.** "There's a period between these two roles I
  couldn't account for from the record — what were you doing then?" The candidate should
  be able to hear exactly what was seen and answer that, rather than guess at the
  concern behind a vague question.
- **Where the kit's own framing rests on a guess, label the guess.** A prep note that
  reads like an observation but is actually an inference about the person must look like
  an inference, per
  [inference-must-look-like-inference](../../_laws.md#inference-must-look-like-inference).
  Interviewers walk into the room carrying the prep pack's framing, and an unlabelled
  guess becomes a premise.

## The answer scaffold

Each question travels with a small structure the interviewer uses in the moment. The
most portable shape is the four-part narrative the structured-interviewing tradition
already trains people to expect — the situation, the task the person owned, the actions
they personally took, and the result with a number on it — written out *for this
question, from this candidate's record*, so the scaffold names the actual project the
answer should be about rather than gesturing at a template. An interviewer holding a
filled-in scaffold knows within thirty seconds whether the answer is going to close the
gap; one holding four generic headings does not.

On top of the shape, three fields:

- **What a good answer contains** — the elements that would actually close the gap.
- **What to listen for** — the observable behaviours that separate a strong reading from
  a weak one, phrased in the same terms as the rubric's anchors. If the scaffold's
  listen-fors and the scorecard's anchors describe different behaviours, the plan and the
  instrument are measuring different things.
- **The follow-up** — the narrowing question to use when the answer stays abstract, which
  is the default failure of a first answer.

Without the scaffold, a question is a prompt for a story, and what gets rated is the
story. The scaffold is also what makes a kit usable by an interviewer who did not write
it — which is most of them.

## Decision rules

- **One question per gap, and gaps ranked by how much the decision depends on them.**
  When the count has to be cut, cut the gaps whose resolution would not change the
  verdict.
- **A gap that no question closes is recorded as unassessed** rather than guessed at,
  per [absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence](../../_laws.md#absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence).
- **Personalised questions ride only the personal phases.** Gap-anchored questions are by
  construction candidate-specific, and candidate-specific material on a phase whose
  ratings are compared across a cohort destroys the comparison. Round design owns that
  rule; this technique must respect it, which in practice means the kit populates the
  personal phases and the shared phases carry identical material for everyone.
- **The kit travels with a signals checklist** — the handful of cross-cutting things
  worth watching for regardless of which question is running — kept short enough to be
  glanced at, because a checklist that requires reading during a conversation is not
  used during a conversation.

## When not to use this

- **Shared-stimulus phases** in a comparison round, per the rule above.
- **Records too thin to yield gaps.** An early-career record does not have enough in it
  to generate meaningful gap-anchored questions, and forcing the method produces
  interrogation of a two-line résumé. Those rounds are built on shared work material and
  designed problems instead.
- **When the "gap" is a protected characteristic or its proxy.** Absences and
  discontinuities that correlate with caregiving, health or military service are not
  question fodder; the defence question is for things the candidate chose, not things
  that happened to them.
