# Disclose how far they got last time

A re-approach that does not name the prior interaction reads as amnesia or as
a mail-merge, and both are worse than not writing. The remedy is a disclosure:
in the first message, name the role, name roughly when, and name how far they
got. This is not a politeness wrapped around the pitch — it is the content
that makes the second conversation credible, because it is the only thing in
the message that a generic system could not have produced.

## What the disclosure contains

Four elements, in the first message, before the pitch:

1. **The role and roughly when.** Specific enough to be recognisable — the
   title they applied under and a season or month, not "a previous role".
2. **How far they got**, in the vocabulary they experienced: they spoke with a
   hiring manager, they completed a work sample, they reached a final panel,
   they received an offer.
3. **How it ended, honestly and without re-litigating.** "We went with another
   candidate" if that is what happened. "The role was put on hold before we
   could come back to you" if that is what happened — and that second sentence
   is owed rather than optional, because people abandoned mid-process usually
   never learned why.
4. **What is different now.** The new role, the changed team, the changed
   scope. Without this the message is a memory, not an approach.

What follows is the ordinary pitch, and it should be short, because the
disclosure has already done the work of earning the read.

## Say only what the record holds

Every clause of the disclosure must be derived from stored facts about this
person, never from a template's optimistic default
([say-only-what-the-record-holds](../../../_laws.md#say-only-what-the-record-holds)).
The failure mode is specific and common: a template that says "you were a
strong finalist" fires for someone who was screened out at document stage,
and the person now knows both that you do not remember them and that you are
willing to say flattering things you cannot support.

Three prohibitions follow, and they are absolute:

- **No comparative claims you did not record.** "You were our second choice"
  is sayable only if a record says so. In most systems nothing does, because
  ranking positions are computed, transient, and were never a considered
  judgment about that person.
- **No claims about other candidates.** Why someone else was chosen is that
  person's information, not this one's, and "the person we hired had more
  platform experience" is a comparative disclosure about a third party.
- **No invented warmth.** "We were so impressed" requires an assessment that
  says so. Where the record is thin, the honest disclosure is thin: "you
  reached our final round last autumn" is enough, and is believable precisely
  because it is unembellished.

If the record cannot support any of the four elements — because it aged out,
was scrubbed, or predates your outcome vocabulary — you do not have a
rediscovery approach. You have a cold approach to someone who may remember
being rejected, which is a different and worse message. Send the cold version
without the false familiarity, or do not send.

## Store the facts, render the sentence

Do not persist the disclosure as a written phrase. Store the structured facts —
the outcome kind, the stage reached, the role, the date — and rebuild the
sentence on every surface that shows it. A stored phrase is frozen in one
language, in one product's vocabulary, at the moment it was generated; it
cannot be re-rendered for a candidate who reads another language, cannot be
corrected when the outcome is corrected, and cannot be suppressed when the
underlying record is redacted. Systems that persisted the sentence discover
this when they add a second locale and find their candidate-facing history is
monolingual and unfixable.

The corollary governs records that predate your structured fields: they read
as *absent*, not as zero. A missing stage is not "reached the first stage"; a
missing outcome is not "rejected". Where the structured facts are absent, fall
back to whatever the record genuinely holds, or omit the disclosure — never
let a null render as a confident default.

## Internal labels are not the disclosure

The stored outcome is an operational value chosen for branching, not for a
human to read
([meaning-does-not-live-in-a-label](../../../_laws.md#meaning-does-not-live-in-a-label)).
"Declined after assessment", a stage code, a fit tier, a score: none of these
go in the message. Translate to what the person actually experienced, and drop
anything they were never told. A person who was rejected for a reason nobody
communicated to them does not learn it now, inside a recruiting email, as a
side effect of your transparency ambitions.

The same applies to the machinery. Do not explain that an automated sweep
surfaced them, that a model matched their profile, or that they scored above a
threshold. It is true, it is uninteresting to them, and it converts a personal
approach back into the mail-merge you were trying to escape.

## The prior verdict does not travel

A disclosure describes what happened; it must not imply the old verdict
governs the new role
([a-verdict-is-bound-to-what-it-judged](../../../_laws.md#a-verdict-is-bound-to-what-it-judged)).
"You weren't quite right for that one, but" is a sentence that carries a
judgment forward and invites the reader to argue with a decision they cannot
change. Name the fact, name what is different, and let the new process be new.
This also protects you: a person who accepts a second approach on the basis
that the old assessment has been set aside, then finds it weighting their
ranking, has been misled.

## Decision rules

- Disclose in the first message, never after they reply. Disclosure held back
  as a reveal is a negotiating tactic applied to a person who owes you nothing.
- Derive every element from stored facts; render nothing from a default.
- Where the record is silent on an element, omit the element rather than
  soften it.
- Never state or imply a ranking against other candidates.
- If the person was abandoned mid-process, say so and apologise for it, before
  anything else.
- If the disclosure cannot be made, send a cold approach or nothing.

## When not to use it

Do not disclose to a third party. If the approach reaches the person through
an intermediary — an agency, a referrer, a shared inbox — the prior-process
detail is not theirs to receive, and the disclosure waits until you are
speaking to the candidate directly.

Do not disclose where the prior interaction is itself sensitive: a process
that ended in a complaint, an accommodation request, a health-related
withdrawal. The existence of the file is not something to reopen unprompted in
a sales message, and the correct handling of those records is exclusion from
the sweep rather than a carefully-worded email.

And do not treat the disclosure as licence to keep going. It earns one honest
message. What happens after that — cadence, follow-ups, and when a silence
means stop — belongs to the outreach and halt-rules discipline.
