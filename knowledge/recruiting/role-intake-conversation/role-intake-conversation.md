# The role intake conversation

Intake is the conversation that turns "I need another one of those" into a
defensible statement of what the work is and what evidence would prove
someone can do it. It is the most upstream decision in a hire, and it is made
in a room where nothing is measured, nobody is scored, and the only
instrument is the questions asked.

Everything downstream inherits from it: the search query, the screening
rubric, the interview loop, the scorecard axes, the comparison of finalists,
the offer band, and — when a rejection is challenged — the account of what
the job actually required. An error here is not caught downstream, it is
*amplified* downstream, because every later stage treats the brief as given.
A requirement invented in minute four of a kickoff will be defended in a
debrief three months later by five people who have no idea it was invented.

The naive reading is that intake is transcription: the hiring manager knows
what they want, the recruiter writes it down, a job description results. That
fails on one observation — **the person who owns the need is the person least
practiced at articulating it.** They hire two or three times a year. They
think in people ("someone like the one who left"), in artifacts ("here's the
old description") and in solutions ("senior platform engineer, five years,
degree required") — almost never in outcomes and evidence. Transcription
faithfully records the least useful layer of what they know.

## Intake inverts the interview register

Recruiters are trained in a conversation whose register is *evaluative*: you
ask, they answer, you do not help them construct a better answer, and
steering them toward a response is a defect. Intake demands the opposite in
every one of those dimensions.

| | Candidate interview | Role intake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | measure what exists | construct what does not yet exist |
| Helping the respondent | contamination | the job |
| Supplying structure | leading | required, for most requestors |
| Silence | pressure, kept deliberately | dead air to fill with a reflection |
| A vague answer | a finding about the person | a finding about the question |
| Contradiction | a credibility signal | the richest vein in the session |

Running intake in the interview register produces a defensive manager and a
thin brief: they answer narrowly, you write down what they said, and both
parties leave believing the job is defined. Running an interview in the
intake register contaminates the evidence. Anyone who works both sides must
switch deliberately — this is the most common cause of a hollow kickoff among
otherwise strong interviewers.

**Say the non-judgment out loud.** "There's nothing to get right here — vague
is exactly what this session is for." Requestors carry evaluation anxiety,
because admitting they do not know what level they need feels like admitting
they cannot manage, and anxious requestors give short, safe, generic answers.
The disclosure gain from naming the non-judgment is largest exactly where the
session is hardest, and it costs one sentence.

The extreme case settles the argument: a meaningful share of requestors
**cannot compose the role at all.** They have a departure to backfill, a
budget line and a feeling. For them "what are you looking for?" is not
under-specified, it is unanswerable. The conversation must be able to *supply
the frame* — outcomes, the shape of the first ninety days, the difference
between a must and a preference — while sourcing every substantive claim from
them. Structure from the interviewer; content from the requestor. Blurring
that line in either direction is malpractice: supply content and you are
writing fiction, supply no structure and you are running a silence.

## Job-description-first hiring is the disease intake treats

The default path is: find the last description for a similar title, edit it,
publish. Three facts make it indefensible.

- **An old description encodes the last hire, not the next need.** It was
  written for a team of a different size and a gap that has since moved.
- **Requirements ratchet upward under copying and never downward.** Each
  editor adds; nobody has standing to delete someone else's line. Degrees are
  the cleanest published evidence: in an analysis of some 26 million
  postings, 67% of production-supervisor listings demanded a degree that only
  16% of the people already doing that job held. A 51-point gap is not a
  standard failing to be met; it is sediment, and a filter excluding a large
  pool for a reason nobody ever articulated.
- **A description is an advertisement, not a specification.** It is written to
  attract and to satisfy an approval chain, so it optimizes for neither the
  screening rubric nor the interview design — and the moment anyone
  downstream needs a gradeable criterion, they invent one privately.

Intake produces the specification the description is later *derived from*,
never the reverse. An existing description on the table is an input to be
interrogated, not a starting point to be edited — treat it exactly as a
premature solution and quarantine it.

## Outcomes before requirements — the ordering is load-bearing

The highest-leverage rule in the craft is an ordering rule: **ask what this
person must have accomplished by the end of their first ninety days before
asking anything about what they must have.**

Asked first, requirements anchor and never move. "Five years and a degree"
spoken in minute two becomes the frame every later answer is fitted into,
including the outcomes — which are then reverse-engineered to justify the
requirement. Asked second, requirements become derivable and arguable: once
the outcome is "the weekly close runs without manual reconciliation by day
90", it is a short honest step to what someone must have done before, and an
equally short step to noticing that a degree is not on the list.

The ninety-day question is also the best de-specification device available. A
requestor who cannot describe a first-quarter outcome does not have a role;
they have a workload complaint — a finding worth surfacing, and frequently
the moment a requisition is correctly withdrawn, rescoped or split.

- **Every requirement traces to an outcome or it is not a requirement.** Which
  ninety-day outcome becomes impossible without this? "None" means it is a
  preference wearing a stronger word.
- **Grade the requirement to the evidence that would satisfy it.** "Strong
  communicator" is not a requirement; "can take an ambiguous request from a
  clinical team and return a written spec they sign off on" is, because it
  names what would count as proof. Stop at the demonstration, not the
  quality.

## The must line, and why it needs a cap

"Must-have" is a word requestors spend freely because in the moment it costs
nothing. It costs everything later: each must-have is a hard filter that
multiplies against the others, and twelve of them describe a person who does
not exist at the offered band.

**A must-have must survive the disqualification test**: would you decline to
interview an otherwise outstanding candidate who lacked exactly this? "Well,
no, we'd probably still talk to them" means it is a nice-to-have and must
move — in the session, out loud, with the requestor's agreement.

**Cap the list at roughly six.** The number is not sacred; the cap is,
because past that count requestors stop discriminating and the list stops
being a decision. When a session generates more, do not silently trim: ask
the requestor to **rank the top three** and record the rest as preferences.
People who cannot classify can almost always order.

Sustained requirement inflation is its own subject with its own downstream
controls — market-fit checks before publishing, adverse-impact review of
proxy requirements. Intake owns the first line of defence: the requirement
never spoken cannot inflate anything.

## Turn discipline

The mechanics are not etiquette; they determine how much the requestor
recalls and how much survives into the record.

- **One question per turn.** Stacked questions get the last one answered and
  the first two lost — worse in writing, not better.
- **Reflect before you ask**, roughly two reflections per question. A
  confirmation-shaped paraphrase ("so, a senior engineer, right?") harvests
  agreement instead of information and is how a session goes hollow while
  *feeling* productive.
- **Reuse the requestor's exact words until they are unpacked.** Their word
  is the handle on a tacit construct; replace it with your synonym and the
  construct is gone.
- **Keep turns short** — a reflection plus one question, a few sentences.
- **Do not offer a menu until an open question has stalled**, and frame it as
  disposable: "neither is fine, that's a real answer." An undisposable
  contrast is a leading question with extra steps.
- **Name contradictions out loud, in the session.** Silent reconciliation
  afterwards is where the brief stops being the requestor's.
- **Ladder every hard requirement exactly once.** Zero is transcription;
  three times trains the requestor to stop offering requirements at all.
- **A skipped question is recorded as skipped.** Compensation especially:
  "we'll come back to it", unrecorded, becomes silence indistinguishable from
  "no constraint". Per
  [absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence](../_laws.md#absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence),
  an unreached topic needs a state of its own — never a default, never an
  empty string, never a neutral value.

## The close is a turn of its own

Failing to summarize at the end is one of the most frequently catalogued
interviewer mistakes in the elicitation literature and the cheapest to fix.
Every intake ends with a **structured read-back of everything captured plus
exactly one open correction invitation** — "what did I get wrong, or miss?"
One open invitation, not a field-by-field confirmation walk: the walk
produces assent, the invitation produces the thing you missed.

The read-back and the close are **two turns, not one.** Summarizing and
closing in the same breath makes the invitation decorative — the requestor is
handed a door already shut, and whatever they say next arrives after the
record was sealed. Read back, stop, wait. What comes next is a confirmation
(close) or a correction, and a correction is recorded in their stated words
before the close.

## When the requestor doubts the role should exist

An increasingly common opening is not a specification but a question: "do we
even need this role now that the tooling does half of it?" Treating it as an
objection to overcome puts the recruiter in the position of selling a hire,
which destroys the advisor stance; treating it as a blocker ends a session
with real content in it.

It is a **story opener**. Anchor it where the ordering rule already points:
the ninety-day outcomes — *what must get done, regardless of who or what does
it*. That is answerable whether or not a person ends up doing the work, and
the answer is the specification either way. Role-shape hypotheses may then be
offered — a narrower role, a split, a different level, no role — as
disposable options marked as the interviewer's proposals, never as advocacy.
An intake concluding "this should not be a requisition", with reasons, is a
successful intake.

## What intake must never do

- **Decide.** It surfaces the seniority ambiguity, the budget conflict, the
  two-roles-in-one-requisition problem; it does not resolve them by recruiter
  fiat between sessions. Per
  [every-decision-names-its-actor](../_laws.md#every-decision-names-its-actor),
  a requirement's owner is whoever said it, and one the interviewer supplied
  is a *proposal* until the requestor adopts it in their own words.
- **Launder inference into record.** An inferred level or skill is rendered
  as an inference and put back to the requestor, per
  [inference-must-look-like-inference](../_laws.md#inference-must-look-like-inference).
  A brief where the interviewer's reasoning is indistinguishable from the
  manager's statements cannot do the one job it must later do: explain, under
  challenge, where a requirement came from.
- **Accept a label as a construct.** A title, a degree, a tool name and a
  years figure are labels; the requirement lives underneath, which is the
  whole justification for laddering
  ([meaning-does-not-live-in-a-label](../_laws.md#meaning-does-not-live-in-a-label)).
- **Coerce the requestor's vocabulary into its own.** Where an answer does not
  fit the intake's closed vocabulary — a public-sector pay grade offered where
  the tool knows four seniority levels — keep their term verbatim and leave
  the typed field unset. Force-mapping produces a brief wrong in a way nobody
  can see.

## The seam: the conversation and the brief are two crafts

This subject owns the **conversation** — how the session is opened, ordered,
budgeted, repaired and closed. The **artifact** is a neighbouring practice:
how requirements are graded, how each field records which turn it came from
and who asserted it, how a second session merges into the first rather than
overwriting it, how the brief is versioned once sourcing has begun.

One rule sits on the seam and belongs to both: **a named dealbreaker is
captured the moment it is spoken**, as its own graded requirement, not held
in narrative to be sorted out at the read-back. Sessions that defer the
sorting produce a rich transcript and an empty requirement list — the
requestor reads the brief, sees none of what they said was non-negotiable,
and stops trusting the artifact. Laddering may later demote such a
requirement; it never justifies not having recorded it.

Keep the two crafts separate in tooling as well as in thought, because they
fail differently: a good conversation captured into a structureless artifact
loses its provenance within a week, and a beautifully typed artifact fed by a
leading conversation is a well-formatted invention. Downstream, the brief is
consumed by advertisement writing (which must not re-inflate what intake
deflated) and by interview design (which must trace each scorecard axis back
to an intake outcome).

## Failure modes this standard exists to prevent

- **The hollow kickoff** — warm rapport, full agreement, and a brief
  containing nothing that could not be guessed from the title.
- **Specification by inheritance** — the old description edited into the new.
- **The anchored requirement** — asked before outcomes, never revisited,
  quietly setting the market the search will fail in.
- **The solution detour** — twenty minutes on a tool, a former employee or a
  title debate, ending with no statement of what the work is.
- **Silent reconciliation** — the recruiter noticing the budget and the
  seniority cannot both be true, and fixing it alone afterwards.
- **The interrogation** — laddering everything, repeatedly, until the
  requestor defends rather than explores.
- **Uniform pacing** — one fifteen-question script for the requestor who
  needed four questions and the one who needed forty.
- **The decorative correction invitation** — a read-back that asks "what did
  I miss?" and closes the session in the same turn.
- **The narrative-only capture** — dealbreakers stated plainly and left as
  prose, so the brief the requestor inspects contains none of them.
