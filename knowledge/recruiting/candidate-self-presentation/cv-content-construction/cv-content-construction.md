# CV content construction

A CV is an argument for one next step - a conversation - made to two readers at once: a
person skimming a pile, and a search index that person queries. Everything the rest of
this bundle says about *reading* a career document is the environment this subject writes
into. The reader discounts a bare assertion, weighs a claim by the evidence behind it,
treats an unexplained gap as a question and an implausible claim as a flag. A writer who
knows that writes differently: not louder, but more checkable.

So the craft is not persuasion by volume. It is **making true claims legible**: choosing
which facts lead, giving each one the words and the shape a reader can verify in a glance,
and cutting what costs attention without buying credibility.

## The writer's toolkit has four tools, and addition is not one of them

Selection, ordering, phrasing and grouping. A CV author - human, template or model - may
decide which recorded facts appear, in what order, in what words and under which heading.
It may not add a fact the record does not hold: no metric, tool, title, scope, employer,
date or credential that the person did not supply.

This is the bundle's law [say only what the record
holds](../../_laws.md#say-only-what-the-record-holds) seen from the writing side, and it is
not a footnote to the craft - it is the craft's boundary. The temptation here is sharper
than anywhere on the reading side, because the author *wants* the invention: a stronger
number is in the candidate's interest right up to the reference check, the technical
interview or the first month in the job. Misrepresentation on CVs is common in every
survey that asks (vendor surveys, so read the levels loosely, but the direction is
consistent), and the academic work that separates fabrication, embellishment and omission
finds that fabricators go on to perform worse and deviate more. A tool that manufactures
embellishment is manufacturing the candidate's future liability.

What an honest tool does when the fact it wants is missing is the whole subject in
miniature: it **asks the owner**. A missing metric is not a zero to be filled and not a
blank to be papered over - [absence of evidence is not
evidence](../../_laws.md#absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence) - it is a question with a
named slot ("what changed because of this work, and how do you know?"). A suggested
wording that depends on an answer the owner has not given is a proposal, rendered as one,
never a line on the page; [inference must look like
inference](../../_laws.md#inference-must-look-like-inference).

## What the evidence actually supports

Most CV advice has no controlled evidence behind it, and a standard built on the popular
version inherits its myths. What survives scrutiny, as of 2026:

- **Specific, detailed, clearly structured content helps.** A field-tracked study of
  applicants coded for detail, clarity and structure found more interviews per
  application and faster placement (n=183, 2025, correlational, students). A randomized
  field experiment on an online labour market (n~481,000 new jobseekers, 2021 data) found
  that surface writing help - fewer errors, easier reading, *no generated content* -
  raised hiring by about 8% with no drop in employer satisfaction, and helped non-native
  writers most. That is the one causally measured "boost", and note what it was.
- **Signals the reader can check beat claims the reader must believe.** Randomized CV
  experiments with hiring managers find that credentials and demonstrated activities
  move selection; bare adjectives do not. Self-assessed ability correlates with measured
  performance at roughly r=.29 across twenty-two meta-analyses - which is why a
  self-rating carries little information.
- **Chronology is how readers reason.** Audit studies consistently find that a *current*
  long gap lowers callbacks (size varies by market and study; one large replication found
  no duration effect at all), and a preregistered field experiment (n~9,000, 2023)
  found that stating years-in-role instead of calendar dates raised callbacks by about 8%
  without gaps and about 15% with them. Presentation of time is causal; hiding time is
  not the same thing.
- **Cheap tailoring loses its value.** On one large platform, after a generative
  writing tool arrived, the correlation between a letter's alignment with the job ad and
  its callback fell by half, and employers shifted weight to work history (difference-in
  -differences, 2025). A model of the same effect predicts a less meritocratic market
  when writing costs nothing. Alignment still matters for search; it no longer
  *distinguishes*, and evidence does.
- **Unguarded machine rewrites invent.** A controlled evaluation (180 runs, 2026, small
  and synthetic) found unsupported claims in 97% of unguarded CV rewrites, 50% with prompt
  guardrails alone, and only a human checkpoint removed the invented identities. The
  dominant fabrication types were inflated roles, upgraded metrics, and
  skills borrowed from the job advertisement the rewrite was tailoring to.

What did **not** survive: the precise "seconds per CV" figures, "most CVs are rejected by
software before a human sees them" (a vendor sales claim with no method, traced to 2012),
"quantified CVs get N times the callbacks", the specific distrust percentages quoted for
functional CVs, and "one page, always" (a vendor simulation found reviewers preferred
two pages - but its two-page versions also carried nearly twice the words, so it measured
detail, not page count). Where this subject states a rule anyway, it states the reason
the rule holds without those numbers.

## Content is ordered by the reader's question, not by the writer's life

A reader asks, in order: *what is this person now, is it what I need, can I believe it,
and what else.* The document answers in that order. That fixes three things:

- **Reverse chronology is the scaffold, never optional.** Readers reason about careers as
  intervals, and a document that withholds the intervals is read as withholding
  something. Relevance changes *what is expanded*, not *what is shown*.
- **Space is spent by recency and relevance.** The most recent relevant role gets the
  most lines; a role from fifteen years ago gets one. Compress, never delete: a deleted
  role is a manufactured gap, and a gap costs more than a line.
- **A summary earns its place or is cut.** It earns it when the reader's first question
  is not answered by the most recent title - a career change, a return, a portfolio
  career, a senior generalist - and it is then three factual lines, not a paragraph of
  self-description.

## Tailoring is emphasis inside the record

Tailoring to a target role is legitimate and useful when it reorders and re-words toward
what the target needs *using what the record holds*. Where the target asks for something
the record does not show, a writing tool's job is to tell the owner - "this posting asks
for X; your CV does not show it; if you have it, where did you use it?" - not to write it
in. The owner's answer, recorded, becomes a fact; the tool's guess never does. Recruiters
really do search by skill and title terms, so using the employer's word for a thing the
candidate has done is honest and worth doing; repeating it to game a count is the
stuffing the reading side is built to flag.

## A career change is a story the chronology must still carry

The career-changer is the person a naive writing tool fails hardest, because their most
relevant evidence is recent and small and their longest experience is elsewhere. Hiring
research on moves between jobs finds that employers read related, incremental moves as
coherent and unrelated jumps as erratic - so the document's job is to make the *relation*
explicit: a target line that names the direction as sought (never as held), a summary
that bridges, the bridging evidence (projects, study, the parts of the old job that were
already the new job) promoted, and the full history kept in order. The functional CV,
which hides the history behind a skills list, is the format readers most distrust and the
one a career changer is most tempted by.

## Machine-written prose is a writing risk, not an authenticity finding

The reading side of this bundle is settled that a machine-drafted document is not a
finding against the candidate. The writing side has a different obligation: a rewrite
must not make the candidate less *distinguishable*. Generated prose converges - on the
same verbs, the same adjectives, the same summary shape - and a document that reads like
every other polished document has spent its one chance to be specific. Screening models
also measurably prefer text written by their own family, which makes a product that both
rewrites and scores CVs a party to a bias it must disclose and design around.

The durable differentiator is specifics: names of systems, sizes of teams, the actual
problem. A rewrite that replaces a specific noun with a general one to make a sentence
"read better" has made the CV worse.

## What the naive readings get wrong

- **"Boost" as intensification.** Stronger verbs, bigger adjectives, "responsible for"
  turned into "led". A verb that raises the claim - responsibility into ownership,
  participation into leadership - is an embellishment, and the owner must choose it.
- **Quantify everything.** Where the record holds a number, lead with it. Where it does
  not, a number is an invention, and scope, frequency, audience or a before/after the
  record states are the honest substitutes.
- **Every sentence a bullet.** Splitting a paragraph into sentence bullets is formatting,
  not construction; it produces six bullets of mixed weight instead of three of
  decreasing importance.
- **Skill bars as polish.** A self-rating drawn as a meter looks like measurement and
  carries the information of a guess. Evidence - where the skill was used - is the
  honest form.
- **Tailoring as vocabulary copy.** Lifting the posting's terms is how a rewrite borrows
  a skill the person never claimed.

## Neighbours

How a document is parsed, weighed, screened for manipulation and redacted belongs to the
candidate-evidence and fairness subjects of this bundle; this subject writes for those
readers and never re-states their rules. How the words are laid out on the page, what a
parser makes of the layout, and which personal data a market expects belong to the
sibling presentation-and-parseability subject. Whether authorship is a finding belongs
to authenticity screening (it is not); what a rewrite may change belongs here.

## What a principal practitioner holds true

- The writer's tools are selection, order, wording and grouping; addition belongs to the
  owner alone.
- A missing fact is a question for the owner, never a gap for the tool to fill.
- Chronology is the reader's scaffold: relevance decides what expands, not what is shown.
- Specifics outlast polish; a rewrite that generalises a noun made the CV worse.
- Tailoring is emphasis inside the record, and a requirement the record does not meet is
  reported to the owner, not written onto the page.
- Every change a tool makes is listed, attributable and reversible, because the person
  whose name is on the document answers for every line of it.
