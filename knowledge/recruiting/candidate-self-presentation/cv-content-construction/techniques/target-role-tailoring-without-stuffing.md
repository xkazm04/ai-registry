# Target-role tailoring without stuffing

Tailoring means the same career, told toward a different reader. It is worth doing for
two measured reasons and one unmeasured one. Recruiters search their systems by skill,
title and experience terms, so a thing the candidate has done but described in other
words may never be found. Readers reward relevance on the first screen. And, less
measurably, a document that speaks the target's language is simply easier to read.

It has also become cheap, and cheap signals stop discriminating: after a generative
writing tool reached one large labour platform, the link between how well an application
matched the posting's wording and whether it got a callback weakened by about half, and
employers moved their weight to work history. Tailoring now buys *findability* and
*first-screen relevance*. It does not buy credibility; only evidence does.

## The procedure

1. **Read the target into requirements.** From the posting - or from the union of many
   postings at the target title, counted by how often each requirement appears - produce
   a ranked list of what the role asks for. State the basis: one posting, twenty
   postings, or a generic lexicon standing in because no posting exists yet; [a claim
   carries its sample and its basis](../../../_laws.md#a-claim-carries-its-sample-and-its-basis),
   and "typical for this role" is a weaker claim than "asked for by 14 of 20 postings".
2. **Map each requirement to the record, three ways.** *Evidenced* - the record shows it
   used in a role, project or credential. *Listed only* - it appears in a skills list and
   nowhere else. *Absent* - the record does not mention it.
3. **Reorder toward the evidenced.** Inside each role, bullets that evidence a top
   requirement lead. Inside the skills section, target-relevant groups and items lead.
   The summary's most target-relevant fact leads. Nothing is removed by this step.
4. **Align vocabulary where the record already has the thing.** When the record says
   "customer data platform integration" and the target says "CDP", using the target's
   term - or both - is honest and findable. This is renaming a fact, not adding one.
5. **Report the rest to the owner, never to the page.** *Listed only* requirements get
   a question: "you list X; where did you use it?" *Absent* requirements get: "this role
   asks for X; your CV does not show it. If you have it, tell me where." The owner's
   answer becomes a recorded fact; nothing else does.

## Decision rules

- **When a requirement is absent from the record, it is absent from the CV.** No
  bridging phrase that implies it ("exposure to", "familiar with") unless the owner
  supplies the fact. Skills borrowed from the posting are the most common fabrication in
  machine-tailored CVs; [say only what the record
  holds](../../../_laws.md#say-only-what-the-record-holds).
- **When a term already appears, do not repeat it for count.** One natural use in the
  most relevant place, plus the skills section, is enough for a search index and reads
  normally to a person. Repetition, lists pasted from the posting, and text hidden from the
  human reader are what the reading side's stuffing and smuggling screens exist to catch -
  and hidden text is rare in practice and ignored or penalised by modern readers.
- **When emphasising a target term typographically, cap it.** Bolding every matched term
  turns the page into a keyword highlight and reads as a machine pass. At most one or two
  per role, and never inside the skills list where every item would qualify.
- **When the gap report shows the target is mostly absent, say so plainly.** A CV with
  one of ten requirements evidenced is not fixed by ordering, and telling the owner that -
  with the two or three things that would change it - is the most useful output the
  tailoring pass has.
- **When there is no real posting, label the stand-in.** A built-in lexicon for a role
  family is a guess about the market and must be shown as "typical for", never as "the
  market asks for".
- **Tailoring never touches identity, dates or the held titles.** A target title never
  replaces or decorates the title the person actually held; the direction goes in a
  labelled target line.

## When not to use this

When the owner is applying through a referral or to a reader who already knows them, a
heavily tailored document can read as generic in the other direction; a lightly ordered
general version is often better. And a target the record does not support at all is a
career-change problem, not a tailoring problem - use the career-change framing instead of
reordering a history that has nothing to reorder toward.
