# Career-change framing

A career changer's most relevant evidence is usually recent, small and partly unpaid; their
longest experience is in a field the reader is not hiring for. Written in the order they
lived it, the document shows the reader the wrong career first. Written to hide that
order, it shows the reader a candidate who is hiding something. The technique is the path
between the two.

What little research exists points one way: hiring research on job-to-job moves finds
that employers read a history of *related*, incremental moves as coherent and a history
of unrelated jumps as erratic, most strongly for complex roles. The career-change CV's job
is therefore to make the *relation* between the old work and the new target visible - not
to make the old work disappear. No study tests specific career-change formats; the
distrust of the functional CV is practitioner consensus with no traceable survey behind
its quoted percentages, and it is consistent with everything readers are known to do with
chronology.

## The structure: hybrid, never functional

1. **A target line under the name** that states the direction *as sought*: "Seeking:
   data analyst roles" or "Moving into data analysis". Never a title the person has not
   held written where a held title would go - a reader takes the line under the name as
   who the person is. The target line is where a generic "objective statement" was
   rightly abandoned and a specific one still earns its place.
2. **A three-line summary that bridges**: the direction, the evidence that already points
   there, and the transferable strength from the prior career, each stated as a fact
   from the record.
3. **A "relevant projects" or "relevant experience" section above the main history**
   when the bridging evidence is outside paid roles - a course with a delivered project,
   a side project, volunteering, the part of the old job that was already the new job.
   Dated like everything else.
4. **The full chronology, kept in order**, with prior-career roles compressed per the
   recency rules and their bullets reordered so the transferable work leads.

The functional layout - a skills list with the employment history reduced to a bare
list or removed - is the one to refuse, even when the owner asks for it: explain that
readers treat it as concealment, and offer the hybrid.

## Transferable framing without invention

Transferable framing names the part of old work that *is* the new work, in the new
field's vocabulary, where the record supports it. A QA engineer targeting machine-learning
evaluation did write test plans and measure failure rates; "designed regression test
suites for a payments system" can honestly lead a role and be described as evaluation
work. What the framing may not do is attribute the target's skills to the old role - a
QA engineer did not "build evaluation pipelines for models" unless they did.

**Decision rules:**

- **When a transferable claim needs the reader to accept an analogy, state the fact and
  let the analogy be the reader's.** The fact is checkable; the analogy is a proposal.
  A tool that drafts one shows it as a suggestion with its reasoning, never on the page
  unaccepted; [inference must look like
  inference](../../../_laws.md#inference-must-look-like-inference).
- **When bridging evidence exists outside employment, promote it and date it.** A project
  with a link, a course with a delivered artifact, a certification with its date - each is
  stronger than any adjective about aptitude.
- **When no bridging evidence exists, the honest document is a prior-career CV with a
  target line**, and the useful output is telling the owner which one or two pieces of
  evidence would change it. Do not write a bridge that is not there; [say only what the
  record holds](../../../_laws.md#say-only-what-the-record-holds).
- **When the change is a step down in level or pay, say nothing about it.** The
  document is not the place to explain motives; a short, factual cover note is.
- **When the prior career is long, compress it harder than a same-field CV would** - one
  or two bullets per old role, transferable ones first - but never below title, employer
  and dates.

## When not to use this

A move within a field (a promotion track, a change of industry for the same function) is
tailoring, not a career change; the standard chronological CV with reordered bullets is
right. A return to a field after a break is a chronology problem - show the break's
interval honestly and its content if the owner wants to - not a reframing problem.
