# Fit tiers with a secondary-skill cap

Every match between a real experience and a predicted question is a judgement, and the
judgement has to be written in a form that tells the candidate what to do. The form is a
closed scale of four tiers, and each tier is defined by the action it triggers, not by a
number it sits on.

| Tier | The match | The action |
|---|---|---|
| Strong fit | The competency the question tests is what the experience is *about*, and the experience is solid | Use it as it stands |
| Workable | The competency is present but not central, or the experience is central but modest in strength | Use it with a bridge: say which part to foreground |
| Stretch | The competency is absent from what happened and is reachable only by reframing | Use it only with explicit framing, and prefer anything better |
| Gap | No experience reaches the competency at any tier | Do not match; hand the question to the gap response |

## Why four tiers and not a score

A score invites arithmetic, and the arithmetic is fiction. A weighted sum of competency
match, strength, alignment and freshness needs weights that nobody has measured, and a
reader cannot tell a 7.4 that is mostly competency from a 7.4 that is mostly strength.
The tiers do the opposite: they name an action, so two matches in the same tier are
interchangeable for planning and two in different tiers are not. The scale also needs its
last state to be a true state. A gap is not a very weak stretch; it is the absence of a
match, and [absence of evidence is not
evidence](../../../_laws.md#absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence): coercing it to the lowest
rung hides it inside a table of matches, where it will be read as something to polish
rather than something to handle differently. Four is also the smallest number that keeps
those four actions apart; a fifth tier would not trigger a fifth action.

## The factors, in the order they decide

Factors are applied in order and each breaks ties the previous one left, so the order is
lexicographic rather than a blend.

1. **Competency match.** Primary beats secondary beats reframed. An experience whose centre
   is the tested competency wins over any experience that only contains it.
2. **Strength of the experience.** Stronger beats weaker within the same match type.
3. **Alignment with the employer and the role.** Domain, scale and context that the
   listener will recognise. Real, but third: familiarity is a reason to pick between
   comparable matches, never a reason to pick a worse match.
4. **Freshness.** Whether the listener, or listeners the candidate is competing alongside,
   have already heard it; see the technique that owns it.
5. **Variety.** A property of the whole set, applied during allocation, not here. Applying
   it per question penalises a good match for being good twice.

## The cap, and where the cap stops

A match reached through a secondary competency is **workable at best, never strong**. The
reason is the probe, not the story. A question is a doorway to follow-ups, and a follow-up
digs into the tested competency. An experience whose centre is something else runs out of
material on that competency at the second or third probe, even if the story is excellent
overall. The cap says: the first answer may hold, the probe will not, so treat the match as
needing a bridge.

That makes the interaction with strength precise. A strong experience matched through a
secondary competency and a modest experience matched through the primary one can both land
in the workable tier, and within the tier strength decides: the strong secondary may lead.
It cannot reach the strong-fit tier, and a strong-fit match (primary competency, solid
strength) beats it outright. Strength never lifts a match across a tier boundary; it only
orders matches inside one.

The cap itself has an edge on the other side, where a secondary match becomes a stretch.
The test is whether the candidate can point to a **concrete action in the experience that
is the competency**, not a lesson drawn from it afterwards. If the person did the thing
inside the story, even in passing, the competency is secondary and the match is workable.
If the link exists only because the candidate can tell an analogy, it is a stretch. A
candidate who says "it wasn't really about influence, but I did get the platform team to
reorder their quarter" has a secondary match; one who says "it was a lot like influencing
someone" has a stretch.

## How the grade is made honest

- **Write the reason on the line.** Each tier carries one sentence naming the deciding
  factor, so a later reader can tell a downgrade for strength from a downgrade for
  competency. An unexplained grade is an unreviewable one.
- **The grade is the candidate's inference, and must read as one.** A strong-fit label on a
  match never checked in rehearsal is a prediction about a listener, not a result; the
  candidate treats it as untested until the answer has been spoken aloud, and a tool shows
  it as an estimate; [inference must look like
  inference](../../../_laws.md#inference-must-look-like-inference).
- **Strength is a self-rating with criteria.** Write down what the grade rests on (a stated
  action, a decision with a cost, a result that can be said plainly). A strength grade
  without criteria drifts upward with every rehearsal.
- **A workable or stretch tier produces its bridge at once.** The output of grading is
  the tier plus the bridging sentence: "foreground the cross-team coordination; it is
  secondary here but it is what the question is testing."

## When not to use this

When a question's competency is unknown (a purely open prompt), there is nothing to grade
against; choose by the experience the candidate most wants heard and grade it afterward
against whatever the follow-up reveals. When the candidate has fewer experiences than
questions, the tiers still apply but allocation, not grading, is the binding problem.
