# Gap response chosen by story strength

Some questions will have no experience behind them. The candidate's choice is not whether
to have a gap but how to behave inside one, and the usual answer is temperament: the
confident improvise, the anxious apologise. Both are the wrong selector. The response
should be chosen by the **strongest true evidence the candidate actually has**, and the
evidence comes in a small number of grades.

## The selector

| What the candidate has | The response |
|---|---|
| A usable experience | No gap handling. Use it. |
| A real but thin experience | Use it as a springboard: say briefly what happened, then move to the principle it taught and how it would apply here |
| An experience so thin that telling it would hurt | Do not tell it. Reframe honestly to what the candidate does bring |
| Adjacent experience, not the thing asked | Bridge: name the closest real situation, what was learned, and how it transfers |
| No experience at all | State the approach the candidate would take, built from principles they have really applied, plus what they would learn first |
| A competency the candidate already knows is a development area | A growth account: what is being done about it, naming only what has actually begun |

Read the table top down and take the first row that is true. The order is the order of
evidence, so the response never claims more than the record supports.

## Three rules that never bend

**Never fabricate.** A gap is the exact place where an invented story is most useful and
safest-looking, and the first follow-up finds the seam. The bundle's rule is [say only
what the record holds](../../../_laws.md#say-only-what-the-record-holds), and a
candidate has no more licence to break it than a tool has. This includes the soft forms:
a composite of two real events told as one, a conclusion drawn in hindsight and presented
as the reasoning at the time, an outcome rounded up.

**Never stop at "I haven't done that".** The sentence is honest and it is half an answer.
The listener asked a question to learn something about the candidate; the honest "no" is
followed by the nearest true thing the candidate can offer. A gap that ends in silence
gives the interviewer nothing to score except the absence.

**Never cover a personal gap with "we".** A plural pronoun used to lend a team's work to
the speaker is an attribution the record does not hold. It is also the first thing a
follow-up undoes ("what was your part?"). Say what the team did and what the speaker did,
in separate sentences. The same care runs the other way: crediting a team for work that
was one's own is not modesty; it is a different misstatement.

## Making a hypothetical look like one

A stated approach is useful and risky. It is useful because it shows reasoning; it is
risky because the same sentences, delivered fluently, sound like experience. Mark the
mood: "I haven't done this, so here is how I would start" and not "what I do is". [Inference
must look like inference](../../../_laws.md#inference-must-look-like-inference) applies to
a candidate's speech as it does to a model's output: a plan the person has not executed
must not be phrased in the grammar of a result. The approach is built from principles the
candidate has applied elsewhere, and says so; and it ends with what they would learn
first, which is the part of an answer that shows judgment about their own limits.

## Choosing between thin and very thin

The boundary between a springboard and a reframe is whether telling the experience helps
or hurts. A thin experience has real content but cannot carry the answer; as a
springboard it earns a sentence and then yields to the principle. A very thin one, told in
full, invites a probe it cannot survive and costs credibility on everything near it. The
test is a follow-up: if the candidate cannot answer "what was the hardest decision, and
what did it cost?" from the experience, it is too thin to tell. Because the grade of
strength is the candidate's own, the test is made out loud, not decided silently.

## A growth account is made of things that happened

The growth response is the one most prone to decoration. "I am working on delegating" is
a sentence about intention. A growth account names what has actually begun: a thing
tried, a result, a next step. If nothing has begun, the honest state is that the
candidate has identified the area and has not yet acted, and the response is to say that
in one sentence and offer the approach, not to supply activity that did not occur.
[Absence of evidence is not
evidence](../../../_laws.md#absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence): a development area with
no action behind it is shown as exactly that.

## Do not over-explain

An apology for the gap, or a long account of why it exists, spends the time the answer
needed and reads as defensiveness. One clear sentence naming the gap, then the response.
The candidate may add one line of context only when the gap has a plain cause the listener
will otherwise guess wrong, and only if it is true.

## When not to use this

When a usable experience exists, use it; this technique is for shortage, not for avoiding
a story the candidate finds uncomfortable. A candidate who routes to the gap response for
a competency they do have experience of is avoiding, and the avoidance is a different
problem handled elsewhere in this subject. And when the gap is structural (a required
credential the candidate lacks), no answer script fixes it; the right move is to say so
plainly and let the employer decide.
