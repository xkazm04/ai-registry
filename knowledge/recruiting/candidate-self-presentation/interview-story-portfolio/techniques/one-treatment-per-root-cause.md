# One treatment per root cause

A candidate who avoids conflict in every story will score low on substance (the hard part
is missing), low on distinctiveness (the insight lives in the hard part) and perhaps low
on credibility (the account sounds too smooth). Scored dimension by dimension, that is
three weaknesses, three drills and three sources of discouragement. They are one habit.
The technique is to notice when several weak dimensions share a cause, track the cause as
a single item, and treat it once.

## When a cause graduates from an observation

A per-answer observation ("this answer skipped the disagreement") becomes a tracked cause
when the same underlying habit appears in **two or more sessions**, or across two or more
answers within a session, and it plausibly explains weakness in more than one dimension.
The threshold is a floor on evidence, not a measurement: it exists to stop a single bad
afternoon becoming a diagnosis. On a handful of sessions it may still be coincidence,
so the entry is created as a hypothesis, not a finding.

## What makes this a candidate-side rule

The general idea, treat the cause not the symptoms, is common coaching sense and needs no
subject of its own. What changes the rule in this setting is the evidence base. The
candidate has a few sessions, rated by a small rubric, often by one rater, and the
rater's own habits are among the things producing the pattern. So three adjustments:

- **The cause is a hypothesis, labelled as one.** A shared cause inferred from two
  sessions is an inference and renders as one: [inference must look like
  inference](../../../_laws.md#inference-must-look-like-inference). It is phrased as "this
  looks like", with the evidence listed, and the candidate is asked whether it matches
  their experience. A cause the candidate cannot recognise is likely the rater's artefact.
- **The treatment is also the test.** One intervention aimed at the cause is run, and the
  prediction is stated before it begins: if the hypothesis is right, all the affected
  dimensions should move together. If one moves and the others do not, the cause is
  partly right or wrongly specified, and the entry is revised, not declared a success.
- **Resolution is practice-level only.** A cause is resolved when the affected dimensions
  show sustained improvement over several sessions on the same rubric version and the
  pattern stops appearing in new answers. That claims the habit changed in practice. It
  says nothing yet about real interviews, which the calibration technique forbids
  claiming on a small sample; [a claim carries its sample and its
  basis](../../../_laws.md#a-claim-carries-its-sample-and-its-basis) applies to a resolved
  cause as it does to a score.

## The procedure

1. Record each observation with the session, the answer, and the dimensions it touched.
2. When the habit recurs, open one entry: the cause in plain words, the dimensions
   affected, first sighting, the evidence list.
3. Choose **one** intervention aimed at the cause itself. For avoidance of conflict:
   drills that mine each story for the real disagreement, not separate drills on substance
   and distinctiveness.
4. State what movement would confirm it before running it.
5. Review each session: are all affected dimensions improving together? Record the
   result, including a partial one.
6. Close the entry when movement is sustained and the pattern is gone; write down what
   worked.

## Avoidance is a cause, and a delicate one

Sometimes the cause is that the candidate steers around a topic: they skip the same
competency, choose the safe drill, shorten answers where it is uncomfortable. Naming
this to the candidate is useful once the pattern has recurred, and the framing matters. It
is offered as an observation with its evidence, a question about what makes the topic
hard, and a concrete next exercise, never as a character verdict. A hard observation must
come with its fix in the same breath; a critique with no change attached is a comment,
not coaching. It is also worth separating avoidance from a style difference: a candidate
whose natural way of building to a conclusion scores low on structure may be showing a
cultural convention, not a skill deficit, and a drill that tries to remove it is the wrong
treatment.

## Why one treatment and not several

Parallel drills split the candidate's limited practice time across symptoms, produce
partial improvement in each that is hard to attribute, and let a stubborn cause persist
behind a rising score on easy dimensions. One treatment concentrates effort, gives a clean
prediction, and gives a clean failure: if the single intervention does not move the
dimensions, the cause was misdiagnosed and the next hypothesis starts from evidence.

## When not to use this

When only one dimension is weak, treat that dimension; there is no shared cause to find.
When the dimensions are weak for independent reasons (thin experiences in one area, poor
pacing in another), a single cause would be a fiction invented to tidy the list. And when
the candidate does not recognise the pattern after being shown the evidence, treat the
entry as unconfirmed and keep observing rather than treating on the coach's say-so.
