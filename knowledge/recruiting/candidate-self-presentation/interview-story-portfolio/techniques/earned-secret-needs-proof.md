# Earned secret needs proof

Two qualified candidates answer the same question competently and the interviewer cannot
tell them apart. The cure is not a better phrase; it is content only one of them has. The
useful name for that content is an **earned insight**: something the candidate learned
from doing the work that is not what a thoughtful outsider would say. The technique is the
test an insight must pass to be offered as one, because the cheap version of this advice
produces the opposite of distinction: confident generalities in the voice of experience.

## The test

An insight qualifies only if all four hold:

1. **It is non-obvious.** A smart person who had not done the work would not have said it
   unprompted. "Communication matters" fails; "the decision stalled until we wrote down
   who could overrule whom" might pass.
2. **It was learned from direct experience.** The candidate was there, and the learning
   changed something they did. Wisdom absorbed from a book or a talk is borrowed, and a
   listener can usually tell.
3. **It is defensible if challenged.** The candidate can say what the counter-case is and
   why it did not apply, or where the insight stops holding. An insight that cannot
   survive a polite "is that always true?" was a slogan.
4. **It is backed by a specific story, number or artefact.** Something the interviewer
   could in principle ask to see. This is the part most often skipped, and it is the
   difference between an insight and an opinion with a story-shaped decoration.

Failing any one is not a small defect. An insight with no proof is an assertion, and in
this bundle's terms a claim without a basis: [a claim carries its sample and its
basis](../../../_laws.md#a-claim-carries-its-sample-and-its-basis). It may still be a
perfectly good opinion; it is simply not an earned insight, and the candidate should not
say it with the confidence the label carries.

## What does not qualify

Generic advice, borrowed wisdom, obvious observations, and predictions about the future
with no evidence behind them. A fifth category deserves its own line because it is the
tool-made one: **plausible-sounding insights that a coach or a model suggested and the
candidate adopted.** The test for those is the third and fourth property: if the
candidate cannot tell the story the insight rests on without prompting, it was not
learned, it was supplied. [Say only what the record
holds](../../../_laws.md#say-only-what-the-record-holds): a suggested insight is a
proposal, held back until the owner confirms there is a real event behind it.

## Extraction prompts, and why they are not the technique

Prompts help a candidate find candidates for insights: what did you believe before that
turned out to be wrong; what would surprise people who have not done this; what do most
people in your field get wrong about it; what counterintuitive thing did you learn; what
would you tell your earlier self. They are good questions and they reliably produce
output, which is exactly their weakness. A prompt that always yields an answer does not
distinguish a real insight from a manufactured one. The technique is the four-property
test applied to whatever the prompts return; a candidate who asks the five questions and
keeps everything has produced a list of opinions.

Two further cautions on extraction. Hindsight rewrites: the lesson named now was often
learned later, and presenting it as the reasoning at the time is a misstatement of the
record. And the question "what do most people get wrong" invites contrarian framing, which
is a different thing; see below.

## Using an insight

Write it as a short unit: the insight in a sentence or two, the proof, and the kinds of
question it answers. Weave it into the experience it came from rather than keeping it as
a separate layer; an insight detached from its story loses its proof.

In allocation, an insight is a **tie-breaker inside a tier**. Between two experiences at
the same fit and similar strength, lead with the one carrying the stronger proven insight.
It is not a tier lift. Treating a strong insight as moving a workable match up to a
strong one lets distinctiveness override fit, and an interviewer who asked about one
competency and received a memorable answer about another has not been answered. If
practice outcomes later show that distinctive answers do advance this particular
candidate, that is evidence worth recording, subject to the sample limits in the
calibration technique, not a reason to retune the tier scale on three data points.

## The line this subject does not cross

A distinctive point of view is sometimes taught as a stance some listeners would disagree
with, chosen to be memorable. This subject does not adopt that. A stance chosen for its
disagreeability is a position the candidate does not hold, and it is a small
fabrication: the listener will probe it, and the candidate will find themselves defending
something they never believed. Where a candidate truly holds a minority view, backed by
what happened to them, the four-property test admits it and it is offered with its proof.
Where they do not, the correct amount of contrarianism is none, and a competent plain
answer beats a manufactured provocation.

## When not to use this

When a question is purely factual (a tool used, a date, a scope), an insight is
padding. In a short screening call, one clear experience is worth more than an insight
that needs a minute to deliver. And a candidate with a thin inventory should repair
inventory before polishing distinctiveness: an insight attached to an experience the
listener found thin does not rescue it.
