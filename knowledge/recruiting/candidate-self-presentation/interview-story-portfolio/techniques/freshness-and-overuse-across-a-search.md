# Freshness and overuse across a search

Allocating experiences inside one interview is only half of the budget. A job search is a
series of audiences, and the audiences are not independent: the rounds at one employer are
usually the same panel's colleagues, interviewers write up what they heard, and
candidates in one profession, one city or one referral network sit in front of overlapping
people. Spend an experience in round one and it is partly spent for round two. The
technique counts use across the search, not across the conversation.

## The two effects

**Within one employer.** An experience already told in an earlier round is known to the
people who will decide, since feedback from rounds is typically shared. Told again, it
signals a thin inventory, unless the new interviewer asks for more depth on it, which is
a different event: that is a request, and answering it is not repetition. So:

- an experience used in an earlier round at the same employer drops one tier in the fit
  scale for the later round, **unless the interviewer asks to go deeper on it**;
- the drop is visible in the plan, with the reason, like any other downgrade;
- if the later interviewer will clearly not have seen the earlier write-up (a separate
  business unit, a different hiring manager), say so and keep the tier.

**Across the whole search.** An experience told many times has two risks. It decays in the
telling: a story rehearsed to its smoothest version loses the specifics that made it
credible, and a listener who has not heard it can hear the polish. And it may have been
heard, by someone the candidate does not know is connected to a later audience. Neither
risk is a reason to avoid a strong story; both are reasons to keep alternatives warm.

## State the shape, not a number

The usual formulation counts uses and warns at a threshold: a gentle warning at a few
uses, a stronger one at more. The thresholds are the weakest part of it. No measurement
backs three or five, and the right point depends on facts the number cannot see: how many
alternatives the candidate has, how strong they are, how overlapping the audiences are,
and how long ago the earlier uses were. [A claim carries its sample and its
basis](../../../_laws.md#a-claim-carries-its-sample-and-its-basis); a number with no
sample behind it is a decoration. So the standard states the shape and leaves the points
to the candidate:

1. **Record each use** with the date, the audience (employer and round), and whether the
   interviewer probed it.
2. **Escalate with count**: no flag at the first use, a prompt to look at alternatives
   once an experience is the default choice for several questions, a stronger prompt when
   it has been told so often that the candidate recites it.
3. **Set the points where rotation becomes cheap**: the more good alternatives exist, the
   earlier the flag fires. A candidate with two strong experiences should not be told to
   rotate them away.
4. **Treat the flag as an advisory.** It asks the candidate to check for an alternative of
   comparable fit, and it ends when the answer is "there is none, and I will tell it from
   a different angle".

A tool that must pick numbers may; it says they are defaults, and lets them move.

## Unknown use is not zero use

A candidate who has not kept a record has no basis for "fresh". The default for an
experience with no use history is *unknown*, and the plan shows it as unknown rather than
silently scoring it as unused. [Absence of evidence is not
evidence](../../../_laws.md#absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence): a missing history that
reads as freshness lets the most-worn story look newest. If the use history is
unrecoverable, assume reuse at the same employer and ask the candidate; that guess errs
toward a cheaper mistake (preparing an alternative) rather than a costlier one (repeating
a told story).

## Proven performers, and why they do not cancel the rule

An experience that advanced the candidate in earlier rounds is evidence that it works, and
there is a case for keeping it. Both pulls are real, and the technique resolves them by
**audience**, not by experience. Reuse the proven performer for an audience that has not
heard it; rotate it for one that has. The record of what it did is a reason to put it
first in a fresh audience, not an exception to the same-employer drop.

## What the technique does not do

It does not create experiences. When freshness removes the strongest option and nothing
comparable remains, the result is a downgraded fit or a gap, handled by the other
techniques, not a quiet invention to fill the hole. It also does not apply to a request to
elaborate. "Tell me more about that project" is the interviewer asking for the same
experience, and answering it in depth is the correct and expected behaviour.

## When not to use this

A single short process with a single interviewer needs no search-wide record; the
within-interview constraint from the allocation technique is enough. A candidate who has
only ever told an experience in practice sessions, never to a real audience, has not used
it, whatever the rehearsal count says.

## What the field evidence supports

Between-interview variation in presentation is normal: in a 448-interview study, applicants
varied their impression-management tactics from interview to interview, honest defensive
tactics most ([Roulin and Bourdage, 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00029)).
That study measured self-reported tactics and no outcomes, so it cannot say that a re-told
story costs anything; the cost described above remains reasoning. What it supports is the
practice of pinning the verifiable particulars of a reused experience (dates, role,
numbers) and letting only the emphasis vary by audience.
