# Interview story portfolio

A candidate will be asked questions they can only partly predict, and they own a finite
set of true experiences. Between those two facts sits a step that most preparation skips:
turning "I have experiences" into "I walk in with an allocation". Which experience answers
which kind of question, which one is held back because two questions want it, what is said
when none fits, what makes an answer sound like it came from this person and no other, and
whether the candidate's own practice scoring deserves any trust. This subject owns that
step and nothing on either side of it.

The word *portfolio* is chosen over *bank* or *library* on purpose. A library is a place
you look things up; a portfolio is held against a budget and allocated under constraints.
The set is small (a dozen strong experiences is a generous inventory for most careers), the
demand is uncertain, and the same item cannot be spent twice in one conversation without
the reader noticing. Every technique here is a way of making that allocation explicit
enough to be checked, because an allocation made inside the candidate's head the night
before is exactly the one that falls apart at the second question.

## Where this subject starts and stops

This subject starts after the candidate's written record exists. How a CV is written,
ordered or tailored belongs to the CV content subject, and how it is laid out or parsed
belongs to the presentation subject; here the document is a given and the experiences in it
are the raw stock. Nothing in this subject rewrites what the document says.

It stops short of the employer's side. How a hiring team designs a round, builds a question
kit from the gaps in a candidate's record, and anchors a rubric is owned by the assessment
subjects of this bundle, and this subject is their mirror image: the interviewer prepares
by asking what the record leaves open, and a candidate who prepares well is in large part
anticipating those same open places in their own record. A reader who needs the
interviewer's procedure should read that side; nothing in it is restated here, and nothing
in this subject assumes the other side is rigorous.

It also stops short of employer-side score calibration. A hiring system that scores
candidates and asks whether the score predicts anything has thousands of outcomes, a
labelled holdout and a statistical apparatus. The calibration technique in this subject is
the candidate-coach version of the same question, asked of a practice rubric with a sample
of a handful, no holdout and different confounds. It shares the vocabulary and none of the
machinery, and the reader should not borrow the employer-side thresholds for it.

Finally, the boundary every other boundary rests on: this subject never invents
experience. The bundle's law [say only what the record
holds](../../_laws.md#say-only-what-the-record-holds) applies to a candidate coaching
themselves, or being coached by a tool, as much as to a recruiter's system. A coach may
select, order, frame and rehearse the experiences a candidate has. It may not supply one,
upgrade one, or borrow another person's. The temptation is sharpest in exactly the places
this subject works: a gap with no story is where an invented story is most useful and
easiest to get away with, until the first follow-up.

## The unit: an experience with attributes

A portfolio entry is a true experience described by a few attributes, kept deliberately few
because every attribute is something the candidate must keep current:

- **The competencies it demonstrates**, split into the one or two it is *about* and any it
  merely *contains*. This split carries more weight than any other, and the first technique
  is built on it.
- **A strength grade**, which is the candidate's own judgement of how much a hostile
  listener could not take from it: specific actions, a decision with a cost, an outcome
  that can be stated. This is a self-rating, and self-ratings carry little information
  unless the criteria behind them are written down; a grade with no recorded reason is a
  mood.
- **An insight it earned**, if any: something the candidate learned that is not obvious and
  that they can prove.
- **A use history**: where, when and in front of whom it has been told. An unrecorded use
  is not a zero; it is unknown, and [absence of evidence is not
  evidence](../../_laws.md#absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence).

Notice what is absent: no polished script. A rehearsed script is a performance of an
experience, and the portfolio is the experience. Candidates who store scripts retrieve
scripts, answer the question they rehearsed instead of the one asked, and get found out at
the first probe.

## The order of operations

Preparation runs in a fixed order, and most of the failures this standard names come from
doing the steps in a different one.

1. **Grade each experience against each predicted question** on a closed scale, with the
   deciding factors in a stated order ([fit-tiers-with-a-secondary-skill-cap](./techniques/fit-tiers-with-a-secondary-skill-cap.md)).
2. **Allocate the whole set at once**, because the best experience for one question is
   usually the best for another, and picking question by question hands the strongest
   experience to whichever question was prepared first
   ([portfolio-assignment-before-per-question-pick](./techniques/portfolio-assignment-before-per-question-pick.md)).
3. **Adjust for use across the search**, not within one interview: a search is a series of
   audiences who may compare notes
   ([freshness-and-overuse-across-a-search](./techniques/freshness-and-overuse-across-a-search.md)).
4. **Decide what is said where nothing fits**, by the strength of the best available
   evidence rather than by temperament
   ([gap-response-chosen-by-story-strength](./techniques/gap-response-chosen-by-story-strength.md)).
5. **Make the answer distinguishable**, by attaching a proven insight where one exists and
   saying nothing distinctive where one does not
   ([earned-secret-needs-proof](./techniques/earned-secret-needs-proof.md)).
6. **Check the instrument**, by comparing practice scores to what actually happened, and
   refusing to claim more than the sample allows
   ([practice-score-needs-outcomes-before-it-is-trusted](./techniques/practice-score-needs-outcomes-before-it-is-trusted.md)).
7. **Treat recurring weakness as one cause**, not a list of unrelated defects
   ([one-treatment-per-root-cause](./techniques/one-treatment-per-root-cause.md)).

Steps 1 to 3 are allocation. Step 4 is honesty under shortage. Step 5 is differentiation
and is the step most likely to be abused. Steps 6 and 7 are about the candidate's own
instrument and the habits that bend it.

## Why the allocation is the hard part

The naive reading treats preparation as a lookup: for each likely question, find the best
story. Done once, for one question, that is correct. Done for a list, it is wrong in a
systematic way. The experiences that are best for the questions a candidate expects most
are a small set, and several questions will name the same one. Prepared one at a time,
the first question prepared gets the experience at full strength and the rest get whatever
is left, with no one noticing which were downgraded or why. In the room the effect is
visible to the interviewer in minutes: the same project, the same cast of characters, told
for a question about conflict and again for a question about ambition.

The corrective is not cleverness; it is visibility. Lay the whole matrix out, find the
collisions, and decide them deliberately and in writing, including which question loses
and what that question will do instead. A downgrade that is flagged can be rehearsed (the
losing question gets a framing that makes a workable experience read well). A downgrade
that is silent is simply a worse answer nobody chose.

The allocation is a plan, not a script. The questions are predictions; the interviewer will
ask something adjacent, or combine two. So each entry is held by what it demonstrates, not
by the single question it was assigned to, and every question keeps a backup. A candidate
who has memorised "question four gets experience six" is brittle in the exact way an
interviewer reading from a script is brittle.

## What to do when nothing fits

Some questions have no experience behind them. Candidates who have been taught a story for
everything respond to this in one of two bad ways: they stretch an unrelated experience
until it snaps, or they say they have not done that and stop. Both are failures of the same
duty. The honest response is selected by what evidence actually exists, from a usable
experience (use it) down through a thin one (use it as a springboard to the principle),
adjacent experience (bridge to it), no experience (state the approach and what would be
learned first) and a known development area (say what has really been started). Two
rules never bend: nothing is fabricated, and a plural pronoun is never used to cover a
singular gap. The interviewer asks what *you* did.

## What makes an answer distinctive

An answer is distinctive when it contains something only this person could say. The
standard is not a flourish of style; it is a claim, and a claim needs proof. A statement
that sounds like insight but is borrowed, obvious or unevidenced does not distinguish the
candidate; it makes the answer sound like the other polished ones, and it is a small
fabrication of expertise. Distinctiveness is also not the same as having a contrarian
position. This subject deliberately does not teach adopting a stance because some
listeners would disagree with it: a manufactured stance is a position the candidate does
not hold, and the first probe finds the seam. Where a candidate truly holds a minority
view with evidence, the earned-insight test admits it; where they do not, the right amount
of contrarianism is none.

## The candidate's own scoring is a hypothesis

Practice produces scores, and scores tempt. A candidate who scores themselves, or is
scored, on a rubric across many sessions will come to treat the number as a forecast. It
is a measurement of how an answer fares against that rubric, taken in a setting that lacks
the stakes, the stranger and the competition. Whether it forecasts real outcomes is an
empirical question whose answer arrives slowly and, for most candidates in one search,
never arrives in a usable quantity. The standard here is therefore less about how to
calibrate than about what to refuse: refuse to say a rubric is calibrated on a handful of
outcomes, refuse to rescale a rubric on one interviewer's remark, and report "cannot tell
yet" as a legitimate and common state. A coach that cannot say so is selling confidence.

## One concern a coach owes, and one it does not

A critique that ends with no change the candidate can make is a comment, not coaching:
whenever a weakness is named, the same breath names one concrete thing to do next, singular,
the highest-leverage fix and not a list. This is a rule about how feedback is delivered, not
a technique with its own procedure, so it lives here as a standing constraint on every
technique above. It matters most for the harsher forms of review (the strongest case
against an answer, the likeliest way it fails), which are useful only because they are
followed by a fix.

What a coach does not owe is certainty about the interviewer. Every technique here is a
claim about a listener the candidate has not met, built on a record of a few events. State
the claim, state what it rests on, and leave room for the room to disagree.

## What the naive readings get wrong

- **"Pick the best story for the question."** Correct for one question and wrong for a
  list, because the strongest experience is a shared resource.
- **"A story that fits the competency is a strong fit."** Fit has a primary competency and
  secondary ones, and a match through a secondary one is weaker however good the story is.
- **"Never repeat a story."** The rule is to repeat only when no alternative exists, say so,
  and tell it from a different angle; a blanket ban sends a candidate into a weak story to
  satisfy a rule.
- **"Always have an answer."** Having an honest response to a gap is the requirement; having
  a story is not.
- **"Be memorable."** Memorability earned by manufactured contrarianism is a risk the
  record cannot support. Memorability earned by a proven, specific insight is an asset.
- **"Our scores predict how interviews go."** Not until real outcomes exist in a quantity
  that separates signal from chance, and often not in one search.
- **"Fix each weak dimension with its own drill."** If one cause depresses several
  dimensions, per-dimension drills treat symptoms and look like effort.

## What a principal practitioner holds true

- The portfolio is an allocation under constraint; make the whole matrix visible and decide
  collisions on purpose.
- A competency a story is only incidentally about caps the fit at workable, however strong
  the story is.
- Use is counted across the whole search, not one conversation, and the state of "unknown
  use" is never "unused".
- Where no experience fits, the response is chosen by the strength of the evidence
  available, and the candidate never claims what the record does not hold.
- An insight earns its place with proof or is not offered as an insight.
- A practice score is a hypothesis about outcomes, and on a handful of outcomes the
  honest verdict is that it cannot be tested yet.
- A recurring weakness is one cause with one treatment, judged by whether all the affected
  dimensions move.
