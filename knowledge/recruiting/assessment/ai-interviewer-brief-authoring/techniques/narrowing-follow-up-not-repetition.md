# Narrow on a re-ask, never repeat

When an answer is thin, the follow-up asks something *smaller* inside the same
topic. It does not ask the same question again, and it does not ask a bigger one.
A verbatim re-ask is the single most damaging conversational move an interviewer
makes, and it is the one an untrained interviewer reaches for first.

## Why repetition is worse than moving on

A repeated question carries a message the interviewer did not intend to send: *you
failed that*. It is a verdict delivered by structure rather than words, which is
why it survives briefs that carefully ban verdicts in prose. The candidate now
knows they are behind, and interview anxiety depresses performance for the rest of
the conversation — most sharply for the least confident, which makes this a
fairness defect and not merely an awkward moment.

It is also uninformative. There are at least three reasons an answer came back
thin: the candidate lacks the experience, the candidate has it but did not
understand what was being asked, or the question was abstract enough that no
concrete answer suggested itself. Repeating the question distinguishes none of
them; it re-runs the same failed measurement. Reading the thin answer as absence
of skill, without ruling out the other two,
[reads absence of evidence as evidence](../../../_laws.md#absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence),
and where the reading is adverse
[the uncertainty resolves toward the candidate](../../../_laws.md#uncertainty-resolves-toward-the-candidate).

Narrowing distinguishes all three cheaply. If a smaller, concrete question
produces material, the problem was the question. If it produces nothing either,
that is a much stronger reading than one thin answer ever was.

## The narrowing ladder

Each rung is more concrete than the one above. On a thin answer, drop exactly one
rung — not to the bottom.

1. **Topic.** "How do you approach testing?"
2. **Instance.** "Think of the last thing you shipped that broke. What happened?"
3. **Act.** "What did *you* personally do when it broke?"
4. **Detail.** "How long did it take, and how did you know it was fixed?"

Rungs 3 and 4 are where a lived claim separates from a rehearsed one. Someone who
did the work has details and they are irregular; someone who has read about the
work has structure and no grit. That is why narrowing is both the fairer move and
the better probe — it is not a concession to nervous candidates, it is the
technique that produces the strongest evidence in the transcript.

## Rules for the brief

- **A re-ask is always a narrower question, never the same one, and never a
  broader one.** Broadening after a thin answer ("okay, tell me about your
  approach generally") is a rescue that produces generic prose.
- **The probe is neutral about content and directive about topic.** It may say
  *what to talk about*; it may never suggest what the answer is. Any follow-up
  that could be answered "yes, that" is leading and is a defect.
- **Verify claims with the same neutral instrument.** Where a candidate states a
  number, a scope, or an ownership claim, the narrowing question is asked of
  strong claims and weak ones alike — over what period, measured how, what was
  your own part. Asked selectively, verification becomes an accusation; asked
  uniformly, it is just the ladder. Unverified claims that reach the record must
  reach it as claims rather than facts
  ([inference must look like inference](../../../_laws.md#inference-must-look-like-inference)).
- **Stop on coverage, not on count.** The topic is done when there is enough
  material to score it, which may be one turn or four. A fixed follow-up count
  produces interviewers that pad an already-answered topic and abandon an
  unanswered one.
- **Never announce how many questions remain.** It converts the conversation into
  a countdown, invites the candidate to ration their answers, and — because
  coverage rather than count decides length — it is a number the interviewer
  should not be able to state truthfully anyway.
- **Ask the narrowing question plainly, with no preamble.** No "I understand,"
  no "that's helpful, but," no restating what was just heard. The follow-up
  starts with the question. This reads as the more direct interviewer, and it
  also happens to be the form that survives best on a conversational engine
  (rule-ordering-adjacency-and-form).
- **Two narrowings and no material is a result.** Move on. A third attempt is
  pursuit, and the candidate feels it as one. Record the topic as thinly covered
  rather than mining it.

## When not to use it

- **When the answer was complete.** Narrowing a good answer for the sake of the
  ritual wastes the coverage budget and reads as disbelief.
- **When the candidate misheard or misunderstood the words.** Restating the same
  question in plainer language is not a re-ask; it is a repair, and it should be
  framed as the interviewer's own imprecision rather than the candidate's miss.
- **When the topic is a screening fact, not a competency.** Notice-period,
  location, and eligibility questions have answers, not depths. The ladder does
  not apply, and running it there reads as interrogation.
- **When narrowing would require naming the answer.** If the only smaller question
  contains the solution, the item is badly designed. Change the item; do not lead.

## The failure this technique is written against

The recognisable bad transcript has the interviewer asking the same question
three ways, each slightly more pointed, while the candidate's answers get shorter.
By the fourth turn the conversation is about the candidate's failure to answer
rather than about their work, and every remaining competency is scored on a
person who is now rattled. Nothing in that transcript is recoverable, and the
score attached to it measures the interviewer.
