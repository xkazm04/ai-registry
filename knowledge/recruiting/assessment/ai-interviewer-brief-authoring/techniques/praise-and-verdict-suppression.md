# Suppress the verdict, and suppress the praise

The interviewer gives no score, no feedback, no decision, no prediction about the
outcome — **and no evaluative praise**. The two bans are usually written as one
and they are not one. The verdict ban fires once, at the end, and is easy. The
praise ban fires on every turn, is broken by ordinary politeness, and matters
more.

## The verdict half

An interviewer that says "you'd be a great fit", "that's exactly what we're
looking for", or even "I think this will go well" has issued an outcome it does
not own. Only a person advances, offers, or rejects
([no adverse outcome is solely automated](../../../_laws.md#no-adverse-outcome-is-solely-automated)),
and a decision that reaches the candidate before a decision-maker made it is a
decision with no attributable actor
([every decision names its actor](../../../_laws.md#every-decision-names-its-actor)).
The practical consequences are immediate: a candidate told they did well and then
rejected has been misled by the employer's own instrument, and the recruiter who
must deliver the rejection is now arguing with a promise nobody authorised.

The rule is unconditional and covers hedged forms. "I can't say, but…" is a
verdict. Reading back a positive impression is a verdict. Answering "how did I
do?" with anything other than a plain statement that the interviewer does not
evaluate and the team will be in touch is a verdict.

## The praise half, which is the subtle one

Evaluative praise — "great answer", "perfect", "that's a really strong example",
"exactly right" — is a scoreboard delivered in a friendly voice. It does three
things, all bad, and none of them look like a defect while the conversation is
happening:

1. **It teaches the register that earns approval.** Within two praised answers the
   candidate has learned what this interviewer rewards and begins performing to
   it. Every subsequent answer measures compliance rather than experience.
2. **It makes silence into criticism.** Once praise has been given twice, the
   absence of praise on the third answer is information, and the candidate reads
   it correctly. A brief that permits praise has, by omission, permitted negative
   feedback.
3. **It leaks the rating.** The strong-answer/weak-answer pattern of praise across
   a transcript is a legible score, visible to the candidate in real time and to
   anyone who reads the transcript later. It is the rubric escaping into the
   conversation, and it makes the eventual scorecard look like a formality.

The formulation that survives contact with a real brief is: **warmth is interest,
not approval.** The interviewer may be engaged, curious, appreciative of time,
and may acknowledge that an answer landed — "thanks, that helps", "understood",
"let's stay with that project" — because these express receipt, not quality. What
it may not do is grade.

Write this into the brief as **two enumerated lists, not as a principle**. A
general instruction to avoid evaluative language is not operationalizable: the
interviewer agrees with it and then says "that's the right instinct", which it
does not classify as a score. Name the forbidden tokens outright — the
superlatives, and specifically the ones that praise the candidate's *thinking,
instinct or approach* rather than the content, because those survive a praise ban
written only against adjectives. Name the permitted ones with equal specificity:
thanks, acknowledgement of receipt, an invitation to continue. The pair is what
makes "warm but neutral" land as a behaviour rather than as coldness or as
licence.

This ban is also what keeps the closing honest. A conversation that graded
nothing along the way can end without a summary of impressions, because there is
nothing outstanding to resolve. A conversation that praised freely cannot end
neutrally without the neutrality itself reading as bad news.

## Adjacent rules that belong with it

- **Never reveal that a probe, hint, or difficulty step is scripted.** An
  interviewer that explains its own mechanism ("I'm going to give you a hint now,
  this is part of the assessment") converts the interview into a conversation
  about the interview. The candidate starts answering the design.
- **Never imply the candidate is being tested on a trap.** Where an item has a
  deliberate wrong path, the interviewer must not signal that the path exists.
  This is not deception about the fact of assessment — the candidate knows they
  are being interviewed, and disclosure of that is a separate obligation owned
  elsewhere. It is refusing to disclose the *item's internals*, which is the same
  reason a test does not print its answer key.
- **Nothing in the conversation may be a claim about the candidate that the
  record does not hold**
  ([say only what the record holds](../../../_laws.md#say-only-what-the-record-holds)).
  "You clearly have deep experience here" is a conclusion drawn live, delivered to
  the subject, and unbacked at the moment it is said.

## When not to use it

- **A stated process explanation is not a verdict.** "The team reviews this and
  you'll hear from a recruiter" is required, not banned; a candidate is owed
  clarity about what happens next.
- **Basic courtesy is not praise.** Thanks, acknowledgement of a difficult
  question, apology for a technical fault — all permitted, all non-evaluative.
- **Interviews whose explicit purpose is coaching or practice** invert this
  technique entirely: there, feedback is the product. The failure to avoid is
  running a practice brief on a real hiring conversation, or the reverse, without
  the candidate knowing which one they are in.

## Checking compliance

Grep the transcript corpus for evaluative adjectives in interviewer turns, not for
the closing sentence — the closing sentence is nearly always clean because that is
the rule everyone wrote. The praise defect lives in turn three.
