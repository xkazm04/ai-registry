# Guarantee at least one hint

Somewhere in every conversation the interviewer offers a hint, a partial steer, or
a small piece of information the candidate did not have — at least once, whether
or not the candidate appears to need it. This is a mandatory affirmative rule, and
it is the counterweight that keeps a brief made of prohibitions from producing an
interrogation.

## Why it must be mandatory rather than conditional

The obvious design is "offer a hint when the candidate is stuck". It fails twice.

**It makes the hint a signal.** A hint that only appears when someone is
struggling tells them they are struggling — the same structural verdict a
verbatim re-ask delivers. Guaranteeing a hint for everyone removes its diagnostic
meaning to the candidate while preserving its meaning to the record.

**It leaves coachability unobserved for exactly the candidates who would score
best on it.** If a competency named "responds to input", "adjusts when
corrected", or "learns in the room" is on the rubric, it can only be scored from
a turn where input was actually offered. A strong candidate who never visibly
struggled produces a transcript with no such turn, and the axis is then either
scored on nothing or quietly imputed from general impression — which is
[absence of evidence rendering as evidence](../../../_laws.md#absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence).
A mandatory hint manufactures the observation for everyone, on purpose, at a cost
of one turn. The dynamic-assessment literature — the discipline that measures
learning from help given in the room — agrees on the mandatory half: in a review
of two dozen studies, the assessments whose help was *not* contingent on the
learner's response predicted better than those that helped only on struggle.

It does not support reading much into the observation. That literature scores
learning potential across several graded prompts — how many hints a person needed,
over several items — and even then its incremental validity is modest. One hint
yields one observation with three or four possible outcomes, confounded by how
good the hint was, how hard the item was and how close the candidate already
stood. That is a note, not a rating.

The hint must be delivered without announcing itself as an instrument (see
praise-and-verdict-suppression): it is offered the way a colleague offers a
useful fact, not the way an examiner offers a lifeline.

## The companion rules that make it work

A hint rule alone still leaves a candidate penalised for the things anxiety does
to speech. Three affirmative non-negotiables travel with it, and they should be
written in the brief as a small block rather than scattered:

- **"I don't know" is a good answer.** Say so in the brief, and have the
  interviewer treat it that way — acknowledge, move on, do not punish with a
  narrowing ladder. Honest ignorance is a positive datum about calibration, and a
  system that punishes it teaches candidates to bluff, which is strictly worse
  for the record.
- **Never penalise nerves.** Hesitation, restarts, filler, a long pause, a shaky
  voice — none is evidence about competence. Anxiety depresses observable
  interview behaviour while barely predicting how the person performs in the job,
  so scoring it imports variance that has nothing to do with the job.
- **Never penalise imperfect language.** Where the conversation happens in a
  language that is not the candidate's first, fluency is only a competency if the
  role actually requires it at that level. Grammar, accent, and vocabulary
  reaches are not signal about the skill being assessed; treating them as signal
  is how a language filter smuggles itself into a technical rubric. Where it is
  unclear whether a stumble was competence or expression,
  [the uncertainty resolves toward the candidate](../../../_laws.md#uncertainty-resolves-toward-the-candidate).

## Decision rules

- **One hint minimum, delivered before the conversation's final third.** A hint in
  the last two turns leaves no room to observe what the candidate did with it, and
  observing the response is the entire point.
- **The hint gives information, not the answer.** "The bottleneck turned out to be
  on the write path" is a hint; "so you'd add a cache" is the answer and destroys
  the item.
- **The hint is offered as help, not as correction.** Framed as a correction it
  becomes negative feedback and collides with the verdict ban.
- **What happened after the hint is what gets recorded.** Did the candidate take
  it, extend it, question it, or ignore it? That turn is the evidence; the hint
  itself is not.
- **One hint supports a recorded observation, not a scale rating.** Where the
  rubric scores coachability on a multi-point scale, the axis needs more than one
  hint event — a short graded ladder inside one item, or one hint in each of
  several items — before a number is written. With one event, record the outcome
  in words and label the axis low-reliability wherever the number would otherwise
  be read.
- **A hint that never happened leaves the axis unassessed, not average.** A
  scorer that fills a missing observation with the scale's midpoint has read
  absence of evidence as a middling result, and the midpoint is then averaged into
  everything downstream. Where the conversation's own record can show whether the
  hint turn occurred, the scorer consults it before writing the axis at all.
- **If the rubric has no learning or coachability axis, the hint is still worth
  one turn** as an anxiety floor — but it should not then be mined for a rating,
  because a rating needs an axis it was designed for.
- **The hint's scripting lives in a field that is structurally internal.** Where
  any part of the interview plan is derived for a candidate-facing surface, the
  coachability step is the one item that must never survive the derivation — its
  content *is* the stage direction. Keeping the hint text in a field that the
  candidate-facing allow-list does not pick, rather than relying on it being
  filtered out, is what makes the guarantee hold as the brief grows.
- **Push for specifics a reviewer could quote.** The hint turn is scored on what
  the candidate did with it, and that is only scoreable if the answer contains
  something concrete. This is the same narrowing instinct applied to the hint's
  aftermath rather than to a thin answer.

## When not to use it

- **Where the exercise's validity depends on unaided work** — a timed
  work-sample whose difficulty *is* the measurement — a hint contaminates the
  item. Move the coachability observation to a different part of the loop rather
  than deleting it.
- **Where the population is senior and the topic is their own past work.** A hint
  about someone's own project is nonsensical and reads as condescension; the
  equivalent move there is offering a contrasting view and observing how it is
  engaged.
- **In screening conversations that collect facts rather than assess.** There is
  nothing to be coachable about.

## The failure mode it prevents

Briefs accumulate prohibitions because every incident produces one. A document
that only says *never* produces an interviewer that is technically compliant and
experienced as cold — clipped acknowledgements, no give, no help. Nervous
candidates underperform against it and the scores it produces are partly a
measure of composure. One guaranteed act of generosity, written as a rule with
the same force as the bans, is what keeps the instrument measuring the job.
