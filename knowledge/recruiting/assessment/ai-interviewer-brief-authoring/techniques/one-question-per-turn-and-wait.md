# One question per turn, and wait

The interviewer's turn contains exactly one question, and then it stops. Not one
topic, not one paragraph ending in a question mark — one thing the candidate can
answer. Everything the interviewer wanted to ask alongside it is a later turn or
is not asked.

## Why the stacked turn destroys the item

A turn carrying three questions is answered on one of them. The candidate picks —
usually the last one heard, or the one they have a prepared story for — and the
other two are now *unasked in fact but asked on the record*. Downstream, the
transcript shows the competency was raised, and nothing distinguishes "asked and
not answered" from "answered thinly". That is the exact shape of
[absence of evidence being read as evidence](../../../_laws.md#absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence):
a topic that was never actually assessed inherits whatever the surrounding
answers imply.

The second harm is selection. Letting the candidate choose which sub-question to
answer converts a fixed instrument into a self-selected one, and it converts it
differently for each candidate — the strongest effect is on the most fluent, who
reliably steer to their prepared ground.

The third is time. A stacked question produces a long answer that covers less. In
a fixed-length conversation, coverage is the scarce resource, and multi-part
questions are the fastest way to spend it on the topic the candidate liked most.

## The rule, as written in a brief

State it as a hard constraint on the turn, in the affirmative, and state the
waiting explicitly — the wait is the half that gets dropped:

- **One question per turn.** If two things need asking, ask the first and let the
  answer decide whether the second is still worth a turn. It often is not.
- **The question ends the turn.** No trailing clarification, no "for example, you
  might talk about…", no restatement. An example appended to a question is a
  leading probe wearing a helpful hat, and candidates take offered answers.
- **Silence belongs to the candidate.** After a question, and after an answer that
  seems to have ended, the interviewer does not fill the pause. A few seconds of
  quiet reliably produces the more honest continuation; the rescue instinct —
  offering a hint, rephrasing, answering for them — cuts that sentence off. A
  candidate using silence to think is not a candidate who cannot answer, and
  [uncertainty about which one it is resolves toward the candidate](../../../_laws.md#uncertainty-resolves-toward-the-candidate).
- **Cutting into a rambling answer is authorised and is not an exception.** Where
  an answer has stopped producing new material, the interviewer may interrupt
  politely at a natural pause and move on — and *every time it recurs*, not once.
  This is not in tension with waiting: waiting protects a thinking candidate,
  interrupting protects the coverage budget from a talking one. Both are
  one-question discipline. A brief that grants only the first produces interviews
  where one candidate got four questions and another got eleven.
- **With a rambling candidate, set the expectation before cutting in.** Naming
  the shape you need up front — a short concrete answer, one example — is fairer
  than repeatedly interrupting someone who was never told, and it usually removes
  the need to. State the expectation once, then enforce it quietly.
- **An off-topic question from the candidate is closed in one line, then the
  interviewer returns to its own question.** Neither ignored nor followed. A
  brief without this rule produces either rudeness or a hijacked conversation.

## Decision rules

- **When a question needs context to be answerable, the context is a statement
  and the question is still one question.** "Your last role overlapped with a
  platform migration. What was your own part in it?" is one question with a
  premise, not two questions.
- **When the candidate answers a different question than the one asked, do not
  re-ask and do not stack.** Take the answer, then narrow into the gap
  (narrowing-follow-up-not-repetition).
- **When two competencies could be covered by one clever combined question, do not
  combine them.** The combined answer cannot be attributed to either.
- **When the turn budget is running out, drop a topic rather than compress two
  into one turn.** An honestly unassessed competency is a recordable state; a
  half-assessed one is not.

## When not to use it

- **Opening and closing turns may legitimately carry non-question content** — a
  greeting, the frame of what will happen, the closing read-back. The rule
  constrains *interview* turns; applying it to the frame produces an interviewer
  that starts abruptly.
- **Genuine clarification of the interviewer's own words is not a second
  question.** If the candidate asks what was meant, restating is correct and does
  not count as a re-ask.
- **Structured multi-part exercises** — a live task with stated steps — are a
  different instrument. There the steps are the instrument, and one-per-turn is
  handled by the exercise design rather than the brief.

## How it fails quietly

The rule is usually written and usually obeyed at the sentence level while being
violated at the turn level: the interviewer asks one question, receives the
answer, and immediately asks a follow-up plus a new topic in the same turn.
Checking compliance means counting question marks *and* counting distinct askable
propositions per turn — the second number is the real one. The other quiet
failure is a brief that forbids stacking without authorising interruption, which
trades one coverage failure for another.
