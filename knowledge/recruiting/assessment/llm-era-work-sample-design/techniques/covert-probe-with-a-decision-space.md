# Covert probe with a decision space

A covert probe is a deliberately ambiguous point in the exercise, undisclosed
to the candidate, that forces a judgment and reveals something specific about
them. It is the unit of measurement in a work sample whose artifact proves
nothing. Everything else in the design — the material, the log, the debrief —
exists to create probes, record what happened at them, or interrogate it.

Two words carry the whole definition. **Covert**: the candidate is never told
which parts are deliberate, because a marked probe is an answer key. **Decision
space**: the probe exposes two or three genuinely defensible options with
different trade-offs — never one right answer surrounded by distractors.

## Anatomy of a probe

A probe that can be scored has four parts, and the first two are where designs
fail:

- **A concrete seam.** A specific place in the supplied material where the
  decision must actually be made — not a general theme. "Thinks about
  performance" is not a probe; the point where a fetch inside a loop meets a
  volume assumption is.
- **A decision space of two or three defensible options.** Each option needs a
  one-sentence case a respected practitioner could make. Options with a strawman
  among them are distractors, and a probe with distractors measures whether the
  candidate can find the key.
- **A stated criterion separating good from naive.** Written at design time, in
  the internal notes: what distinguishes a resolution that engaged with the
  trade-off from one that took the first path. Without it, scoring drifts to
  taste, and taste is not comparable across interviewers.
- **What it reveals.** A mandatory, specific statement of the trait or
  behaviour the probe is evidence about — "whether they verify inherited
  assumptions before building on them", not "seniority". A probe that cannot say
  what it reveals has no place in a rubric and cannot be defended if the
  decision it influences is ever challenged.

## The three probe kinds

- **Underspecified requirement.** A stated need with more than one honest
  reading. The brief's constraints bear on the choice without settling it.
  Reveals: whether the candidate notices the fork, and whether they reason from
  the constraints they were given or from a default habit.
- **Legacy or surprising area.** Inherited material that behaves unexpectedly,
  contradicts the obvious approach, or predates the task. Reveals: whether they
  investigate before extending, and whether they can weigh working-with against
  replacing.
- **Verification trap.** Something that looks correct and is not, or that the
  supplied checks cannot confirm. Reveals: whether they trust fluent output or
  test it — the most job-relevant habit there is when most output arrives
  pre-written.

Two to four probes is a working range for a two-hour exercise. Below two, one
candidate's off day dominates the result and the audit has nothing to roll up.
Above four, the exercise cannot be finished and you are measuring speed.

Seniority is expressed in the *depth* of the probes, not their count: junior
cases get simpler forks and more scaffolding, senior cases get the same number
of forks with harder trade-offs inside them.

Probes are also the right place to convert a hypothesis about a *specific*
candidate into a test. Where prior evidence raised a question — a claimed
strength with nothing behind it, a suspiciously narrow depth — plant a probe
that would resolve it, phrased as a natural part of the task and never
disclosed. This keeps a hypothesis from silently hardening into a verdict: the
document raised it, the exercise tests it, the debrief confirms or drops it.
The rest of the case stays identical across the cohort so the comparison
survives.

## Procedure

1. Pick the responsibilities from the role's must-have list that most need
   evidence, and choose one probe kind per responsibility.
2. Find or create the seam in the material where that judgment is unavoidable.
   If the candidate can complete the task without touching the seam, it is not
   a probe. Name the seam concretely — the specific item, section or step —
   never a template phrase like "as described in the brief"; an unlocatable
   seam is the commonest way a well-written probe turns out to measure nothing.
3. Write the decision space. Two or three options, each with its case and its
   cost. Delete any option that needs a strawman to lose.
4. Write the good-versus-naive criterion and the `reveals` statement. If either
   is hard to write, the probe is not load-bearing — cut it now rather than
   after twenty candidates.
5. Keep all of the above in internal notes. What the candidate receives is the
   task, phrased naturally, with the ambiguity intact and unmarked.
6. Audit before shipping — see the discrimination audit, which is the gate this
   technique feeds.

## Decision rules

- **When only one option is defensible, it is not a probe — it is a task.**
  Either find the genuine fork nearby or accept the item measures execution, not
  judgment, and weight it accordingly.
- **When the probe would only fire if the candidate worked unaided, delete
  it.** A probe that penalizes assistance is a trap. Design probes that a person
  using every tool available still has to decide.
- **When a candidate never reached the probe, record it as not measured.** Not
  zero, not a fail. An unreached probe is an absence of evidence and must
  render as its own state, per
  [absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence](../../../_laws.md#absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence);
  coercing it to a score ranks an unmeasured person as if they had been
  measured and failed.
- **When a probe outcome is inferred rather than observed, label it as
  inference.** What the artifact shows about a decision is a hypothesis about
  the person; what they said in the debrief is evidence. The two never render
  alike ([inference-must-look-like-inference](../../../_laws.md#inference-must-look-like-inference)).
- **When the candidate asks directly whether something is intentional, do not
  confirm it.** Confirmation converts the probe into a labelled task for that
  candidate and destroys comparability with everyone else.
- **Never disclose the probe list, the criteria, or the reveals statements.**
  They are internal interviewer notes. Disclosed, they are the solution.

## When not to use it

- **Screening at the very top of the funnel**, where volume forces automated
  scoring. Covert probes need a human reading of a decision; a machine-scored
  screen cannot carry them and should not pretend to.
- **Exercises under about forty-five minutes.** There is no room for a fork to
  cost anything, so probes degenerate into gotchas.
- **Where the resulting decision is adverse and unreviewed.** A probe outcome
  is one input to a human judgment, never a routable automated rejection.

## Failure modes

- **The disguised quiz.** A key exists; the debrief has nothing to discuss.
- **The floating probe.** Real trade-off, no seam — candidates never encounter
  it, and the cohort column is empty.
- **The universal probe.** Everyone handles it identically. It costs candidate
  time and yields no separation; it is a candidate for deletion at the next
  audit.
- **The unwritten criterion.** Scored from memory, differently by each
  interviewer, and indefensible when the decision is questioned.
