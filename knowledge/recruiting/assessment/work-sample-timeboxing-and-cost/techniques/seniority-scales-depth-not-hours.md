# Seniority scales depth, not hours

The rule: a more senior exercise raises **ambiguity and depth of judgment** and
keeps the hours and the deliverable count flat or lower. It never adds
sub-deliverables, never adds tasks, and never adds time.

This contradicts an instinct so strong that most per-level tables violate it on
the first draft — junior gets ninety minutes, senior gets four hours, staff gets
a day — as though seniority were measured in endurance. It is not, and the table
that says it is has three separate defects.

## Why the longer senior exercise fails

- **It filters the exact pool the exercise exists for.** Senior candidates have
  other processes running, less need to prove themselves, and a sharp sense of
  what unpaid time costs. They decline long exercises at a much higher rate than
  junior candidates do. Extending the senior version therefore produces the
  worst possible sample: the strongest people gone, the ones with the most free
  time remaining, and a scoring distribution that looks reassuringly wide because
  it is measuring availability.
- **It buys volume, and volume is the thing capable tooling makes free.** More
  deliverables is more artifact, and artifact quality is roughly constant across
  a pool with the same tooling. You spend the candidate's scarcest resource on
  the dimension with the least discriminating power left in it.
- **It crowds out the judgment it was supposed to measure.** Deciding well takes
  thinking time. A senior candidate given six deliverables in four hours does
  each one shallowly — you have converted a judgment test into a throughput test
  and then graded the throughput.

## What "more depth" actually means

Depth is cheap in effort and expensive in judgment. Concretely, within the same
task count and the same hours:

- **A fork with a real cost on both sides**, where the brief's constraints bear
  on the choice but do not settle it, rather than a requirement with one obvious
  reading.
- **Less scaffolding.** The junior version names the file, the constraint and
  the acceptance check; the senior version states the business need and lets the
  candidate locate the seam. Same work, more judgment, no extra minutes.
- **Consequences that reach beyond the task** — a decision that is locally fine
  and expensive at ten times the volume, or that a downstream consumer will pay
  for. Senior work is distinguished by second-order reasoning, and second-order
  reasoning is elicited by a constraint, not by another deliverable.
- **A harder trade-off to articulate.** What they gave up, and what would have
  changed their mind, is the senior signal. It is drawn out in the debrief, which
  costs the exercise nothing.

The design vocabulary for these — planted ambiguity, decision spaces, seams — is
the work-sample-design neighbour's territory. What belongs here is the budget
discipline: whatever they design, it fits the same hours.

## Procedure

1. **Write the per-level table with the same effort ceiling on every row.** Vary
   ambiguity, scaffolding and depth down the rows; hold hours and deliverable
   count constant or decreasing.
2. **Key the row off a stable internal level vocabulary, not a title string.**
   Level names are a display convention that differs wildly between companies and
   drifts inside one; nothing in the sizing may derive meaning from the words on
   a job advertisement
   ([meaning-does-not-live-in-a-label](../../../_laws.md#meaning-does-not-live-in-a-label)).
   An unrecognised level maps to the most junior scaffolding and the same hours —
   never to the longest exercise.
3. **Review each senior variant against its junior sibling and count.** If the
   senior one has more deliverables, the calibration is wrong regardless of how
   good the tasks are. Cut back to parity and push the difference into ambiguity.
4. **Check the ceiling is the binding constraint, not the floor.** A senior
   exercise that comes in shorter than the junior one because the ambiguity
   replaced scaffolding is a success, not a mistake.
5. **Compare drop-off across levels after shipping.** Senior drop-off materially
   above junior drop-off means the senior variant is more expensive in practice
   than the table claims, whatever the table says.

## Decision rules

- **When a senior exercise needs to be "harder", add a fork, not a task.** The
  smallest useful edit is usually deleting scaffolding from a task that already
  exists.
- **When a level genuinely needs to see more surface** — a lead role that must
  demonstrate breadth — get it from the debrief, which is bounded by a scheduled
  session and costs the candidate no unpaid preparation.
- **When the level is unknown or ambiguous at invitation time, size down.** A
  candidate who found the exercise trivially easy costs you one debrief question;
  a candidate priced out of it costs you the candidate.
- **When a per-level row would exceed the cap, the row is wrong.** The cap is not
  negotiable by seniority; that is what makes it a cap.

## When not to use it

The rule assumes a single instrument scaled across levels. Where levels are
assessed by genuinely different instruments — an entry-level structured task
versus a senior architecture conversation — there is no table to calibrate, and
the question becomes whether each instrument's own cost is justified. It also
does not apply to paid trials, where scope is negotiated and compensated rather
than capped.

## The failure it prevents

The recognisable signature is a senior submission set that is uniformly
competent, uniformly complete, uniformly hard to rank — because the exercise
asked for volume and everyone's tooling supplied it — sitting next to a senior
invitation-acceptance rate far below the junior one. Both symptoms have the same
cause, and adding time makes both worse.
