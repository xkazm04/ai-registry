# Per-question time budget that tightens

Minutes per question is not a constant. It is a **function of the question count**, and
the function decreases: the more questions a plan carries, the less time each one gets,
because the total available for questions was fixed before the list was written.

Stating this as a rule rather than discovering it live is the entire technique. A plan
that assigns a generous constant per question and then lists nine of them has not
allocated ninety minutes of interview; it has written a promise the clock will break, and
the breakage always lands on the last items in the list.

## The shape of the function

Let the question budget be the booked duration minus the fixed opening and closing, minus
the open block's minimum. Then per-question minutes is that budget divided by the count,
subject to three constraints:

- **A floor** on per-question minutes. Around four minutes for a competency question in
  a human round: enough for the answer plus at least one narrowing follow-up. Below it,
  the question gets the rehearsed narrative and nothing else. Structured interviewing
  earns its validity in the follow-up — the move from "we improved reliability" to "what
  did you personally change, and how did you know it worked" — and a plan that budgets
  the answer but not the probe has budgeted the least informative half.
- **A ceiling** on per-question minutes. Past roughly eight to ten minutes a single
  question is no longer a question; it is an unstructured segment wearing a question's
  label. If a short list leaves that much per item, the surplus belongs in a named open
  block, not spread thin across the questions.
- **A maximum question count** for the round. It follows from the floor, and it should be
  written down as its own number because it is the constraint a kit generator will
  otherwise violate cheerfully. When the generator produces more than the maximum, the
  plan carries the maximum and the remainder is visibly held back, not silently
  appended.

## Decision rules

- **When the floor binds, cut questions — do not shorten the floor.** The instinct is to
  keep coverage and accept three minutes each. That trades a plan that assesses six
  competencies properly for one that assesses ten badly, and a badly assessed competency
  does not produce a cautious rating; it produces a confident one grounded in nothing.
- **A competency with no minutes is recorded as unassessed, not rated low.** Per
  [absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence](../../../_laws.md#absence-of-evidence-is-not-evidence),
  the axis that never came up in the conversation has no reading, and the scorecard must
  be able to say so. The alternative — an interviewer filling the box from general
  impression — is the most common way an unfed axis enters a decision meeting looking
  like data.
- **Publish per-question minutes in the plan the interviewer holds.** Not just the total.
  An interviewer who can see "6 minutes" beside a question paces to it; one who sees only
  a list paces to the conversation and discovers the shortfall at question five.
- **Recompute when the list changes.** Adding one question to a full plan shortens every
  other question. If the person adding it does not see that happen, they will believe
  they added coverage when they subtracted depth.
- **Weight before you divide, if you weight at all.** Equal minutes per question is the
  right default; where a round has one question that genuinely decides it, give that one
  a multiple and divide the remainder evenly, and mark it in the plan so the interviewer
  knows which one to protect.

## The ratings this protects

A rating is bound to what it judged, per
[a-verdict-is-bound-to-what-it-judged](../../../_laws.md#a-verdict-is-bound-to-what-it-judged).
Two candidates whose plans allocated eight and three minutes to the same competency were
not assessed on the same instrument even though their scorecards look identical, and a
cohort comparison across those two ratings is comparing a probe with a mention. So the
per-question allocation is not only a pacing aid — it is part of what makes ratings
comparable, and it belongs in the round record alongside the version of the phase table.

## When not to use this

- **Work-sample and case rounds** where the whole round is one extended activity. The
  budget there is a phase budget inside the activity, not a per-question one; the
  arithmetic is the same but the unit is not.
- **Deliberately unstructured segments**, which should be one named block with one
  duration rather than a set of questions with per-item budgets that nobody will follow.
- **Rounds where the count is externally fixed** — a standardised question set required
  for comparability or compliance. There the count is a given and the *duration* is the
  dependent variable, so the booking must move, not the list.
