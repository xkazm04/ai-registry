# Interview run of show

A run of show is the plan for one booked conversation: an ordered set of blocks, each
with a start, a length, a purpose, and the material to run it. It is the artifact that
stands between a well-designed interview and an interview that was actually conducted.
Everything upstream — which rounds exist, which competencies each round owns, what
rubric the ratings land on — is a design. The run of show is the only place that design
meets a clock, and the clock wins every argument it is allowed to have.

The naive reading treats it as formatting: take the questions, print them in order, hand
them over. That produces the two artifacts every talent team has seen. The first is the
plan that does not fit — nine questions and a closing in a forty-five minute block,
which is not a forty-five minute interview but a sixty-five minute one that will be
amputated live, always from the end, which is where the closing and the candidate's own
questions live. The second is rarer and worse because it looks fine: the plan that
under-fills its block — a sparse question set expanded into a schedule that genuinely
runs twenty-two minutes, printed under a heading that says forty-five, and conducted as
a short conversation everyone afterwards believes was a full interview.

## A run of show is a budget, and every budget states priorities

This is the whole subject in one sentence. Minutes are finite and non-transferable: an
interview cannot borrow time from next week. So a plan is an allocation, and like every
allocation it is a statement about what matters — including the parts nobody said out
loud.

The consequence is uncomfortable and worth stating plainly to any team that resists it:
**the competency that never gets minutes is the one the loop is not actually assessing,
however prominently it appears on the scorecard.** A rubric axis with no block feeding
it does not produce a weak rating; it produces a rating manufactured from general
impression and the halo of everything else that happened, which is worse than no rating
because it carries a number. When the plan and the scorecard disagree, the plan is the
truth. The scorecard is an aspiration.

That gives the single most useful review question for any run of show: hold the
scorecard beside it and ask which axis is fed by which block. Every unfed axis is either
a design error to fix here or an axis to remove from this round's instrument. Both are
fine. Leaving it unfed and still on the form is not.

## The booked block is an input, not an output

A run of show does not decide how long the conversation is. It is *handed* a duration by
whoever booked the meeting, and its job is to be runnable inside that duration. The
integrity of the booking itself — that the invitation, the calendar entry, the
candidate's understanding and the plan all say the same number, that a rescheduled slot
carries its length with it — belongs to the sibling discipline on calendar integrity.
This subject consumes the booked duration and owes it one thing in return: honesty about
whether the plan fills it.

Which produces the rule that fixes the second failure mode above. When a plan's blocks
genuinely sum to less than the block that was booked, the correct move is to **promise
the shorter, truthful duration**, not the longer, aspirational one. A candidate told
"about twenty-five minutes" who is released at twenty-five has been dealt with
straight. A candidate told forty-five and released at twenty-five draws the obvious
inference — that they were dismissed early — and it is frequently wrong, but the process
handed them the evidence for it. Say only what the plan holds.

Upstream, the same arithmetic is a symptom, not a fact of life: a plan that cannot fill
its block usually means the brief it was generated from is too thin to interview
against. Fix the brief, or shorten the booking. Do not pad the plan with blocks that
exist to consume minutes.

## Fixed blocks, then a budget for what is left

Every conversation has a spine that does not vary with how many questions there are:

- **An opening** — who is on the call and why, what the round is for, how long it will
  run, what happens afterwards, and any disclosure the candidate is owed before
  assessment begins (that a machine is participating, that a recording is being kept,
  that notes will be shared with a panel).
- **A closing** — the candidate's questions, and a concrete statement of what happens
  next and by when.

These blocks are small — a few minutes each — and they are the first thing an overrunning
interview eats, which is exactly backwards. The opening is what makes the following
thirty minutes interpretable; a candidate who does not know what the round is for gives
answers aimed at the wrong target and is then rated for the miss. The closing is where
the candidate's own decision-making is served, and cutting it converts a two-way
conversation into an examination. It is also, in practice, the single most common
source of "the process felt disrespectful" feedback: not the hard questions, the
truncated ending.

So they are fixed first, deducted from the booked duration, and only the remainder is
budgeted. The remainder — not the whole block — is the question budget. This one
reordering removes most overrun.

## Depth is the dependent variable

Given a fixed question budget, minutes-per-question is a function of question count, and
the function tightens: more questions means less time each. That is arithmetic, not
policy. The policy question is what to do about it, and there are only two honest
answers — ask fewer questions, or accept shallower ones — and one dishonest one, which
is to plan generous per-question time for a long list and let the interviewer discover
the shortfall at minute thirty.

Make the tightening explicit in the plan so the trade is visible while it can still be
changed. And put a **floor** under it. Below roughly four minutes a competency question
stops being a structured probe: there is time for the answer and no time for the
follow-up, and the follow-up is where structured interviewing gets its validity — the
first answer is usually a rehearsed narrative, and the probe into specifics is what
separates the candidate who did the thing from the candidate who was nearby when it
happened. A plan that has driven per-question time under the floor has not made the
interview more thorough; it has converted it into a quiz. When the floor binds, cut
questions. There is also a **ceiling on count** for the same reason: past a certain
number, adding a question can only subtract depth from every other one.

The corresponding cap on the other side: a very short question list should not inflate
per-question minutes without limit, because "twelve minutes on this question" is not a
plan an interviewer can execute — it is an instruction to improvise for eight minutes.
Slack is better named than diluted, which is the next section.

## Slack is a block with a name

Plans are built from estimates, and estimates written as ranges get summed at their
lower bounds. The residue is real: a plan whose header says forty-five minutes and whose
last block ends at thirty-eight has seven unplanned minutes, and unplanned minutes are
not unused — they are improvised, by whoever is holding the clock, in the least designed
part of the conversation. The sibling subject on round design found the same arithmetic
one level up, where phase ranges sum below the round's advertised length, and reached
the same rule: **assign the slack to a named block.**

The right home for it is an open-discussion block placed late but before the closing —
the interviewer's chance to chase whatever the earlier answers raised, deepen a thin
one, or let the candidate expand on something they clearly want to talk about. It
absorbs both directions of drift: it grows when the questions ran fast, and it is the
designated casualty when they ran long. Every plan should have exactly one such block,
and every interviewer should know that it is the one they are allowed to sacrifice —
because if nothing is designated, the thing sacrificed is the closing.

## A question earns its minutes by naming a gap

The generative half of the subject. What separates a real question kit from a topic list
is grounding: each question is tied to a **named gap in the evidence** — something this
specific record does not settle — rather than to a subject area. "Tell me about a time
you led a project" is a topic. "The record shows two years leading a team of four but
nothing about the outcome of the migration described as your main project — walk me
through how it ended" is a gap.

Three consequences follow, and each is a discipline in its own right:

- **The kit is organised by what kind of gap it closes**, not by difficulty or seniority.
  A working taxonomy: behavioural gaps (patterns of past conduct the record hints at but
  does not evidence), technical gaps (claimed capability with no demonstration behind
  it), and defence gaps — the places where the record contains something a reviewer
  will worry about. Any taxonomy needs a catch-all bucket, because a generated question
  that fits no bucket must land somewhere visible rather than being silently dropped;
  a question that disappears between generation and printing is a gap that goes
  unprobed and is then recorded as unassessed for no reason anyone can reconstruct.
- **The defence questions are named out loud.** Where the record has an apparent problem
  — a gap in employment, a short tenure, a step that looks like a step down, a missing
  credential — the question states the worry directly and invites the answer. This feels
  confrontational and is the opposite: the concern exists whether or not it is voiced,
  and a candidate who is never asked cannot answer it. The practitioner's formulation is
  that *defensiveness reads worse than the gap itself* — which is precisely why the
  question must be phrased as an invitation to explain rather than an accusation to
  parry, and why the interviewer needs it written down rather than improvised in the
  moment when the tone is hardest to control. The worry is a question, never a finding;
  where the record does not settle it, the uncertainty resolves toward the candidate.
- **Each question carries a scaffold for the answer** — what a good answer contains,
  what to listen for, what follow-up to use if the answer stays abstract. Without it the
  question is a prompt for a story and the rating that follows is a rating of the story.
  This is the seam with the scorecards subject: the scaffold's listen-fors must be the
  same behaviours the rubric's anchors describe, or the plan and the instrument are
  measuring different things.

The kit is a *prep pack*, not a script. It travels with a short signals checklist — the
handful of things worth watching for across the whole conversation regardless of which
question is running — and with the plan itself.

## The plan is written on, and regeneration must survive that

A run of show is generated, then a human edits it: crosses out two questions, writes
"ask about the second role instead", adds the thing the hiring manager mentioned in the
corridor. Then something upstream changes — the brief is updated, the round is
reassigned, a newer version of the kit exists — and the artifact is regenerated.

The default merge, where the fresh generated content wins, destroys the interviewer's
work, and it does so silently, at the moment they are least likely to re-read a document
they wrote themselves. **Invert it: human content wins.** Generated material fills what
the human has not touched; it never overwrites what they have. And the protection must
not take the form of a list of human-authored parts to spare — such a list is always one
field out of date the day someone adds a new place for a human to write, and the loss is
silent because the list still looks complete. Preservation is the default; the
generator's ownership is the enumerated exception. Imported questions are de-duplicated
against what is already there and capped, so repeated regeneration cannot grow the pack
past what fits the block — which would quietly reintroduce the overrun this whole subject
exists to prevent.

The deeper principle is authorship. A prep pack has two authors with different standing,
and only one of them can be asked afterwards why a question is on the list. Preserve
whose text is whose.

## After the conversation: the debrief is part of the show

The minutes do not stop when the call ends. Writing the ratings takes real time, it is
the part everyone assumes is free, and it is the part that decides whether the loop has
evidence or recollection. Ratings written the next morning are recollections; the
quotable specifics are gone within the hour. So the plan allocates debrief minutes as a
block, scaled to the size of the instrument being filled — more competencies, more
writing — with a cap, because past a certain point a longer form is a form that will not
be completed rather than a longer debrief. Book that time adjacent to the conversation,
not at the end of the day.

## Seams with the neighbouring subjects

- **Round design** owns how many conversations the loop has and what each is uniquely
  for; it hands this subject one round with a purpose and a competency set. Round design
  stops at the boundary of the round, and the run of show starts inside it.
- **Scorecards** own the rubric the plan must reach. The plan does not define what good
  looks like; it guarantees that every axis the rubric asks about had minutes in which
  it could be observed.
- **Machine-interviewer briefs** own instructing an automated interviewer's *behaviour* —
  how it follows up, what it may not say. Timing belongs here, and it should stay here:
  a brief that carries wall-clock instructions ages badly and conflicts with whatever
  surface is actually holding the clock. Give the brief the plan's shape, not its
  stopwatch.
- **Calendar integrity** owns the booked duration itself and everything that can happen
  to it between booking and start.

## Failure modes this standard exists to prevent

- **The amputated ending.** An over-full plan, cut live from the back, so the closing and
  the candidate's questions are what the overrun eats.
- **The short interview in a long costume.** A sparse kit expanded into a plan that runs
  half its advertised length, with nobody told.
- **The unfed axis.** A competency on the scorecard that no block produces evidence for,
  rated anyway from general impression.
- **The unnamed slack.** Blocks that sum below the total, leaving the least designed
  minutes to be improvised.
- **The quiz.** Question count driven so high that no question has room for a follow-up,
  presented as thoroughness.
- **The topic list.** Questions attached to subject areas rather than to gaps this
  record actually leaves open — generic, unrankable, and identical for every candidate.
- **The unasked worry.** A concern in the record that no question names, carried
  unresolved into the decision meeting where the candidate cannot answer it.
- **The overwritten pack.** Regeneration silently discarding an interviewer's own notes.
- **The unbooked debrief.** Ratings written from memory hours later, evidence-free, and
  then treated as data.
