# What a work sample costs the person doing it

A work sample is the only stage of a hiring process where you ask someone to
perform unpaid labour, alone, under observation, on a schedule you set, with no
guarantee of anything in return. Every other stage costs a candidate an hour of
conversation. This one costs them an evening, or a weekend, or a day of leave —
and it costs it *before* you have committed to anything. That asymmetry is the
whole subject. The design question of what the exercise measures belongs to the
neighbouring subject on work-sample design; the question here is what you are
entitled to ask for, from whom, and what you owe them while they do it.

The naive framing is that a longer exercise is a better measurement — more
surface, more to grade, more confidence. It is not, and the failure is not
subtle. Length does two things simultaneously: it improves the measurement
slightly, and it changes *who is in the sample*. Past a small number of hours,
the second effect dominates, and you are no longer measuring capability at all.

## The cost is not distributed evenly, and that is the ethical core

Four hours of unpaid evening work is a mild inconvenience to a person with no
dependents, a stable home, one job, a reliable machine, and a quiet room. It is
close to impossible for a single parent, someone caring for a relative, someone
working a second job to make rent, someone in temporary or shared housing,
someone whose health means their usable hours are unpredictable, or someone
whose current employer's hours leave no contiguous block. These are not edge
cases. In most labour markets they are a large minority of the qualified pool,
and they are correlated with exactly the characteristics that anti-discrimination
law protects — so a length policy is a selection policy whether or not you meant
it to be.

The consequence is blunt: **every hour you add to a work sample filters your pool
toward people with more discretionary time, and discretionary time is not a job
requirement.** The candidates you lose are not the weak ones. Strong,
in-demand practitioners are the *most* likely to decline a long exercise, because
they have other processes running, less need to prove themselves, and a
well-calibrated sense of what their unpaid time is worth. A half-day take-home
reliably produces drop-off in the region of half the invited senior pool — the
pool the exercise exists to discriminate within. You end up with a beautifully
instrumented measurement of the people who had a free Saturday.

Watch this directly rather than assuming it. Track invitation-to-submission
conversion **by level and by demographic segment where you lawfully hold it**, and
treat a fall as an instrument defect, not a candidate-quality signal. A rate that
drops when you lengthen the exercise is telling you the length is the filter. The
funnel-metrics neighbour owns how those rates are computed and what sample they
need to be trustworthy; what belongs here is the reading — that drop-off at this
stage is a cost you imposed, not a property of the market.

## The cap comes first, and the exercise is designed inside it

The order matters more than the number. Decide the maximum candidate effort
**before** designing the tasks, publish it, and then scope the exercise to fit.
The reverse order — write the exercise you want, then estimate how long it takes —
produces overrun every time, because the author knows the answer and the
candidate does not. A designer's estimate of their own exercise is routinely half
the real time; a candidate meeting the material cold pays a comprehension tax the
author never pays.

A defensible policy in most markets, for most roles, looks like this:

- **A hard upper bound in the low single-digit hours.** Two hours is a good
  default, three is defensible, four is the point where you should be arguing
  for it explicitly and probably paying. A day is not a work sample, it is a
  contract.
- **The bound is a cap, not a target.** A candidate who does an honest job in
  half the stated time has not underperformed. Say so in the brief, because
  otherwise the stated number reads as an expectation and everyone spends it.
- **The bound applies to total candidate effort**, including reading the brief,
  setting up, and any write-up or debrief preparation — not just the part you
  think of as "the task". Setup and comprehension are real and they are unpaid.
- **Overrun is not extra credit.** State that work beyond the cap will not
  improve the assessment, and mean it: if the top submissions are the longest
  ones, you have built a stamina test and you are systematically rewarding the
  candidates with the most free time.

Anyone can propose a longer exercise — a hiring manager, a role brief, an
automated case generator. Proposals are inputs, not decisions. The cap is
enforced by clamping every proposed value into the policy band at the moment the
exercise is produced, and the clamp is recorded rather than silently applied, so
that a pattern of managers asking for six hours is visible as a policy problem
instead of disappearing into a maximum function.

## Depth, not hours, is how you calibrate seniority

The instinct to make the senior version longer is the single most common way a
timebox policy dies. It is also unnecessary, because length was never what made
a senior exercise senior. **Raise the ambiguity and the depth of judgment; keep
the deliverable count and the hours flat, or lower them.** A senior case with
three tasks and genuinely hard calls inside them discriminates better than the
same case with six tasks, and it costs the candidate a third as much. The
design side of this — what "more ambiguous" concretely means, how a decision
space is planted — is the work-sample-design neighbour's; the economic half is
here, and it is the reason a per-level table must be bounded by the same cap at
every row.

The corollary is a hard rule for exercise authors: **when the tasks do not fit
the cap, cut tasks — never extend the cap.** Prefer a few focused tasks with real
depth over broad coverage. Coverage is the thing that assistance makes free
anyway, so the hours you buy with it purchase almost no signal.

## When you should be paying

Payment is not a nice gesture, it is what converts a request into a fair
exchange, and there is a workable line for when it becomes obligatory:

- **Pay when the exercise exceeds roughly half a day**, at any level. Beyond that
  point you are asking for a meaningful slice of someone's labour, and the
  ability to donate it is a function of wealth.
- **Pay when the work has any value to you.** If the output is usable — a real
  bug fixed, a real analysis you will read, a design you might ship — it is work,
  not an assessment, and the boundary was crossed at the moment you chose real
  material over synthetic. The safe default is the reverse: build exercises on
  material you will never use, so the question does not arise.
- **Pay when the exercise happens on site or in a fixed window**, because you are
  consuming a whole block of the candidate's day, travel and all.
- **State the intellectual-property position in plain words, before they start.**
  The candidate retains their submission; you use it solely to assess them; you
  will not incorporate it into a product; you delete it on the retention schedule
  you published. Silence here is read — correctly — as a claim.
- **Where you cannot pay, shorten.** Budget constraints are a real reason to keep
  an exercise to ninety minutes; they are never a reason to ask for four unpaid
  hours instead.

Payment does not launder a badly-scoped exercise. A paid six-hour exercise still
excludes the person who does not have six contiguous hours, and payment does not
buy you the right to skip an accommodation. It removes the wealth filter, not the
time filter.

## The duty of care while they are working

Once someone is inside a timed exercise, they are in a state you engineered: a
clock running, a task they cannot fully see the shape of, and an audience. Four
obligations follow, and each has a failure mode that is invisible from your side
of the screen.

**Tell them exactly what is and is not observed.** Before the clock starts, in
plain candidate-facing language, not in a policy document: what artifacts you
keep, what events you record, and — stated positively — what you never capture.
"We do not record your keystrokes or your screen" is worth more than three
paragraphs of process description, because that is the fear people actually have.
The contract must be true of the implementation, and the burden runs the honest
direction: if the tooling can capture something, saying it does not is a lie you
will one day have to defend. Undisclosed observation also poisons the
measurement — a candidate who suspects a hidden camera performs rather than
works. The related question of whether you may infer assistance from those
process artifacts, and how fairly, belongs to the assistance-detection
neighbour; what belongs here is that the candidate was told, in advance, what
exists.

**Make advisories advisory.** Real information about the working conditions — a
long editing task is uncomfortable on a phone, a stable connection helps, a
keyboard is a good idea — should be given as a warning the candidate may
override, never as a gate. A hard device or browser check locks out the candidate
who only has a phone, and that is a wealth filter wearing a usability costume. Say
what is harder and let them decide; they know their circumstances and you do not.

**Treat adjustments as normal, and price them in time.** Publish, in the
invitation and not on request, that adjustments are available and how to ask —
extra time, an alternative format, a live session instead of a written one, a
split across two sittings. Extra time is the workhorse adjustment for a timed
exercise and it should be granted without requiring a diagnosis or a document.
Two disciplines make this real rather than performative: the request must go to
someone who is not scoring the candidate, and the granted adjustment must not be
visible to the assessor as a mark against the work. An accommodation that
appears in the record as "took longer" has become a penalty.

**Never let your infrastructure cost them their work.** A candidate whose
connection drops at minute eighty of a ninety-minute exercise must not lose an
hour of writing. Drafts persist locally and continuously; a reconnect restores
what was there; a submission failure is retried and reported honestly rather than
silently swallowed. This is the strongest form of the rule that a candidate's
process never stalls on your constraints — here the constraint costs them
irreplaceable time, which no apology recovers. Two specifics earn their place
because both are quietly common:

- **Any limit you impose must be stated when it fires.** A silent failure at the
  moment of submission reads, to a person watching a clock, as work lost. A
  message that says what happened and what to do next is the minimum, and where
  the limit was yours rather than theirs, the clock stops.
- **Public assessment surfaces are never rate-limited by network address.**
  Candidates sitting a timed assessment legitimately share one — a university
  lab, a shared office, a household, a carrier-grade translation layer. Keying a
  limit to the address means the second candidate is throttled out of their
  exercise for something the first one did.
- **A shared device must not leak one candidate's work into another's.** Local
  persistence is scoped to the individual invitation, and a completed submission
  clears it. A family computer, a library terminal or a bootcamp lab is a normal
  place to sit an exercise, and the same accessibility argument that forbids a
  device gate makes this a first-class case rather than an edge one.

## Closing honestly is part of the cost

Someone who spent two hours on your exercise is owed a definite outcome and a
timeline, and both should be stated up front — along with an immediate,
visible receipt carrying a reference they can quote, so the moment they close
the tab they hold proof the work arrived. Two failures dominate: the
submission that vanishes into silence, and the closure note that pretends. The
honest closing is a plain statement of what happens next, delivered when you said
it would be, with feedback that is genuinely non-adverse — what the exercise
observed, framed so it is useful to the person and does not read as a judgment on
their worth. Feedback drawn from process artifacts must say only what the record
holds: an exercise that observed three decisions cannot yield a paragraph about
the candidate's character. How the decline itself is worded, and what may be said
about *why*, is the rejection neighbour's subject; what belongs here is that the
obligation is generated by the unpaid hours, and it scales with them.

## Failure modes

- **Estimating the timebox from the author's own run.** Always short. Have
  someone who has never seen the material do it under the clock, and take that
  number.
- **Publishing a cap and then grading on volume.** The rubric leaks the real
  expectation and candidates learn it from each other within weeks.
- **Letting each hiring manager set their own length.** Produces a range from
  ninety minutes to two days across one company, so the same candidate faces a
  different cost depending on which team is hiring, and no comparison across
  roles means anything.
- **Treating an accommodation request as a signal.** It is a fact about a
  process, not about a person, and it must never reach the assessor.
- **Building an exercise on live production material.** Converts an assessment
  into unpaid work and does it invisibly, because nobody made a decision to cross
  the line.
- **Measuring drop-off but not by segment.** A stable overall completion rate
  hides a collapse in one part of the pool, which is the part that matters.
- **Reusing an over-long exercise because it is already built.** Sunk cost is
  paid by candidates, in hours, one at a time.
