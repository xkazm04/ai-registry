# The hard timebox cap

A cap is a single number, decided before the tasks exist, that bounds **total
candidate effort** for an exercise and cannot be exceeded by any level, any
role, or any team. It is the constraint the exercise is designed inside, not an
estimate produced after the design is finished.

The distinction is the whole technique. An *estimate* is descriptive: someone
looks at a finished task list and guesses. It is always low — the author knows
the material, the intended path and the answer, and pays none of the
comprehension tax a candidate meeting the brief cold pays. A *cap* is
prescriptive: it exists before the tasks, so tasks that do not fit are cut
rather than absorbed.

## What the cap covers

Total candidate effort, end to end:

- reading and understanding the brief and any supplied material;
- environment setup, access, tooling, and whatever ritual you impose before the
  work can start;
- the tasks themselves;
- any write-up, decision log, or submission step;
- preparation the candidate reasonably does for a follow-up conversation.

A cap that covers only "the task" is a cap on the part you were already thinking
about and understates the real ask by a wide margin. Setup and comprehension are
unpaid hours like any other.

## Procedure

1. **Set the number first, in policy, per level band, and publish it.** One
   table for the whole organisation. Not per hiring manager, not per role, not
   per requisition — a range from ninety minutes to two days inside one company
   means the same candidate faces a different price depending on which team is
   hiring, and no cross-role comparison survives it.
2. **Bound every row of the table by the same maximum.** A per-level table is
   allowed to vary *below* the cap; it is not allowed to have a row above it. The
   senior row raises depth, not hours — see the sibling technique.
3. **Scope the tasks into the number.** Prefer three or four focused tasks with
   real depth over broad coverage. When the list does not fit, cut a task. Never
   the reverse.
4. **Calibrate against a cold run before first use.** Have someone who has never
   seen the material complete it under the clock and record the true time. Take
   that number over the author's, always. Two cold runs beat one; the variance
   between them is more informative than either mean.
5. **State it to the candidate as a cap, with three sentences.** How long it
   should take; that it is a maximum and finishing early is fine; that work
   beyond it will not improve their assessment. All three are load-bearing — the
   first alone reads as an expectation, and everyone spends the full number.
6. **Design the exercise to remain assessable at partial completion.** A
   candidate who ran out of time at task three must still produce a gradeable
   submission. If the instrument only works when everything is finished, the cap
   is fiction and you have built a completion test.
7. **Instrument the cap after shipping.** Track actual elapsed time and
   invitation-to-submission conversion by level. Systematic overrun means the
   exercise is mis-scoped, not that candidates are slow.

## Decision rules

- **When actual completion times consistently exceed the stated cap, cut the
  exercise — do not raise the number.** The published cap was the promise a
  candidate accepted the invitation on; raising it retroactively converts an
  agreed price into a bait.
- **When a manager asks for more hours, ask what task they would cut instead.**
  Almost always the answer reveals coverage anxiety, which the exercise cannot
  buy off with time anyway.
- **When you exceed the cap deliberately, pay, and record who decided.** An
  exercise above the policy maximum is a decision with a cost imposed on people;
  it carries a name
  ([every-decision-names-its-actor](../../_laws.md#every-decision-names-its-actor)),
  a reason, and an expiry, rather than becoming the new normal by drift.
- **When the cap changes, verdicts recorded under the old cap do not transfer.**
  Candidates who sat a three-hour version and a ninety-minute version did not sit
  the same instrument, and comparing their scores straight across is a comparison
  the record cannot support
  ([a-verdict-is-bound-to-what-it-judged](../../_laws.md#a-verdict-is-bound-to-what-it-judged)).
  Version the exercise and compare within a version.
- **When the material is real and useful to you, the cap question is moot** —
  you are commissioning work and should be paying regardless of length.
- **When a candidate reports it took far longer than stated, log it as an
  instrument observation, not a candidate observation.** Three such reports are
  a defect. They must never surface in the assessment record for those people.

## When not to use it

- **Live, interviewer-present exercises** are already bounded by the scheduled
  session; the cap there is the session length and the technique that matters is
  scoping to fit it.
- **Paid trial engagements** — a genuine short-term contract with compensation
  and a signed scope — are a different instrument with different ethics. They
  need a scope and a rate, not a cap.
- **Portfolio review of work that already exists** costs the candidate no
  production time and needs no cap, only a bound on how much of it you ask them
  to walk you through.

## What the cap does not buy

It does not make an exercise fair on its own. A ninety-minute exercise still
requires a contiguous ninety minutes, a working machine, and a quiet room, and a
cap does nothing about candidates who have none of those — that is what
scheduling flexibility and adjustments are for. And it does not improve a weak
instrument: an exercise with no discriminating power is cheaper to sit and just
as uninformative. The cap bounds the cost; whether the cost buys anything is the
instrument-validation question.
