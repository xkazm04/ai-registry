# Inclusive job advertising

A job advertisement has exactly two ways to fail, and they pull in opposite
directions. It can **shrink the applicant pool** — through language that tells
whole populations, below the level of conscious reading, that the role is not
for them, and through vagueness that gives a cautious reader nothing to act on.
Or it can **promise what the role cannot deliver** — a band nobody approved, a
flexibility the manager will not honour, a culture the team does not have.
Fixing the first failure by inventing detail causes the second. Fixing the
second by removing detail causes the first. The whole craft lives in the narrow
channel between them: **concreteness where it converts, and language that does
not select people out before they apply.**

This subject owns the *advertisement's language*. It does not own the decision
about what the role genuinely requires — that is requirement-inflation control,
which supplies the discipline for deciding what belongs on a must-have list and
the cap that keeps the list short; this subject only enforces that cap as a
lint against the finished text. It does not own where the pay figure comes
from — compensation banding and market honesty owns the derivation of a
defensible band. It does not own outbound campaign copy sent to a person who
did not apply — sourcing-campaign honesty owns that, and its rule (only stated
facts may appear; a defaulted or assumed value is simply absent) is the same
rule as this subject's stated-fact test, applied on the other side of the
seam. What this subject owns is the moment where a real role, a real band and a
real requirement list are turned into sentences that a stranger reads in about
eleven seconds and decides, largely pre-verbally, whether to act on.

## The advertisement is a conversion surface, not a specification

The most common category error in job advertising is writing the posting as an
internal document that has escaped. The requisition, the scorecard and the
interview plan are specifications: they exist to be complete and unambiguous
for people who are obliged to read them. The advertisement is read voluntarily,
by someone who owes you nothing, on a phone, in a queue of ten other
advertisements. It is a marketing surface governed by hiring law.

Two consequences follow, and almost every rule in this subject descends from
one of them:

- **Every sentence that does not carry information is spending attention it
  did not earn.** Filler is not neutral. It occupies the slot where the reader
  would otherwise have learned something, and — worse — it is *diagnostic*. A
  reader who has been through a few processes has learned what a posting made
  of filler predicts about the employer behind it.
- **The reader is self-selecting continuously, and asymmetrically.** People do
  not read to the end and then decide. They accumulate small signals and drop
  out. The signals that cause drop-out are unevenly distributed across
  populations — which is what makes this a fairness subject and not a style
  guide. A phrase that costs you five percent of one population's applications
  and none of another's has changed the shape of your pipeline before a single
  screening rule has run.

## Boilerplate is not filler — it is a red flag

The load-bearing distinction of this subject: **"competitive salary" and
"dynamic environment" are not weak writing that a good editor would tighten.
They are negative information.** A reader decodes them, correctly and almost
instantly:

- *competitive salary* — we will not tell you the number, which means the
  number is not good, or means we intend to price you individually based on
  what you will accept.
- *dynamic / fast-paced environment* — the work is unplanned and the hours are
  not the stated hours.
- *work hard, play hard*, *like a family*, *wear many hats* — respectively:
  long hours, boundaries will be treated as disloyalty, and the role is
  underscoped and you will absorb the gap.
- *rockstar*, *ninja*, *guru* — the employer's self-image is the subject of
  the advertisement, not the job.

Treating these as cosmetic produces the standard, useless intervention: a
rewrite pass that swaps one euphemism for a fresher one. The correct
intervention is that **each of these phrases has a concrete fact it is
standing in front of**, and the fix is to replace the phrase with the fact or
delete it. "Competitive salary" is fixed by a number, never by "attractive
compensation". If the number cannot be stated, the phrase must be *deleted*,
not softened — silence is honest, and the empty space is at least not a signal.
That is the same asymmetry as *say only what the record holds*: a posting says
what is actually decided, in the words the record holds, or says nothing.

## Coded language operates below the level of intent

Nobody writes "we prefer men" or "we prefer people under thirty-five". They
write **aggressive**, **competitive**, **dominant**, **ninja** — masculine-coded
adjectives that, in repeated study, measurably reduce the number of women who
report that a role appeals to them and that they belong in it, without changing
how appealing men find it. And they write **digital native**, **recent
graduate**, **young dynamic team**, **energetic** — phrases whose literal
content is empty and whose received content is an age band.

Three principles govern the response:

- **Intent is irrelevant to the effect, and irrelevant to the exposure.**
  Whether the writer meant to exclude does not change the deterrent effect on
  the reader, and in most jurisdictions does not change whether the wording is
  a discriminatory advertisement. A defence built on intent is not a defence.
- **The offending words are usually load-bearing for the writer.** The manager
  who wrote "we need someone aggressive" was trying to say something true about
  the job. The fix is not deletion but *translation into the behaviour*:
  "negotiates contested priorities with senior stakeholders and holds the
  line". The behavioural version is more informative, harder to fake, and
  gender-neutral by construction. A pure removal pass loses information and
  the manager will re-add the word.
- **Some of it is grammar, not attitude.** In grammatically gendered
  languages a masculine job title is not a preference, it is the unmarked
  form; whereas an explicit gendered pair or a gendered adjective *is* a
  choice. A check that cannot tell these apart will either scream at every
  posting in half the world's languages or say nothing useful in any of them.

## Vagueness and dishonesty are the same failure viewed from two sides

The two facts a reader most wants — **what it pays** and **where it is done** —
are also the two facts most often replaced by a gesture. "Competitive salary"
gestures at pay. "Flexible, hybrid-friendly, remote-first" gestures at place.
Neither is a disclosure, and the reader knows it.

The test that separates a disclosure from a gesture is **concreteness**: a pay
statement must contain a figure or a range with a currency and a period; a
place statement must contain a named location, or an explicit remote statement
with its boundary (which country, which timezone band, how many days on site
and where). Anything else is a phrase about pay, not a pay figure.

This test has a second, harder edge that is easy to miss: **the concreteness
requirement is not a licence to invent.** If the band has not been approved,
the posting does not state a band. It does not state an aspirational one, and
it does not state a wide one to be safe — a range so wide it contains every
plausible answer is a gesture wearing a number's clothes, and it teaches the
reader that your numbers are not real. The correct output of a posting whose
band is undecided is a *blocked posting*, escalated to the person who can
decide, not a posting with a euphemism in the pay slot. Vagueness and
overpromise are not opposites to be balanced; they are two ways of not
knowing the fact, and the remedy for both is to go and find the fact.

## A long list of requirements is an exclusion mechanism

The requirement list is part of the advertisement's language even though its
*content* belongs to another discipline. The mechanism is well attested and
asymmetric: readers from under-represented groups tend to treat a must-have
list as a checklist to be satisfied completely, and self-select out on the
first item they do not meet, while readers from over-represented groups treat
the same list as an aspiration and apply at a lower match rate. So the length
of the list is itself a filter — one that runs before your filters, on a
population you never see, in a direction you did not choose.

The practical rule: **count the must-haves and cap them.** Roughly five is the
working ceiling; beyond that the list is describing a wish, and each additional
conjunctive item narrows the pool multiplicatively while the writer reasons
about it additively. This subject does not re-teach how to decide which items
survive the cap — the sibling discipline of requirement-inflation control owns
the outcome filter and the forced ranking that does that work. What belongs
here is the *count as a property of the finished text*: a lint that reads the
rendered advertisement, counts the imperative requirement markers, and refuses
to call the posting ready above the ceiling, regardless of how defensible each
line was individually.

Counting markers in prose is a proxy, and it is worth being honest about which
way it errs. It over-counts a posting that repeats "required" in one sentence
and under-counts one that states ten requirements as bare bullets with no
marker word at all. So the marker count sets the *advisory* threshold — which
must therefore sit above the true cap, or it fires on compliant postings — while
the real ceiling is enforced on the structured requirement list, where the items
are countable. A team that owns only the prose has a weaker instrument and
should say so rather than quietly raising the number until the warning stops.

## The check must be instant, deterministic and free

An advertisement is written by iterating. The writer types, reads it back,
cuts, retypes. A quality check that participates in that loop must run **on
every keystroke** — which fixes its architecture completely:

- **Rules, not a model.** A language model can write better copy than a rule
  can, but it cannot be consulted forty times a minute at zero cost with the
  same answer every time. The lint is a deterministic phrase-and-pattern check
  precisely so that it is instant, free, and stable — the same text always
  produces the same findings, so the writer can learn the rules instead of
  negotiating with an oracle. Model assistance belongs to *drafting* and to
  *rewrite suggestions*, never to the always-on check.
- **Advisory, never blocking.** The lint's findings are advice to a human
  author who may have a reason the rules do not know. A hard block on a phrase
  list produces evasion — the writer rephrases past the rule and the posting
  gets worse — and it is also simply wrong often enough to destroy trust in
  every other finding. The one thing that may legitimately gate publication is
  a *stated fact* that policy requires and that is measurably absent, and that
  is a policy gate, not a lint.
- **Silent on an empty draft.** A check that fires on a three-word stub is
  nagging, not helping, and a writer who learns to ignore the panel in the
  first minute will ignore it in the tenth. Findings are gated on the draft
  having enough substance to be judged.
- **One source for each fact.** Every surface that asks "does this role have a
  stated salary?" — the lint, the publication gate, the campaign copy, the
  candidate-facing card — must ask the same predicate. Two implementations of
  the same question will disagree, and the disagreement always surfaces as a
  posting that one screen calls complete and another nags about, which trains
  the writer to distrust both.

## Machine-drafted postings inherit every rule, plus one

When a posting is generated or seeded from a template, three additional rules
apply, and skipping them is the most common way a well-linted product ships
bad advertisements anyway.

**Generated text is subject to the same lint as typed text.** Any seeded
filler, sample posting or template default must itself pass the check. A
demonstration posting containing "competitive salary" teaches every user that
the phrase is acceptable house style, and does it more effectively than the
lint teaches the opposite. The demand is stronger than "avoid the flagged
phrases": the filler must *pass every check*, including the positive ones — so
each language's sample text has to carry a real work-mode or location word, or
the seeded posting fails the place test the moment a user opens it, and the
first thing the product teaches is that its own template is defective.

**A generator may not promote an unstated fact.** A model asked to write an
attractive posting will supply the missing band, the missing benefits and the
missing flexibility, because that is what attractive postings contain. Every
value in the output must trace to a stated input; a defaulted or assumed value
is absent from the copy, not rendered as though the record held it. The
sibling subject that owns outbound campaign copy states this same boundary for
messages, and the two must not drift apart.

**Structure follows the document's language, not the reader's.** A posting
written in one language does not get section headings in another because the
person viewing the editor has a different interface language. Headings the
author wrote are the author's text and are never machine-translated on
render — a posting is a legal advertisement, and silently restating its
scaffolding in another language changes what was advertised. *Meaning does not
live in a label*: the structure is keyed off stable roles, and the words are
composed in the document's own language at render time.

## Failure modes of the naive reading

- **Treating the lint as a style guide.** The output is a rewrite pass that
  makes the prose smoother and changes nothing about who applies. The findings
  that matter are the ones that name a missing *fact*, not the ones that name
  an ugly word.
- **Blocking on the word list.** Produces evasion, produces resentment, and
  produces postings that are worse than before because the writer optimized
  against the checker.
- **Building the check for one language.** A phrase list in the interface
  language, applied to postings written in another, reports zero findings and
  is read as a clean bill of health. A silent check is more dangerous than no
  check, because it is trusted.
- **Substring matching without word boundaries and without diacritics.** The
  two mechanical failures that make a multilingual lint quietly wrong: matching
  a stem inside an unrelated word, and a naive letter class that stops at the
  first accented character so an inflected form never matches. Both fail in
  the safe-looking direction — no finding — which is why they survive review.
- **Confusing "we removed the coded words" with "we widened the pool".** The
  coded-language pass is necessary and small. The concrete facts — pay, place,
  a short honest requirement list, what the first ninety days actually contain
  — are what move application rates. A posting that is scrupulously neutral and
  says nothing converts no better than one that is neither.
- **Auditing the posting and never the outcome.** The advertisement's language
  is measurable at the funnel: view-to-apply rate by source and, where the
  data supports it, the demographic shape of the applicant pool against the
  qualified population. A change that reads better and converts identically
  taught you nothing; a posting nobody flagged that halves one group's
  application rate is the failure this whole subject exists to catch.
