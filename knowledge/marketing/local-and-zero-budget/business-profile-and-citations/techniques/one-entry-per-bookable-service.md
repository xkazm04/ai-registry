# One entry per bookable service

A profile's services, products and categories are lists of jobs the business will
turn up and do. The test for an entry is not its wording, it is the job behind it: can
a customer book this today, and would the business actually deliver it? One job gets
one entry, in its strongest phrasing. Nothing in the interface prevents five phrasings
of the same job, or a service the business has never performed, and both are the
stuffing pattern that the policy judge flags and the ranking judge does not reward.

## Two tests, in order

**The delivery test.** Before an entry goes on any list: would the business take this
job if it were booked tomorrow? A no cuts the entry, whatever its search volume. A
category or service the business cannot fulfil is a lie on the engine's own property,
it is the documented quality-review trigger, and it risks the whole profile - every
review and every ranking - at once. The same test applies to every service-area
locality: each city on the list is a claim that the business will drive there.

**The duplicate test.** For the entries that pass delivery, group by job. "Drain
cleaning", "drain cleaning service" and "cleaning of drains" is one job in three
costumes - keep the strongest phrasing, drop the rest. "Drain cleaning", "emergency
drain unblocking" and "sewer line cleaning" is three jobs with different urgency,
price and customer - three entries, correctly. Demand decides which phrasing survives
and what order the list runs in; it never decides how many entries exist.

## Procedure

1. Pull every service from the business's own website, then from the top three
   map-pack rivals' menus to find gaps the business could genuinely fill.
2. Run the delivery test per entry, with the owner where the answer is not obvious.
   Record the cut entries and the reason.
3. Sweep the platform's predefined list for the primary category and take every one
   the business delivers; then add custom services with descriptions.
4. Run the duplicate test over the merged list. Report what was merged and why, so the
   owner can see the decision rather than a shorter list.
5. Order by measured demand, highest first. The city stays out of every name.
6. Products: each named thing with a price and a destination page is one product;
   a service business's products are its fixed-price jobs. The same two tests apply.
7. Say the warning out loud in the delivered list: these extras are options, not
   requirements; do not add a service you do not perform; do not list one job twice
   to catch more keywords.

## Decision rules

- When a rival's menu carries a service with real demand that the business does not
  perform, it goes in a "could offer" note for the owner and not on the profile,
  because the profile is a record of delivery, not of ambition.
- When two phrasings of one job are close in volume, the higher one is the name and
  the other goes once in the description, because the description is free text and
  the name is a slot.
- When the honest list is shorter than the working target, deliver the short list and
  say so, because a list padded toward a number is a list of undelivered or duplicated
  jobs by construction.
- When generating for an owner, produce more options than slots and mark the overflow
  as extras with a "use this if" each, but hold every extra to the delivery test, because
  oversupply is permission to choose, never permission to stuff.

## When NOT to use

- Categories are picked from the platform's closed list and cannot be duplicated by
  wording; the delivery test applies to them, the duplicate test does not.
- The website's service pages follow their own architecture (hub and spoke, one page
  per rankable term) and may legitimately hold more pages than the profile holds
  entries; this rule governs the profile list, not the site map.
- A marketplace or an online shop listing a catalogue of goods is a different surface
  with its own feed rules; the product-feed subject owns that.
