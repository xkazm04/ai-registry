# The buyer, the do-it-yourselfer and the peer

Volume says how many people typed a phrase. It never says who. Three different
people type the same words, and only one of them can be converted by a money
page, so every root keyword of a money page is asked *who is actually typing
this* before the volume number is allowed to matter.

- **The buyer** wants to hire someone or buy something. The only one a money
  page converts.
- **The do-it-yourselfer** wants to do it alone. Reads the page, never calls.
  Belongs to an article, and the article is worth writing.
- **The peer** is a competitor, an agency, a student researching the niche.
  Worth nothing, ever, and often a large share of a high-volume term.

The test is a judgement, informed by the results page and by the phrase's
grammar, and it is written on the block as a judgement.

## The trap the test exists for

`[service] for [audience]`: "bookkeeping for restaurants", "marketing for
dentists", "search optimisation for contractors". The audience is correct, which
is why it feels like a buyer term. But that phrasing is how a guide is titled,
so the results fill with how-to content, and the people typing it are mostly
the second and third kinds - the restaurant owner reading up before deciding
whether to hire at all, and the agency working out how to sell to restaurants.
High volume, few buyers. It is the single phrase family where the audience word
fools both a modifier list and a hurried human.

The buying signal is not the audience. It is a noun of hiring - services,
company, agency, consultant, firm, contractor - or a place - near me, in a
city - or a verb of engagement - hire, book, quote, emergency, same day. Price,
cost, quote and packages are buying-adjacent and split by industry; they are
verified on the page, never assumed.

## Procedure

1. **Say the phrase in the searcher's mouth.** "I need bookkeeping for my
   restaurant" is a buyer; "bookkeeping for restaurants" as typed is a
   heading. If the phrase reads like a title, most of its searchers are
   readers.
2. **Look for the buying noun.** A hiring noun, a place or an engagement verb
   marks a buyer. Its absence in a `[service] for [audience]` phrase marks a
   guide term.
3. **Read the results page for the same signal.** A page full of guides and
   forum threads confirms readers; a page of service pages and ads confirms
   buyers. The count decides the intent; this test decides which of the
   transactional-looking candidates is actually typed by buyers.
4. **Compare the candidates by who types them, then by volume.** A
   "services"-noun term at a third of the volume of a guide-shaped term is
   the better root for a money page, because a page ranking first for the
   guide earns readers and a page ranking first for the services term earns
   calls.
5. **Write the reason when the smaller term wins**, in one clause on the
   block. A smaller root without a stated reason reads as a mistake; with the
   reason it reads as the decision it was.
6. **Route the guide-shaped term** to an article that links to the money page.
   It is the top of the funnel, not a cut.

## Decision rules

- When a root's phrasing is `[service] for [audience]` and carries no buying
  noun, treat it as an article term regardless of volume, because the grammar
  is that of a guide title and the results page will confirm it.
- When two candidate roots pass the intent count and one carries a buying
  noun, choose the buying-noun term as the money page's root even at lower
  volume, because ranking earns what the searcher came for and buyers came to
  buy.
- When a chosen root has lower volume than one of its own secondaries, the
  block states why, because that is the easiest error to spot on review and
  it is only an error when the reason is missing.
- When price, cost or quote appear in a candidate, verify on the page rather
  than trusting the noun, because those words split by industry and are the
  most misread in the craft.

## When NOT to use

- On a query whose results page already counts informational. The test picks
  among transactional-looking candidates; it does not overturn a count.
- On comparison queries. "Best bookkeeper for restaurants" is typed by a buyer
  choosing, and it is a comparison verdict, not a `[service] for [audience]`
  trap.
- On local queries. A place is itself the buying signal, and the peer share of
  a near-me term is negligible.
- To justify cutting a term. The test decides which term roots the money page;
  the losers are routed to linking content, never removed.
