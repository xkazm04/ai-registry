# The answer block under a question heading

Every heading phrased as a question is followed directly by a short,
self-contained answer - by convention forty to sixty words - that could be
lifted verbatim into a featured snippet, an AI answer or a chat citation and
still make sense. The deep explanation comes after it, never before it. The
answer restates its subject by name, carries no pronoun that points outside the
block, and contains the one number or fact the section exists to deliver.

This is the same edit that serves the human reader: decades of reading research
show people want the payoff first and scan for it, and a passage retriever
wants exactly the same shape. The two readers do not conflict; padding and
buried answers fail both.

## Procedure

1. **Write the heading as the question a searcher actually types**, not a
   clever label. The questions come from the results page's "people also ask"
   feature, the business's own inbound questions and the money-page FAQ, in
   that order of evidence.
2. **Answer it in one block immediately beneath.** Convention: forty to sixty
   words. Test by reading the block alone, with the heading, on a blank page.
   If it needs the previous section, a pronoun's referent or the page title to
   mean anything, it fails.
3. **Name the subject in the first sentence.** "The company's revenue grew 3%"
   is useless once retrieved without its page; "The business's revenue grew 3% in
   2025" survives. An orphan pronoun at the top of a section is the single
   most common reason a well-ranked page is not lifted.
4. **Put the number in the block, with its unit and date, if the business has
   one.** "From CZK 4,800 in 2026" can be quoted; "affordable recently" cannot.
   If the business has not supplied the number, the block says what it can say
   and the missing figure becomes a question to the owner - never a plausible
   value.
5. **Then expand.** The reasoning, the caveats, the humour and the voice live
   below the block. Nothing above it is spent on warm-up.
6. **Write questions-and-answers blocks as complete sentences.** A business
   profile's live answering feature and an answer engine extract an answer
   rather than displaying a page, so a bullet fragment with no subject is
   unusable to them. One well-written sentence serves the listing feature, the
   answer box and the page itself; there is no separate "FAQ voice".

## Decision rules

- When a section answers more than one question, split it, because
  retrievers evaluate chunks independently and a chunk that answers two things
  is scored as answering neither well.
- When the honest answer is longer than the convention allows - a genuine
  "it depends" - give the short conditional answer in the block ("Usually X;
  Y when Z") and the branches below, because a block that refuses to answer is
  skipped and a block that overruns is truncated at whatever boundary the
  retriever chose.
- When the page is a money page rather than an article, keep the answer block
  straight and put the call to action beside it, not inside it, because the
  extractable zone carries the credibility and the sale is not what the engine
  is quoting.
- When a page ranks under an AI answer and is not cited, rewrite the opening
  blocks before touching anything else, because ranking shows the engine already
  trusts the page and the missing piece is almost always liftability.

## What is convention here

The forty-to-sixty-word length is practitioner convention, chosen because it
matches the length of what the featured-snippet feature has historically shown
and what answer boxes quote; no study fixes it. The eighteen-word average
sentence quoted in citation studies is a correlation on cited pages, not a
rule. "First hundred words state the topic and the answer" is convention with
the same reading-research backing as the block itself. The claim that a large
share of citations come from the first third of a document is a vendor
measurement on an unstated sample; the direction (answer first) is safe, the
fraction is not.

## When NOT to use

- On headings that are not questions. A section titled "Pricing" wants a
  table, not a forty-word paragraph; forcing the block shape onto every heading
  produces the padded, repetitive pages the convention exists to prevent.
- As a licence to state a number the business has not supplied. The lever is
  real - statistics and quotations were the strongest arm in the controlled
  experiment that started this field - and it is exactly the lever most likely
  to tempt a generator into fabrication. A block with no number outranks a
  block with an invented one on every surface that matters, including the
  owner's lawyer.
- As a substitute for the page's own argument. A page that is only answer
  blocks is a glossary; the blocks open sections, they do not replace them.
