# Content brief and article composition

This subject owns two things and the join between them: **what a brief must carry** so
that a writer or a model can produce a publishable search-driven article without
inventing the missing half, and **the composition of the article itself** - where the
answer sits, how the body is paced for a reader who will not read most of it, how many
blocks of each kind a post can hold, where the call to action that actually converts
lives, and where humour earns its keep and where it spends credibility the page cannot
afford.

It does not own what feeds the brief or what wraps the article. Which keyword the
article targets and whether it deserves a post at all belongs to
`search-intent-classification` and `keyword-metric-reliability`. Title-tag and
meta-description lengths, pixel truncation and heading tags belong to
`on-page-and-metadata-craft`. The passage-level craft that gets a section quoted by an
AI answer engine belongs to `answer-engine-visibility`; this subject only insists that
the answer goes first, which serves both readers. Where the brief's facts come from
and how a generator is stopped from fabricating them is `grounded-marketing-generation`;
what counts as proof and how missing proof is handled is
`honest-proof-and-illustrative-data`; the register the article speaks in is
`brand-voice-capture`; the hub the post links up to and the spokes it links across is
`site-architecture-and-topic-clusters`; and the money page the mid-body link points at
is `money-page-conversion-craft`.

## The reader you are actually writing for

Every decision in this subject follows from four measurements a principal practitioner
carries in their head, all from published reading-behaviour research rather than
convention. A usability research group's eye-tracking work puts the share of words a
visitor reads on an average page at **20-28%**, with each additional hundred words
buying about four seconds of attention - roughly eighteen more words read. A content
analytics vendor's dataset of two billion visits found **55% of pageviews get under
fifteen seconds** of attention, that departure risk peaks in the first ten seconds and
flattens near thirty, and that a reader who survives those thirty seconds is likely to
stay two minutes or more. Median scroll depth sits near **50%**, so the bottom half of
any post is read by a minority - and yet **66% of engaged time happens below the fold**,
because the minority that scrolls is the minority that cares. And the same research
group rewrote one page three ways and measured usability: concise +58%, scannable +47%,
objective rather than promotional +27%, all three together **+124%**.

Hold those four together and the shape of a good article is no longer a matter of
taste. The opening is worth more than the rest of the post combined, because it decides
whether the thirty-second cliff is survived. The middle must be paced for a scanner,
not a reader. The most valuable secondary content goes at the midpoint, not the end.
Whatever sits at the end is read by the highest-intent minority and should treat them
as such. And "write more" is never the answer to "they are not reading"; adding words
adds seconds, not comprehension.

## What the brief is for

A brief is the contract that lets a second party - a junior writer, a freelancer, a
model - produce the article without a second research pass and without inventing what
the research should have supplied. The test of a brief is not whether it is thorough
but whether the draft that comes back needs the brief's author to fix its facts. A
brief that carries the target term but not the questions people actually ask, or the
outline but not the internal links the post must place, or the keyword list but not the
proof the business owns, produces a draft that is plausible everywhere and publishable
nowhere. The technique on what the brief carries names the fields; the golden-path
principle behind them is that **every slot the writer would otherwise fill by guessing
is a slot the brief fills by research or leaves explicitly empty**. A brief with a
labelled empty proof slot is honest; a brief that lets the draft supply "over 500 happy
customers" is how fabricated proof reaches a live page.

The brief also fixes the article's angle before a word is drafted. The cheap version of
this is a keyword plus an outline; the principal version is a research dossier that
records the numbers sources agree on, **the places where sources disagree** - because
resolving a disagreement is the one thing a post can do that the ranking pages have
not - the ten questions real people asked in their own words, and the angles where the
business can win rather than match. A brief that names its buyer in one sentence (who
they are, what they fear, what they typed) produces a draft aimed at a person; a brief
that names an audience segment produces a draft aimed at nobody.

## The composition, top to bottom

**The answer goes first.** Directly under the headline, before any framing, a
self-contained forty-to-sixty-word block that fully answers the question the searcher
typed, key number included. It must work alone, because roughly one reader in ten
never scrolls at all, and it must be liftable verbatim, because that is what an answer
box and an answer engine take. The inverted pyramid was tested by the same usability
group decades ago: readers found the main point immediately and the rewritten pages
used half the words. Building suspense before the answer in search-driven content is a
craft error, not a stylistic choice.

**Then the proof line.** One or two sentences on why this author is worth listening
to, drawn from proof the business actually supplied. When the proof is thin, the line
is written from what is true and strong and says nothing about the rest; a disclaimer
that lists what the business lacks is the least persuasive sentence a page can open
with, and the honesty rule has never required it. Never claim a result you do not
have; never announce the absence either.

**Then the structure that carries a scanner.** A subhead every 150-300 words - the
pattern is measured, the interval is convention - with the information-carrying word
first, so that the subhead stack read alone tells the whole story. Sentences averaging
14-17 words with a rewrite past 25, because comprehension is measured near full at
eight words, around ninety percent at fourteen, and collapses past twenty-one.
Paragraphs of one idea and two to four sentences, idea first, because a second idea
inside a paragraph is skipped by a scanner who only reads opening lines. Something to
look at - a table, a figure, a form, a numbered list - within every few hundred words,
because a wall of paragraphs is the layout failure that ships with every required
section present. A table near the top wherever the topic has numbers.

**Then the budget.** An article is a sequence of typed blocks, and a generator or a
writer given no budget produces either a listicle of fragments or a wall. The
composition rule is a block budget: one callout, one closing call to action, at most
one figure placeholder described by what it should show rather than by a made-up
source, and a total in the mid-teens for a standard post. Exactly-one rules are
easier to check and easier to obey than "not too many".

**Then the real call to action, which is not at the end.** One marketing publisher's
study of its own blog, tracking unique URLs across its best lead-generating posts,
found in-body anchor-text links driving 47-93% of each post's leads and the end-of-post
banner driving 6%. That is a single-site self-study and is labelled as such, but its
mechanism is general: the mid-body link sits where the reader is, looks like a
resource rather than an advertisement, and is reached by the scanning majority who
never see the footer. So the post carries a descriptive anchor-text link to the money
page inside the paragraph where the reader's need peaks - and still closes hard,
because whoever reached the end is the highest-intent reader on the page.

**Then the FAQ and the bridge.** Four to six questions phrased the way they were
searched, answered in two or three straight sentences each, and a two-to-three
sentence bridge to the service page plus the link up to the hub. A post with nowhere
to convert is a cost centre.

## Humour, priced honestly

A meta-analysis of 369 correlations on humour in advertising (Eisend, 2009) found a
large effect on attention and positive affect - a correlation near 0.42 - roughly
twice the effect on attitude toward the content as on attitude toward the business,
and a **significant reduction in perceived source credibility**. That is a real trade,
not a free win, and the transfer from advertising to editorial prose is an inference
rather than a measurement, so it is labelled as one. The composition rule that follows
is a zoning rule rather than a volume rule: the answer block, the proof line, prices,
tables, FAQ answers and the call-to-action line stay straight, because they are
carrying the credibility that the humour spends and because they are the passages
that get lifted verbatim; the prose between them may be as funny as the brand's voice
allows, because that is where retention is won and nothing there is quoted. A
publisher who wants a joke every second sentence can have one - in the body, never in
the zones. The related myth to refuse: what drives sharing is arousal - awe, anger,
anxiety - not pleasantness, so a calm, useful, well-structured post is the
worst-sharing profile there is, and the article's structure does not fix that.

## Where the rules live, and why a prompt is not a gate

The failure mode of this subject in generated content is not that the rules are
unknown but that they are asserted in a prompt and enforced nowhere. A brief prompt
that says "five to seven sections, four questions, eight related terms, four link
anchors" and a normaliser that accepts anything up to eight of each is a prompt with a
ceiling and no floor. An article prompt that says "exactly one callout, exactly one
call to action, at most sixteen blocks" and a validator that only checks the body is
non-empty, every figure has a description and the FAQ exists is a composition rule with
no gate. The practitioner's rule: **every composition rule that a draft can violate is
either checked by something that runs, or it is a wish.** A page-rhythm check that
counts consecutive text-only blocks, the longest stretch with nothing to look at and
paragraphs over four sentences catches the wall of text that ships with every required
section present. A block-count assertion catches the second callout. A count of
in-body links to the money page catches the post whose only call to action is the
banner. None of these needs a model; all of them need to exist.

## Failure modes of the naive reading

- **Longer is better.** Length correlates with position at roughly zero in the
  published datasets; what long content earns is links, and the links do the work.
  Length is a marker of investment, not a cause of ranking or reading.
- **Dwell time is a ranking factor you can optimise.** Denied by the dominant engine
  repeatedly; what exists is query-level click aggregation nobody can target. Make the
  page satisfy the click and stop chasing the metric.
- **The banner at the bottom is the call to action.** It is the call to action for
  the minority who scroll to the end. The mid-body link is the one most readers see.
- **A featured-snippet answer and a human opening are in conflict.** They are the same
  block. What kills citation is orphan pronouns and sections that only make sense in
  sequence, not personality.
- **The eight-second attention span.** Traced to a report that never contained the
  number. Never repeat it; the real figures above are worse and better at once.
- **"In conclusion".** Tells the reader nothing new follows and throws away the last
  hundred words, which the peak-end effect weights disproportionately. Close with a
  reframe, one next action, or an open question.
- **Bulleting prose.** Lists beat paragraphs for genuinely parallel items - about 70%
  of a list is examined versus 55% of paragraph text - and fragment an argument when
  the items are not parallel.
