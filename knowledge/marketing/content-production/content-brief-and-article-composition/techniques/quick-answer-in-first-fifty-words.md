# The quick answer in the first fifty words

Directly under the headline, before any framing, sits a self-contained block of
forty to sixty words that fully answers the question the searcher typed, with the key
number in it. It is the first thing read, the only thing read by the tenth of visitors
who never scroll, the passage an answer box takes, and the passage an answer engine
quotes. Everything else in the post is for the reader who survived it.

## Why the number is what it is

The band is not arbitrary but it is not a law either. Published eye-tracking research
puts the first ten seconds as the peak departure risk and the thirty-second mark as
the point past which a reader is likely to stay minutes; forty to sixty words is what
a scanner takes in inside that window. Paragraph-shaped answer boxes on the results
page cluster in the same band. And a block that length fits one screen unit on a
phone. Three independent reasons converge on one size, which is as close to a measured
threshold as this domain gets; the exact edges are convention and are labelled so.

## Procedure

1. **Write the answer as if it were the whole page.** One reader in ten will treat it
   that way. If the question is "how much does X cost", the block contains the range,
   the typical figure and the one thing that moves it. If the question is "how do I",
   it contains the method in one breath.
2. **Put the key number in.** A block without a number is an opinion; a block with one
   is a fact the reader can act on, and a fact is what gets quoted. The number comes
   from the brief's proof inventory or its research; if neither supplied one, the block
   says what is known and never fabricates a figure to look complete.
3. **Re-state the subject by name.** "It costs" is useless once the block is lifted
   from the page; "A drain clean costs" survives on its own. No pronoun refers to
   anything outside the block.
4. **Delete the first paragraph, then check whether the second was the real opening.**
   Throat-clearing is a warm-up the writer needed and the reader does not. "In today's
   world", "in the fast-paced landscape", and a definition of the term the reader just
   searched are all banned openings; promotional language measured worst on nearly
   every usability metric in the rewrite studies.
5. **Keep it straight.** No joke, no bit, no aside. This block is carrying the page's
   credibility and is the passage most likely to be quoted verbatim by a machine; it is
   a proof zone.
6. **Then open a specific gap.** After the answer, one sentence that names something
   known, salient and closable ("one of the three fees below is usually waived if you
   ask") gives the reader a reason to continue. A vague gap ("the results may surprise
   you") creates nothing.

## Decision rules

- **When the answer cannot be given in sixty words, the page is targeting more than
  one question; split the term or narrow the page, because a block that hedges is
  neither read nor quoted.**
- **When the key number is unknown, state the range you can defend and its source
  class, never a plausible point figure, because the answer block is the single most
  copied sentence on the page and a fabricated number there propagates.**
- **When a section deeper in the post answers its own subhead's question, open that
  section the same way - a forty-to-sixty word self-contained answer, then depth -
  because retrieval works on passages, not pages, and every section must survive
  being lifted alone.**
- **When a writer argues the answer-first opening spoils the piece, point at the
  thirty-second cliff: suspense before the answer is paid for in readers who never
  reach the reveal.**

## What it is not

It is not a summary of the article. A summary tells the reader what they are about to
read; the quick answer tells them what they came to find out, and the article then
earns its length by adding the reasoning, the evidence and the story. It is also not
the meta description repeated; the meta description was written to win a click from
the results page and the quick answer is written for the reader who already clicked.
The two may share a number and should not share a sentence.

## When NOT to use

- **A money page.** The hero of a service page carries a headline, one action, a proof
  line and a short answer, but the answer there serves a buyer deciding whether to
  call, not a searcher deciding whether to read; its shape is the conversion subject's.
- **A narrative or opinion piece the business publishes for reach rather than search.**
  A story may open in a scene; a search-driven article may not. The distinction is
  whether the visitor arrived with a question typed into a box.
- **A pillar page that routes to spokes.** Its opening is a short orientation and a
  section per spoke; an answer block there would answer the head term the spokes own.
