# The brief carries keyword, outline, FAQ and links

A brief is complete when the person or model drafting from it cannot be blamed for
inventing anything. Every slot a writer would otherwise fill by guessing is either
filled by research in the brief or left explicitly empty, so the draft's gaps are
visible rather than plausible. The four fields the technique is named for are the
minimum that a search-driven article needs; the fields around them are what keeps the
draft honest.

## What a complete brief carries

1. **The primary term, verbatim, and the three places it must appear.** The exact
   phrase the searcher typed - not a synonym, not a nicer phrasing - and the rule that
   it appears once each in the title, the meta description and the first section
   heading. Once each; repetition past that is stuffing and the brief says so.
2. **The outline: five to seven sections, each with the points it must cover.** Each
   section heading phrased as the question people search, so the subhead stack reads
   as a summary on its own. The range is convention; the point-per-section requirement
   is what stops a model returning headings with nothing under them.
3. **The FAQ: four to six real questions in the words people used.** Real means taken
   from the results page's question box, from forums, from customer messages - never
   composed by the brief's author to sound like questions. A generator asked for
   "frequently asked questions" with no source will produce fluent questions nobody
   asked. Convention on the count.
4. **Internal links: the hub the post links up to and the anchors it places.** Four
   descriptive anchor texts is a convention; that the post links to the money page
   from inside the body, and to its hub, is not optional - a post without a place to
   convert is a cost.
5. **Related terms: six to twelve, ranked by opportunity, negatives excluded.** Volume
   and competition beside each so the writer weights them; terms the business wants
   out of its traffic never reach the brief.
6. **The buyer in one sentence.** Who they are, what they fear, what they typed. An
   audience segment ("SMB owners") aims the draft at nobody.
7. **The proof inventory, or its labelled absence.** Every number, credential, review
   and result the business supplied, with source. Where nothing was supplied, the slot
   says so; the draft is then forbidden to fill it.
8. **The angle, from a dossier when the post matters.** The numbers sources agree on,
   the places they disagree, and where this business can win rather than match.
   Resolving a disagreement the ranking pages left open is the post's information
   gain; a brief that lacks one produces a draft that matches the results page and
   adds nothing.
9. **The type of content and the target length.** Target length is the average of the
   top ranking pages plus or minus a fifth, plus the gaps; never a round number the
   author likes.

## Decision rules

- **When a slot has no researched value, leave it labelled empty rather than
  plausible, because a generator fills any blank with the likeliest text and the
  likeliest proof is a fabrication.** "Proof: none supplied - ask the owner" is a
  complete field.
- **When the FAQ questions were not observed somewhere real, drop them rather than
  invent them, because a fluent unasked question earns no results-page feature and
  trains the writer that the FAQ is filler.**
- **When the brief names a keyword but not the results page's verdict on its intent,
  send the brief back, because the outline for an informational term and a
  transactional term are different documents and the intent is read off the page,
  never off the modifier.**
- **When a brief prompt states counts ("5-7 sections, 4 questions, 8 terms"), check
  that something downstream enforces the floor, because a normaliser that only caps
  at eight accepts a one-section outline unchallenged.**
- **When a grounding block of what "provably worked" is added to a brief, mark it as
  guidance for angle and argument, never as text to quote, because a pattern mined
  from an account's ad performance is a hint about what the audience responds to,
  not a sentence the article may claim.**

## Sizing the brief to the piece

A pillar post that will anchor a cluster deserves the full dossier: fifty-plus sources,
the disagreement list, ten real questions, the win-angles. A spoke post deserves the
nine fields above and no more. A brief that exceeds the piece is not thoroughness; it
is the author's research process leaking into the writer's contract, and the writer
skims it the way a reader skims the article.

## When NOT to use

- **A page the business owner will write in their own voice from their own knowledge.**
  A brief that dictates the outline of a founder's story flattens the one thing that
  page had. Give them the primary term, the answer-first rule and the link targets, and
  stop.
- **A money page.** A service page's brief is a proof inventory, a single offer and a
  single action; the outline-FAQ-links shape produces a blog post where a conversion
  page was needed. The money page's craft is its own subject.
- **A refresh of an existing post.** The brief is the diff - which sections aged,
  which numbers changed, which questions appeared - not a fresh outline that invites
  the writer to rebuild a page that already ranks.
