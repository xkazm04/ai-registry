# The mid-body anchor-text call to action

The link that turns a reader into a lead is a descriptive anchor-text link to the
money page, inside a paragraph, placed where the reader's need peaks - not the banner
at the end of the post. The end-of-post box is still written, because whoever reaches
it is the highest-intent reader on the page; but it is the second device, not the
first.

## The evidence, with its class

One marketing-software publisher tracked unique URLs across its own best
lead-generating posts and found in-body anchor-text calls to action driving **47-93%**
of each post's leads while the end-of-post banner drove about **6%**. This is a
single-site self-study, its posts and audience were its own, and the numbers are not
a forecast for any other site. What transfers is the mechanism, and the mechanism is
consistent with the reading research: median scroll depth is about half the page, so
the banner is unseen by the majority; an in-paragraph link sits where the reader is;
and a link that reads as a further resource is examined where a box that reads as an
advertisement is blinded out. Two in-body links, roughly a third and two thirds down,
each matching the paragraph it sits in, is the practitioner convention that follows.

## Procedure

1. **Find the paragraph where the need peaks.** In a cost guide it is the paragraph
   after the price range, where the reader is deciding whether to do it themselves. In
   a how-to it is the step most people give up on. The brief's research - the
   questions people asked, the phrases they used - says where that is.
2. **Write the anchor as a description of what is on the other side.** "See what a
   drain clean costs in your area" is a resource; "click here" is an advertisement and
   "contact us" is a demand. The anchor is the sentence's natural object, not a
   parenthesis.
3. **Point it at the money page for the term**, never at the home page and never at a
   contact form with no context, because the reader is mid-decision and the page they
   land on has to continue the decision.
4. **Place the second link near two thirds down** only when a second need peaks there;
   a second link with no paragraph to match is a banner in disguise.
5. **Still close hard.** The final block is a reframe or a single next action, never
   "in conclusion", and the closing call to action is one line, straight, pointing at
   the same money page. Two thirds of engaged time happens below the fold; the readers
   who arrive at the end are the ones most likely to act.
6. **Add exactly one "read this next" link**, not a grid. A second pageview makes a
   reader measurably likelier to return - one publisher's dataset put it at 2.75x -
   and a grid of nine dilutes the one choice into none.
7. **Count the in-body links to the money page in review**, and fail a post that has
   zero. The banner does not count.

## Decision rules

- **When a post has traffic and no leads, check the in-body link before the banner,
  because the banner is seen by the scrolling minority and the in-body link by the
  majority; a post with only a banner has never been asked to convert.**
- **When the anchor could be replaced by "click here" without changing the sentence,
  rewrite it, because a descriptive anchor is what makes the link read as a resource
  and is also the internal-linking signal the engine weights.**
- **When the paragraph the link sits in is a proof zone - a price, a table, a quick
  answer - the anchor is written straight and the joke around it is deleted, because
  a call to action next to a bit reads as a bit.**
- **When somebody reports a click rate on the banner alone as the post's conversion
  rate, label it as the descriptive read of one device, because the post's conversion
  is the sum of its links and a banner-only read understates it by most of the total.**

## What the numbers are not

The 47-93% band is the share of leads inside one publisher's blog, not a lift and not
a comparison of equal placements; the banner and the anchor were not the same offer.
Any site's own split is measured by tracking each link separately for a full weekday-
balanced window, and the honest statement before that is "the in-body link is
expected to dominate; not yet measured here".

## When NOT to use

- **A money page.** It has one action above the fold and the same action repeated;
  in-body links to other pages are leaks from a page whose job is to convert.
- **A pillar page.** Its links are to its spokes, inside the section for each; a
  money-page link mid-pillar is fine once, but the pillar's job is routing, not
  conversion.
- **Content published for reach on a platform the business does not own**, where
  the link out is the platform's affordance, not the body's. The channel-native
  subject owns that.
