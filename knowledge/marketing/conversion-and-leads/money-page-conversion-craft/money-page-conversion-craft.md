# Money-page conversion craft

A money page is the page a business would keep if it could keep only one: the
service page, the product page, the city page for a trade, the quote page. Its
job is not to inform, rank or entertain. Its job is to turn a visitor who
already has intent into a lead, and everything on it is judged by that one
outcome. A page that reads beautifully, ranks well and produces no enquiries is
a failed money page; a plain page that a buyer reads in twenty seconds and
submits from is a good one.

This subject owns the composition of that page: what the hero does in the first
five seconds, what stays visible and what collapses, how much proof a page
carries and what counts as proof, the order in which conversion leaks are
fixed, where the conversion is actually counted, and the first diagnostic read
that says whether the page is even the problem. It does not own the arithmetic
of testing two versions against each other - `landing-page-experiment-statistics`
owns the sample, the split and the verdict, and this subject stops at "run the
experiment". It does not own where a proof number comes from and how a demo
number is labelled on a public surface - `honest-proof-and-illustrative-data`
owns provenance, and this subject consumes it as "real and checkable". It does
not own titles, meta descriptions, headings-as-markup, schema or internal
links - `on-page-and-metadata-craft` owns the mechanical layer; this subject
only says that the title's promise and the hero's promise are the same sentence.
What happens after the form - the callback, the assisted reply - is
`speed-to-lead-and-assisted-reply`; whether the leads that arrive are any good
is `lead-quality-and-source-diagnosis`; whether ten city pages are one page
wearing ten labels is `local-page-doorway-prevention`.

## The page has one job, and the job is visible before the scroll

A buyer who has typed a transactional query into a search box and clicked is
the most expensive visitor a business will ever have. The first screen decides
whether that cost was worth paying. A principal practitioner holds that the
first screen must do three things, and only three: say the thing the visitor
searched for, prove in one line that the business is real and competent, and
offer one action. Anything else on that screen - a second button, a navigation
bar of exits, a carousel, a paragraph of welcome - is a choice the visitor did
not ask for, and each choice is a place to leave.

This is the hero-proof-first rule, and its most contested word is "one". The
instinct to offer both "call" and "book" and "get a quote" feels generous; it
is a split decision, and split decisions lower both halves. Where a second
action genuinely earns its place - a booking calendar, for a business that
sells calls - it goes on the page the visitor lands on *after* converting,
because that is the moment intent peaks and nothing is left to lose. A
secondary action on the money page itself is a text link at most, never a
second button.

The proof line under the headline is the part most pages get wrong in the
other direction: they say "trusted by thousands" when the truth is "4.9 from
212 reviews, licensed and insured, since 2011". The second sentence converts
and the first does not, because the buyer's doubt is specific and only a
specific claim answers it. Every item in that line is something the business
supplied and a stranger could check - [never invent proof](../../_laws.md#never-invent-proof)
applies to the hero before it applies anywhere else.

## Less is visible than feels safe

A money page is sales copy, not a document, and the most common failure after a
weak hero is a thorough one: the page that includes everything - the process,
the materials, the timelines, the full proof stack, six answered questions -
because every item felt too important to hide. That page loses to a shorter
one. The buyer who is ready does not scroll through it; the buyer who is
researching does not trust it more for its length; and the search engine, under
mobile-first indexing, reads collapsed content the same as visible content, so
hiding costs nothing in rankings. That last point is documented platform
behaviour, and it retires the older advice never to hide text.

The discipline is to write the depth in full and then collapse nearly all of
it, keeping a fixed short list on screen: the headline and its promise, the
offer with its price and guarantee, one proof line, every call to action with
the form, and one line naming who and where the page serves. The default is
collapsed; visible is the exception that has to be argued for by the question
"does the page fail to sell without this on screen". Questions stay visible
with their answers shut, because the question stack alone reads as the buyer's
real worries. One accordion opens by default so the pattern is visibly a
pattern. The whole visible page reads in half a minute on a phone; that number
is a practitioner convention with no controlled test behind it, and the subject
says so.

The test is a screenshot with every accordion shut. If it does not sell on its
own, the fix is cutting or promoting one line - never reopening a section.

## Proof is counted, and only the checkable kind counts

A page's proof is not an impression; it is a count. A proof touch is a real
number, a review quote, a credential, a guarantee stated plainly, a named
client, a job actually done with its result, an original photograph of the
work. The convention this subject carries is five or more per page, every one
traceable to something the business supplied, and an unverifiable claim counts
*against* the total rather than toward it - "we're passionate about
excellence" is negative proof, because a buyer reads filler as the absence of
anything real to say.

Two corollaries decide most of the hard cases. First, proof is present-tense:
a line about what the business will do, offers, or intends is a commitment, not
proof, and a page that states an intention in the proof position has borrowed
credibility it has not earned. Say what is true today, and say the level at
which it is true - "live", "supported", "on request" - rather than implying
every item is equal. Second, when the honest inventory is thin, the page shows
the best true material honestly framed and never apologises. A section that
says "no results published here yet" fails the page; a padded section that
invents a result fails the business. The right move in between is the smaller
real thing: one job, one number, one named credential.

Where a proof number is illustrative rather than the business's own - a demo
account, a sample - the label sits beside the number and is unmissable, and the
page never counts it toward the five. That rule lives with
`honest-proof-and-illustrative-data`; the money page inherits it.

## Leaks are fixed in order, because each is larger than the next

When clicks arrive and leads do not, the page is the suspect, and the
practitioner works a fixed list top to bottom rather than redesigning. The
order is a convention of size: message match between the ad or title and the
page's first line; one call to action above the fold; the page loading fast on
a phone over a mobile connection; the phone layout designed first, with the
call button under the thumb; proof stacked where doubt peaks, which is beside
the action; the form cut to three or four fields with a button that names the
outcome; and the remaining friction - the three objections answered, the
service area stated so the wrong visitor leaves, the filler deleted.

Be strict about the footing of each rung. Message match is the one with
documented platform behaviour behind it: the dominant ad platform states that
its quality signal includes a landing-page-relevance component, so a mismatch
costs both the conversion and the click price. The speed rung has the most
repeated numbers and the weakest provenance - the "seven percent per second"
figure traces to a single retailer study two decades old and the "half of
mobile visits abandoned by three seconds" figure to the search engine's own
2016 research; the direction is solid, the slope is not portable, and "under
two seconds" is a convention. The form-length rung rests on vendor-published
observational data across tens of thousands of pages and one vendor-run test of
five against seven against ten fields, none with published confidence
intervals; three-to-four fields is a convention with correlational support.
Above-the-fold placement and proof-beside-the-action have no controlled test
this subject could find; they are convention, and any page that presents them
as measured is reproducing folklore.
[Label convention as convention](../../_laws.md#label-convention-as-convention)
is the law this section exists to honour.

## The conversion is counted on the page after the form

Every lead the page produces - form fill, callback request, booking - ends on
one distinct page, and that page is the only reliable place a conversion event
fires. Click and event triggers on the button break silently when a class name
changes or a script loads late, and they undercount without anyone noticing;
a page load is the one signal every tracker, ad platform and analytics tool
can count the same way. The thank-you page is therefore built first, before
any form goes live, with the tracking on page load rather than on a click.

Two properties keep the count honest. The page must never be indexed and never
appear in the sitemap, because a visitor who arrives from a search result has
not converted and every such arrival is a fabricated lead in the platform's
count - [platform-reported is not causal](../../_laws.md#platform-reported-is-not-causal)
begins with a count that is not even a count. And the redirect that lands the
visitor there must follow a successful hand-off of the lead, so the page is
reached only when the lead actually exists. A form whose destination is not yet
wired returns a plain error and is never published, because a lead that
vanishes silently is the worst failure a money page has - worse than no form.

The thank-you page also does the one thing that reduces no-shows more than any
copy on the money page: it says what happens next, with a time. "A real person
calls within thirty minutes during business hours" is a promise the business
must keep, and it is where a second action - the booking calendar - belongs.

## The first read says which problem you have

Before any of the above is touched, the practitioner reads four numbers and
places the page in a quadrant. Many clicks and no conversions: the page - work
the leak order. A good conversion rate but junk leads: the source, not the
page - the traffic is the wrong people, and the deeper read belongs to
`lead-quality-and-source-diagnosis`. Few clicks at decent impressions: the
title and description, not the page - the promise in the result is not being
chosen. Few impressions: bids, budget or query volume - nothing on the page can
fix a page nobody is shown.

The quadrant is a triage, not a verdict. It reads platform-reported counts and
inherits their limits: a "conversion" is whatever the tracker fired, a "click"
is whatever the platform billed, and a week of either on a small business's
page is often too few to place the page in any cell with confidence. The
practitioner says "too few to read" rather than guessing, per
[statistical honesty before a verdict](../../_laws.md#statistical-honesty-before-a-verdict),
and reads the quadrant again after the sample exists.

## Failure modes of the naive reading

- **The thorough page.** Everything visible, nothing collapsed, a document
  where a sale should be.
- **The proof of intention.** "Coming soon", "we plan to", "open to all" in
  the proof line - a commitment borrowed as credibility.
- **The click-trigger conversion.** A count that drifted to zero when a
  button's class changed, and nobody knew for a month.
- **The measured convention.** "Under two seconds" or "three fields" presented
  as documented behaviour. It is not, and the page that claims it ages into
  folklore.

## Seams with neighbouring subjects

rule that an experiment page prints none of its own numbers, because a page
