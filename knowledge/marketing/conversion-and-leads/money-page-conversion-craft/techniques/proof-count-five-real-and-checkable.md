# Proof count: five, real and checkable

Proof on a money page is a count, not an impression, and the count has a
definition. A proof touch is one of: a real number, a review quote attributed
to a real reviewer, a credential the reader could look up, a guarantee stated
plainly, a named client, a job actually done with the number that resulted, an
original photograph of the work. The convention this technique carries is
**five or more per page**, every touch traceable to something the business
supplied, and **an unverifiable claim counts against the total**, not toward it.

Five is a practitioner convention. No published test fixes it; the technique
keeps it because it is high enough to force the page past the two-or-three
generic claims most pages stop at, and low enough that a small business with
one year of trading can reach it honestly.

## What counts, and what counts against

Counts toward:

- An exact number the business owns: review count, years, jobs completed,
  response time actually kept, a price actually charged.
- A review quoted word for word, with a name and where it was posted.
- A licence, certification, membership or insurance status with the issuer.
- A guarantee stated as a sentence a customer could hold the business to.
- A job done: the situation, what was done, the number that resulted.
- A photograph the business took of its own team, vehicles or work.

Counts against:

- "Trusted by thousands", "award-winning", "passionate about excellence" -
  any claim without a number or an issuer.
- A stock photograph presented where a real one is implied; a stock face where
  a real person is implied.
- A rounded or inflated number, or a number the owner did not confirm.
- A statement of intention in a proof position - "coming soon", "open to
  all", "we plan to".

The asymmetry is deliberate. A buyer reads filler as the absence of anything
real to say, so filler is not zero proof; it is negative proof.

## Procedure

1. Build the proof inventory before writing: ask the owner for real numbers,
   reviews, credentials, jobs and photographs, and record where each came
   from. Never write a proof item that is not in the inventory.
2. Draft the page. Place proof where doubt peaks - beside the call to action,
   in the hero line, next to the form - rather than in a testimonials section
   nobody scrolls to.
3. Count. Walk the page and tally touches against the definition above; tally
   unverifiable claims separately and subtract them.
4. Under five: return to the owner with the specific gap ("one more review
   with a name; the licence number"), not a general request for "more proof".
5. Record the count with the page, so an audit can compare it against a word
   count: a four-hundred-word page with six real touches beats a padded
   twelve-hundred-word page with two.

## When the inventory is thin

A new business, or one that never collected reviews, will not reach five on
the first pass. Two failures wait here. The apology - a section that says "no
case studies yet" or "results not published here" - fails the page, because it
tells the buyer the business has nothing. The invention - a plausible number,
a composed testimonial - fails the business and the law. The path between them
is the smallest true thing, honestly framed: one job with one number, the
owner's own credential, a photograph of the work, a guarantee the business is
willing to keep. A page can honestly carry three real touches and say nothing
about the missing two; it cannot carry a placeholder.

**When a proof number is illustrative - a demo account, sample data - it is
labelled beside the number, the label is never fine print, and it does not
count toward five.** The labelling rule belongs to
`honest-proof-and-illustrative-data`; the counting rule here is that
illustrative proof is zero proof for the purposes of the gate.

## Decision rules

- When a claim cannot be traced to the inventory, delete it rather than hedge
  it, because a hedged unverifiable claim is still unverifiable and now also
  reads as evasive.
- When the review count is under ten, show the exact count and the newest
  review's date; recency is itself a proof touch, and a small honest number
  outperforms a suspicious round one.
- When a city page shares a service page's proof, it fails the count for that
  city; a city page's jobs are jobs in that city, and borrowed proof is what
  makes it a clone - the anti-clone test itself belongs to
  `local-page-doorway-prevention`.
- When an audit flags a page under five, route it to the owner for material and
  then to a rewrite; never to a mechanical fix pass, because proof is body copy
  and a fix pass changes only the mechanical layer.

## When NOT to use

- On an index or hub page. A hub links down to the pages that carry proof; a
  hub with five proof touches is a sales page pretending to be an index.
- On a legal or thank-you page. Neither sells; a proof count there is noise.
- As a ranking argument. The count predicts conversion, not position; a page
  with twelve touches and no answer to its query still does not rank.
