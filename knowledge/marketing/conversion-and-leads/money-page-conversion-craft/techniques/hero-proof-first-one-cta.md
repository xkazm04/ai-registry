# Hero: proof first, one call to action

The first screen of a money page has a five-second job: confirm the visitor is
in the right place, prove the business is real, and offer one action. The
technique is a fixed composition for that screen and a rule for everything
that tries to join it.

## The composition

1. **The headline is the searched phrase.** The primary keyword, phrased the
   way a buyer typed it, not the way the business describes itself. "Emergency
   drain cleaning in <city>" beats "Welcome to <company>" every time, because
   the visitor is checking a match, not reading. Where the click came from an
   ad, the headline repeats the ad's promise word for word; this is the
   message-match rung of the leak order and the only rung with documented
   platform behaviour behind it - the dominant ad platform's own documentation
   lists landing-page relevance as a component of its quality signal.
2. **The proof line sits directly under the headline.** One line, three or
   four items, every one specific and checkable: a star rating with the exact
   review count, a licence or insurance status, years in business or since
   when. "4.9 from 212 reviews, licensed and insured, since 2011." Each item
   comes from the business's proof inventory; none is rounded up, none is
   inferred, and a missing item is an empty slot or a question to the owner,
   never a plausible value.
3. **One call to action, visible without scrolling.** A click-to-call button
   on a phone, a short form or a form button on a desktop. The button text
   names the outcome ("Get my free quote"), never the mechanism ("Submit").
4. **The quick answer.** Forty to sixty words on what the business does, where,
   and how fast - written straight, because an answer engine lifts it, and
   because a buyer who reads nothing else reads this.

Sections after the hero repeat the same call to action after every proof
block. Repetition of one action is not a second action.

## The proof line is present tense

A line in the proof position claims that something is true now. "Free during
validation" is proof if there is no payment gateway and the business means it;
"open source" is a commitment if the source is not yet published, and a
commitment in the proof position is a borrowed credibility that will be called
in. When a business supports several channels at different depths, the line
states each level - "live sync", "ad-copy checks", "publishing" - rather than
showing four logos that imply four equal integrations. The rule: **when a proof
item describes an intention, a plan or a future state, move it out of the proof
line, because a buyer who later finds the gap discounts every other item on the
page.**

## The "one" rule and its exceptions

A second primary action splits the decision and lowers both halves. This is a
practitioner convention - the technique found no controlled test of it - but it
is the convention every experienced conversion practitioner holds, and the
reasoning is sound: each visible choice is a place to leave.

- **When a second action is genuinely valuable - a booking calendar for a
  business that sells calls - put it on the thank-you page, not the hero,**
  because the visitor arrives there having already converted, intent is at its
  peak, and a slice of leads books a call the same minute at no cost to the
  form's conversion.
- **When a demo, a tour or a "see an example" must be reachable from the
  hero, render it as a text link beside the button, never as a second button,**
  so the visual hierarchy still has one answer to "what do I do here".
- **When the page serves both a phone-first trade audience and a desktop
  research audience, pick the action per device, not both on each:** call
  button on the phone layout, form on the desktop layout. That is still one
  action per screen.

## Decision rules

- When the headline does not contain the phrase the page targets, rewrite the
  headline before anything else on the page, because no proof or button
  recovers a visitor who has decided they are in the wrong place.
- When a proof item cannot be traced to something the owner supplied, remove
  it and ask; do not soften it into a vaguer claim, because "hundreds of
  happy customers" is both unverifiable and read as such.
- When the review count is small, show the small true number; a low count
  stated exactly converts better than a suspicious round one, and the
  alternative violates the proof law outright.
- When a navigation bar full of exits sits above the hero on an ad-driven
  page, strip it to the logo and the phone number; on an organic page keep it,
  because the searcher may need the sibling pages.

## When NOT to use

- On an index or hub page. A hub names a branch and links down; it carries no
  form and no offer pricing, and a hero-with-form on a hub turns the index into
  a duplicate sales page that competes with its own children.
- On an experiment arm page whose job is to produce a clean trial. The
  composition still applies, but the page prints none of its own performance
  numbers, and the proof line must be identical across arms unless the proof
  line is the thing being tested - `landing-page-experiment-statistics` owns
  that constraint.
- On a blog post. The post entertains to keep the reader and bridges to the
  money page; a hero form on a post converts poorly and taxes the read.
