# Decision records

Most audit records answer *what the system did*. A decision record answers
something the system cannot observe: **what a human decided about
something the system found.** Accepted. Declined, deliberately. Waived
until the quarter ends. These are the records with the longest useful life
in a product — the action they describe happened once and the reason
behind it stays relevant for years — and they are the ones most often
built as an afterthought on top of a checkbox, which is why they are also
the ones that most often vanish.

They have a distinctive hazard. The thing decided about is frequently
**derived**: a finding recomputed on every refresh, a gap produced by an
analysis, a recommendation regenerated by a model. It owns no durable row.
So the decision must attach itself to something that will still be
recognizable the next time the finding is regenerated — and if it attaches
to the wrong thing, the decision does not error, it simply stops matching,
and the item the user dismissed reappears.

## Identity before wording

**Never key a decision on the text of the thing decided.** A key built
from a phrasing changes the instant a rubric is retuned or a generator
rewords its output; the stored decision is orphaned, the finding returns,
and nothing anywhere reports a failure — the user sees a dismissed item
resurrect and concludes the feature is broken, while the record sits in
the store matching nothing
([identity-survives-reuse](../../_laws.md#identity-survives-reuse): mint
identity from what survives regeneration, not from what re-renders).

The ladder of what to key on, best first:

1. **A durable entity identifier plus a stable check identifier.** The
   subject the finding is about, and the *rule* that produced it — both
   are names the system controls and neither moves when prose is
   regenerated.
2. **A generated identifier the producer emits and keeps stable.** If the
   generator can be asked for an id, ask; a producer that emits stable ids
   is cheaper than every downstream consumer inventing identity.
3. **A hash of normalized text — last resort only.** Where an item is
   free prose with no id at all, normalize first (trim, fold case,
   collapse whitespace) so cosmetic churn does not rotate the key, and
   scope the hash inside a stable prefix so collisions are confined to one
   small population. Accept the consequence deliberately and write it
   down: **a materially reworded item becomes a new finding**, and that is
   usually correct — the decision was made about the old wording, and a
   changed statement deserves a fresh look.

Two structural rules protect the ladder. Key derivation lives in **one
pure builder**, never at the call sites, so a newly added signal cannot
invent its own scheme (and so the identity is unit-testable without the
store). And the key's shape is uniform across signal types — a subject
identifier, or a subject identifier plus a suffix — so that "every
decision about this subject" is an exact prefix match rather than a guess.

## Two stores, two shapes: the state and the act

Here is the distinction teams collapse, always in the same direction.
A decision has **current state** (this item is presently dismissed) and a
**history of acts** (someone dismissed it, someone else reopened it, the
original decider changed their mind). These want opposite storage:

- The **state** is a small mutable record, one per (subject, item),
  updated in place when the decision changes. Sparse by construction — an
  undecided item has no record at all, which is what keeps "how many are
  open?" a single cheap read over a population that never grows with the
  untouched majority.
- The **act** is an append-only ledger entry written on every change, with
  actor, time, previous and new status, and reason.

Building only the state gives you a system that cannot say who changed
their mind or when — the exact question a reviewer asks first. Building
only the ledger gives you a system that must replay history to render a
badge. Build both, from the same call: the state upsert commits first,
because it is the source of truth for the surface; the ledger write
follows through the ordinary audit door with the ordinary best-effort
posture. Any further publication — feeding the reason to an assistant, an
index, a notification — is a **derived artifact** and is best-effort and
non-transactional with the decision itself: an entitlement failure or a
slow adjacent write must never roll back a judgment a human just made.

## What may be decided is an allowlist

A decision is legitimate for a gap the decider can genuinely choose to
accept — an unmonitored internal job, a policy the team has consciously
opted out of. It is **not** legitimate for a caveat about the evidence
itself: "we could not observe this" is a limitation of the measurement,
and letting an owner dismiss it turns the mechanism from *accepting a
trade-off* into *silencing the instrument*. So the decidable set is an
explicit allowlist, validated at the write door, and the exclusions are
deliberate entries in it rather than an accident of what someone wired up.

Unknown keys are ignored on read, never rejected: a stored decision whose
item no longer exists is kept and skipped, because items come back and a
cleanup pass that deletes "orphans" is a cleanup pass that destroys
judgments during a rename.

## A decision never moves the measurement

Recording that someone chose to live with a gap **must not change the
score, the count, or the underlying assessment.** Retire the item from the
open list, re-render it in a decided section with its reason and its
decider, and leave the measurement exactly where it was. The temptation to
let a dismissal improve the number is strong and it is the same error as
editing a record to make history look right: the finding was the artifact
exposing a real condition, and suppressing it changes the evidence, not
the condition ([deletion-is-not-repair](../../_laws.md#deletion-is-not-repair)).
A reader must always be able to see the honest measurement and the
decisions taken against it as two separate facts.

## Store the decision outside what gets regenerated

If decisions are written into the same document a refresh rebuilds, the
next refresh erases them — silently, completely, and with no error. Store
them **separately, keyed by the identity above, and apply them as a
read-time overlay** over the freshly produced artifact. Then regeneration
is free to rewrite everything it owns, and the overlay re-attaches the
same judgments to the new output. This one rule is what makes decisions
*memory* rather than an annotation with a short life.

## The record carries the reason, bounded

Persist onto the decision itself: the actor, the time, the status, the
free-text reason, and **the item's title as it read at decision time** —
so the record still means something after the item disappears. The reason
is the payload that outlives everything else, and it is also free text, so
it passes the ledger's ordinary sanitization and a length cap on the way
in. Where decisions are surfaced back into the system's own inputs, filter
to *resolved decisions carrying a reason* and bound the number: a
resolution with no reason contributes nothing but noise, and an unbounded
set crowds out the material it was meant to annotate.

## When not to use this

Ephemeral acknowledgements — "hide this for now", "collapse this panel" —
are interface state, not decision records. Persisting them into a business
ledger fills it with events nobody will ever ask about and dilutes the
records that matter. The test is whether a reader, a year from now, would
want to know that this was decided, by whom, and why. If not, it is a
preference.
